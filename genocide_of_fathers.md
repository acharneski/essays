# Who Decides Who Counts: Ohio's Family Courts and the Architecture of Managed Persons

## I. A Word That Must Be Earned


Calling any modern institution "slavery" is a serious charge. The usual way to make it is to start with a disclaimer: chattel slavery involved kidnapping, hereditary bondage, the sale of human beings, systematic rape and torture, and the destruction of whole cultures, and no modern court does any of that. The disclaimer is meant to sound responsible. In fact it concedes too much, too quickly, and it concedes it on the system's own terms.

Start instead with a principle. That something is legal does not settle whether it is legitimate. Slavery was legal. A legal system has to earn its moral standing. It does not get that standing by issuing orders and calling them law. This essay is not written from inside the legalist frame, where whatever a court does cannot be kidnapping because a court did it. It is written from the position of a parent who has not fully bought into the system's account of itself. From there, the familiar list looks different.

*Kidnapping.* A child is removed from a parent's care by order of a stranger, and the order is enforced by police if the parent resists. A father who keeps his own son past the hour a court has set can be charged with interference with custody under ORC § 2919.23. The legal system calls this the allocation of parental rights. To the parent it is done to, and often to the child, it is a person taken by force without consent. Ohio's system also monetizes it. The moment a parent is removed from the home is the moment that parent becomes a revenue source, through a support order the state is federally paid to enforce.

*Hereditary bondage.* The bondage is not lifelong, but it is real, and it attaches to a person through the fact of having fathered a child. For eighteen years or more, a parent's income, licenses, passport, and liberty are subject to an order he did not agree to. That order may be calculated on earnings the court simply imputes to him, and it is collectible by garnishment and jail. Arrears outlive the child's minority. A man can still be paying for a childhood he was not allowed to be part of long after it has ended.

*The sale of human beings.* Human trafficking is not a historical curiosity. It is common today, and children who pass through state custody and foster care are among the most vulnerable to it. Inside family court the transaction is quieter. Custody carries money with it. The residential designation decides which way support flows, and the state draws federal incentive dollars on what it collects. Among other things, the child becomes a revenue stream that goes to whichever adult and whichever agency holds the right paperwork.

*Systematic rape and torture.* No one claims that judges order abuse. The charge is different. A system that claims sweeping authority over a child's welfare is responsible for what happens under that authority, and it does a poor job of preventing abuse. Children are placed with abusive parents or partners while the protective parent who raises the alarm is labeled "alienating." Abuse in foster care is documented at levels that would shut down any private institution. Recoiling from the word does not change the fact. A system that claims the power to protect and then fails to protect owns the failure.

*The destruction of a culture.* Fatherhood is not only a private relationship. It is a cultural institution passed down through generations. It includes what a man teaches his son about work, restraint, and responsibility, and what a daughter learns about how a man who loves her behaves. A system that routinely reduces fathers to every-other-weekend visitors and payment sources, and that grants fatherhood as a privilege instead of recognizing it as a fact, is taking that institution apart one household at a time. In many communities, most children now grow up without their father in the home. That is the erosion of a culture, carried out through procedure instead of the sword, and it is no less serious for being quiet.

*Auctions and brands.* Ohio courts do not use a block and a gavel. In practice, though, custody goes to whoever can keep paying: the retainer, the custody evaluation, the guardian ad litem's fees, years of motions. The parent who runs out of money first loses. Parents are also branded. "Deadbeat." "High conflict." "Alienator." "Obligor in arrears." These labels go into court records, onto public dockets, into the license database and the passport system. They follow a parent through every later proceeding and often into job interviews. The mark is administrative, not burned into skin, but it is still a mark, and it does what a mark does.

None of this makes Ohio's courts a replica of an antebellum plantation, and the differences in scale and brutality are real. But the honest conclusion is not that the comparison is off limits. The comparison holds in more places than the people who run the system would like to admit. And the deepest resemblance is not in any single practice. It is in structure. Slavery was not first of all a system of labor extraction. It was a claim about standing. Before the master took a single hour of anyone's work, he had already asserted something more basic:

*I am the one whose judgment decides whose interests are real. Yours enter the ledger only when I allow it.*

Everything else, including the coercion, the extraction, the punishment of resistance, and the welfare story told to justify it, followed from that first move.

The claim of this essay is that Ohio's family law system, as it operates in 2026, makes the same structural move. It does not do so through malice or conspiracy, and the people inside it mostly believe they are doing good. It does so through a legal architecture that gives one institution the authority to define whose interests count, to weigh them, and to override the people concerned, all in the name of their own good. The resemblance is partly in specific practices, which are closer than polite commentary admits. Above all, it is in shape. And shapes, unlike intentions, produce predictable results. In Ohio, as across the country, the people this shape most often pushes off the ledger are fathers.

## II. The Membership Function Comes First

Any system that claims to balance competing interests runs two operations. The first decides *who is on the ledger*. The second decides *how much each entry weighs*. Public debate about family court is almost entirely about the second operation. Does the court favor mothers? Does it undervalue fathers? Does it give enough weight to the child's wishes?

These are real questions, but they come downstream. Whoever controls membership has decided most of the outcome before any weighing starts. A ledger that leaves out certain agents is not a balanced ledger with odd weights. It is a cooked ledger. The people left out do not disappear. They become externalities, and reality eventually collects on what was not counted.

Ohio law states its membership function plainly. When a court allocates parental rights and responsibilities, Ohio Revised Code § 3109.04 directs it to act in "the best interest of the children," and lists factors in § 3109.04(F)(1) to guide that judgment. The parents' wishes are on the list. So are the child's relationships, adjustment to home and school, the mental and physical health of everyone involved, and each parent's willingness to facilitate the other's parenting time.

Look at how parents appear on that list. Their interests are not entered as interests. A father's desire to raise his son appears only as an input to a calculation about the son. A mother's wish to keep her daughter in the community where she grew up counts only to the extent it predicts the daughter's adjustment. The parent is an instrument in the child's welfare function, not a stakeholder in their own right.

Siblings, grandparents, extended family, and community appear, if at all, as more instruments. The children who may never be born because a generation of young adults has watched what happens to families that enter this system do not appear. The court itself, with its own budget, workload, and institutional survival, has interests too, and those never appear on the ledger at all, although they shape every outcome.

That is the first move. "We are here solely for the best interests of the child" sounds like moral clarity. Structurally, it is a declaration that the institution has the authority to stop counting everyone else.

The child's interests matter. So do the mother's. So do the father's. So do the siblings' and grandparents'. An honest accounting may, after entering all of them, decide that some deserve more weight than others. That is a defensible operation. Pretending that some of them do not exist is a different operation, and it is the one slavery depended on.

## III. "For Your Own Good"

Slavery was often defended as being in the interests of the enslaved. This is uncomfortable history, but it is true, and it matters. Many proslavery writers were not cynics. They argued sincerely that the enslaved were fed, housed, clothed, supervised, and cared for better than they would be in freedom. They described the master as a benefactor and the institution as a kindness to people who could not manage their own lives.

The horror of slavery is not that it lacked a welfare argument. The horror is that it had one.

"Best interests" language does not protect against domination. It is one of domination's most common forms. The sequence is consistent across many institutions:

1. Agent A gains the authority to *define* Agent B's interests.
2. Agent A gains the authority to *weigh* those interests against other considerations.
3. Agent A gains the authority to *override* Agent B in the name of those interests.

Once all three are in place, the system seals itself. Disagreement becomes evidence of incapacity. An enslaved person who wanted freedom showed that he could not appreciate what slavery gave him. A cult member who wants to leave shows spiritual confusion. A patient who refuses treatment shows a lack of insight.

Ohio's family courts have their own version of this loop. One of the statutory best-interest factors is which parent is more likely to honor and facilitate court-approved parenting time. In practice, a parent who strongly objects to a proposed arrangement, challenges a guardian ad litem's recommendation, or keeps insisting that the process is unjust risks being described as "high conflict" or as failing to support the child's relationship with the other parent. Protest becomes a data point against the protester. The more forcefully a parent claims standing, the more the record shows they lack the temperament the system rewards.

This does not require any bad actor. It follows from the structure. If the institution defines the interests, judges compliance, and treats resistance as a symptom, then nothing a parent says can register as a legitimate objection. It can only register as a sign of unfitness.

Some people really cannot decide for themselves. An infant is not a competent adult. A child cannot represent herself in litigation. This is exactly what makes the principle dangerous. Every coercive institution claims that its subjects are a temporary special case: normally agency matters, but here, for this person, in this situation, we must substitute our judgment for theirs. The questions an honest observer has to ask are:

- Who decides that the exception applies?
- What happens when the person governed disagrees?
- What incentives constrain the one who decides?
- What happens when the institution's interests diverge from the subject's?

A welfare story tells you almost nothing about whether a system is just. Every system of domination in history has had one. The real test is whether the people governed keep their standing as agents whose interests are on the ledger, or become objects managed by those who claim to know better.

## IV. Permission to Be a Parent

In one respect Ohio law makes the membership function unusually explicit.

Under ORC § 3109.042, an unmarried woman who gives birth is the sole residential parent and legal custodian of the child until a court issues an order designating another residential parent or legal custodian. Acknowledging paternity establishes a father's support obligation. It does not, by itself, give him any enforceable right to custody or parenting time. To get those, he has to file in juvenile court and ask.

Think about what that means. A man can be a legally recognized father, fully liable for the child's support, and still have no secured relationship with the child at all. His standing as a parent is not presumed. It has to be granted, and the body that grants it is the same institution that decides how much weight his interests deserve once he is allowed in.

The point is not that mothers are favored, although the default plainly favors them. The point is that parenthood itself becomes a status the court confers, not a relationship it recognizes. A person who must petition for permission to raise his own child has already been placed outside the ledger. Getting back in depends on the discretion of others.

## V. Obligation Without Standing

The slavery analogy is most uncomfortable where it touches compelled labor.

Ohio's child support system, governed by ORC Chapter 3119 and enforced through county child support enforcement agencies, calculates obligations from the parents' incomes. When the court decides a parent is voluntarily unemployed or underemployed, it may impute "potential income," meaning income the parent does not earn but that the court believes they could earn. The obligation that results is not a debt the parent took on. It is an order, issued without the obligor's consent and based on the institution's estimate of what that person's labor should produce.

The enforcement tools are extensive:

- wage withholding at the source;
- interception of tax refunds;
- suspension of driver's licenses, professional licenses, and recreational licenses;
- denial of a U.S. passport when arrears pass the federal threshold;
- contempt of court, which can mean jail;
- criminal prosecution for nonsupport under ORC § 2919.21.

Each of these can be defended on its own terms. Children need food, housing, and care, and a parent who walks away from them is doing real harm. That point deserves full weight.

The structural problem is a decoupling. Ohio law treats support and parenting time as legally independent. A parent's obligation to pay does not depend on actually having time with the child, and denial of parenting time is not a defense to nonpayment. Enforcement of the financial obligation is administrative, automatic, and backed by the state's coercive power. Enforcement of the relational right is slow and expensive, and it depends on the parent hiring a lawyer and returning to the same court that set the arrangement in the first place.

So one side of the ledger is enforced by the state and the other side is left to private litigation. The parent's labor is claimed by order and collected by force, while the relationship that is supposed to justify the claim is left unsecured. That parent's income is no longer fully their own. Their mobility, livelihood, and liberty depend on meeting a production quota set by others. Meanwhile, the bond with the child, which is the only thing that makes the arrangement morally coherent, is treated as optional.

This is not chattel slavery in its full form. The parent is not owned outright, and the bidding for the child happens in legal fees, not on a block. But the shape is unmistakable: labor claimed under threat, standing withheld, a mark on the record for anyone who falls behind, and a welfare story explaining why the arrangement serves the person who did not agree to it.

## VI. What Grows Under Selection Pressure

Institutions are better understood by what thrives inside them than by their mission statements. A forest shows you what survives in that soil and climate. An institution shows you what survives under its incentives.

In Ohio's family law system, several roles depend on family conflict for their income:

- **Attorneys**, whose billing grows with the length and intensity of litigation.
- **Guardians ad litem**, appointed under Ohio Superintendence Rule 48 and often paid by the parties, whose involvement grows with contested issues.
- **Custody evaluators and psychologists**, whose assessments are ordered when disputes become hard.
- **Parenting coordinators, supervised visitation centers, and mandated program providers**, whose caseloads depend on court referrals.
- **Child support enforcement agencies**, which work within a federal funding structure under Title IV-D of the Social Security Act that pays states incentive money based partly on collections and enforcement performance.

None of these people needs to be corrupt for the pattern to matter. Most are probably sincere. But the gradients are clear. The system's resources flow toward conflict, toward procedure, toward continued involvement, and toward the conversion of family relationships into enforceable orders. Families that settle quickly, cooperate, and stay out of court produce almost nothing for this system. The families that sustain it are the ones in conflict.

One feature of the slave economy was that the master appeared to profit from suppression, so the system kept building structures that reproduced suppression. The family-court equivalent does not require anyone to want families destroyed. It only requires that institutional success and family success be weakly linked. When they are, the system will tend to reproduce whatever feeds it, and everyone inside can sincerely believe they are helping while the overall result is harmful.

This is Goodhart's Law in institutional form. The stated target is child welfare. The rewarded targets are hearings, filings, evaluations, compliance, collections, and the use of judicial discretion. Over time, an institution optimizes for what it rewards.

## VII. The Data the System Does Not Collect

A common bitter summary of family court goes like this: the child is harmed, the family is destroyed, and the professionals move up. Whether that is true in a particular case depends on the case. But one fact about the system as a whole stands out.

Ohio's domestic relations and juvenile courts are not organized to measure their long-term effects on the children they claim to serve. Courts track cases filed, cases closed, time to disposition, and support collected. They do not routinely follow whether children whose custody was allocated in a contested proceeding are doing well five, ten, or twenty years later. They do not compare outcomes across different kinds of orders. They do not track whether a parent pushed to the margins later disappeared from the child's life, or whether the conflict the court managed was reduced or made worse.

A system that claims a single goal, the best interests of the child, and never checks whether it achieves that goal is not optimizing for it. Whatever it is optimizing for, it is something it can measure, such as throughput, compliance, and collections. The best-interest standard works less as a target than as a justification.

Broader research on children's outcomes is consistent and sobering. Prolonged parental conflict harms children. Losing meaningful contact with a parent is associated with worse emotional, educational, and economic outcomes. Children generally do better when both parents stay substantially involved, except where real abuse or danger exists. An institution that held itself accountable to that research would look very different from the one that exists. It would treat preserving both parental bonds as a core outcome, see prolonged litigation as a failure, and check its own results.

## VIII. The Strongest Objections

An honest essay has to face the best case for the current system.

**First: some parents are dangerous.** Abuse, neglect, addiction, and domestic violence are real, and children in those situations need protection that only a court with real power can give. That is true, and any reform that weakens that protection is unacceptable. But danger is the exception, and the system is built as though the exception were the rule. The standing of every parent is subordinated to institutional discretion because some parents are dangerous. This is exactly the special-case reasoning that coercive systems use to justify their general authority.

**Second: children cannot speak for themselves.** Someone has to represent their interests, and parents in conflict are not neutral. Also true. But the answer to an interested advocate is not an institution that acts as if it has no interests of its own. The court, the guardian ad litem, and the evaluator all operate under incentives too. Moving the power to define a child's interests from a parent to a professional does not remove the conflict of interest. It replaces a conflict that is visible with one that is hidden.

**Third: judges are bound by statute.** Ohio judges apply the law the General Assembly writes, and many of them work hard and humanely within its limits. This is right, and it supports the structural argument. The problem is not mainly with individual judges. It is with an architecture that would produce similar results no matter who sat on the bench.

**Fourth: the slavery comparison is inflammatory.** It is. It is also historically grounded. The comparison is not that family court is as cruel as slavery. It is that both rest on the same foundational claim: *I possess legitimate authority to declare which agents count.* Once that claim is accepted, later abuses become easier to justify, because from inside the system they no longer look like abuses. The people harmed have already been removed from the accounting.

## IX. What an Honest Ledger Would Require

If the core problem is the membership function, the remedies start there.

1. **Enter every affected person on the ledger.** Parents' interests should be recognized as interests in their own right, not only as inputs to the child's welfare. Siblings, grandparents, and the wider family should be recognized as stakeholders. The court's own institutional interests should be named and limited, not ignored.

2. **Recognize parenthood instead of granting it.** A legally established parent should have presumptive standing to a real relationship with the child. Lawmakers in Ohio have repeatedly proposed a rebuttable presumption of equal or substantially shared parenting, which can be overcome by evidence of danger or unfitness. A presumption like that changes the burden. The state would have to justify removing a parent, instead of the parent having to justify their own inclusion.

3. **Tie obligations to standing.** If the state enforces support with administrative speed and coercive force, it should enforce parenting time with the same seriousness. A system that collects from a parent automatically and protects that parent's relationship only through private litigation has made its priorities clear.

4. **Measure outcomes.** Courts should track, anonymously and over the long term, how children and families fare under different kinds of orders. An institution that claims to serve child welfare should have to show that it does.

5. **Weaken the conflict gradient.** Fee and appointment structures that reward prolonged dispute should be reviewed. Early mediation, limits on repeated motions, and default parenting schedules can reduce the amount of conflict the system feeds on.

6. **Treat objection as information, not pathology.** A parent who says the process is unjust is making a claim that deserves an answer, not a symptom to be documented. Systems that cannot tell dissent from dysfunction cannot correct their own mistakes.

None of these reforms requires believing that the people in the system are villains. They only require taking seriously the idea that systems should be judged by their gradients, not by their intentions.

## X. Conclusion: The First Question

The most important question to ask of Ohio's family courts is not whether they favor mothers or fathers, or even whether they serve children. It comes earlier:

*What gives this institution the right to define which interests are allowed to matter?*

Whoever controls the ledger controls the outcome long before any balancing happens. A system that starts by declaring that only one interest counts, and then gives itself sole authority to interpret that interest, has already made the structural move that every system of domination has made. It decides who counts. It optimizes. It calls the result justice.

Slavery was the extreme case of this pattern because it admitted openly that some people's interests were not part of the calculation. Modern institutions rarely admit it. They do it implicitly, in the vocabulary of care, and they often believe sincerely that they are the enlightened alternative to the cruelties of the past. "For your own good" has been the preferred language of power for a very long time.

That sentence proves nothing either way. What matters is whether the people governed keep their standing as agents whose interests are honestly counted, or become objects managed by others who claim to know better. By that test, Ohio's family law system has a lot to answer for. It has not built plantations. It has adopted the plantation's first premise: that some institution, somewhere, is entitled to decide who matters.
Fathers matter. Mothers matter. Children, siblings, and grandparents matter. A system that cannot say all of that at once, and enforce it with the same force it uses to collect a payment, has not earned the moral authority it claims, however legal its orders may be.
