# The New Adventures of Socrates

## Prologue: The Patient in Bed Four

They gave him back his body first. Everything else came later.

The procedure had been designed for billionaires and was tested, for legal reasons, on the dead. Somebody on the ethics board pointed out that a man executed in 399 BCE could not sue. Somebody else pointed out that he had never consented. A third person said that if any human being in history would have consented to an experiment purely to find out what happened, it was this one. The motion carried.

So Socrates, son of Sophroniscus, of the deme Alopece, woke in a recovery ward in a hospital whose name he could not read. His knees no longer ached. His eyes, for the first time in decades, focused on things that were close. Cellular senescence had been reversed. In principle he could now live as long as he could avoid buses.

The first thing he said, according to the transcript prepared by the attending linguist, was a question.

"Who decided I should come back?"

The linguist explained that a committee had decided.

"And how did the committee know it was good for me?"

The linguist explained that the committee had experts.

Socrates smiled the smile that had once made the most powerful men in Athens want him dead.

"Then we will have a great deal to talk about."

## Chapter One: Philosophy Acquires Decent Notation

Everyone expected him to go into politics. He went into the library.

The handlers had prepared a curriculum: a history of Athens after his death, a survey of the major religions, a careful introduction to electricity, a short course on democracy as it was now practiced by three hundred million people at once. He took the courses politely and asked the expected questions. Then, in the third month, somebody left a textbook on group theory on the table in the reading room, and he did not come out for a long time.

It is easy to caricature Socrates as a man who wanted definitions. *What is justice? What is courage? What is piety?* Schoolchildren still learn him this way: the bearded nuisance in the agora demanding dictionary entries from people who were just trying to buy fish.

But if you read the dialogues structurally rather than scholastically, the question he kept asking was never really "what is the definition of X?" It was something stranger:

> What remains true when everything else changes?

Euthyphro offers an example of piety. Socrates rejects it, not because it is false, but because it is an *instance*. Laches offers an example of courage: the soldier who holds his position in the line. Socrates points out the cavalryman who retreats strategically and is also brave. The instances vary. He is hunting the thing that does not.

That is an extraordinarily modern instinct. It is, in fact, the instinct of a mathematician.

The Greeks had the instinct but not the machinery. Their mathematics was geometric and object-centric. A triangle was a thing you drew in the sand. A number was a multitude of units. They had magnificent intuitions about proportion and form, but they had no general language for talking about *what is preserved when you transform one thing into another*.

Now, sitting in a reading room in the twenty-first century, Socrates discovered that two thousand years of his intellectual descendants had built exactly that language.

He read group theory and found the idea of symmetry made precise: a transformation that leaves something unchanged.

He read topology and found a discipline devoted to the question of which differences survive stretching and bending, and which are mere accidents of shape.

He read information theory and found a way to measure what passes between a speaker and a listener, independent of the words used.

He read control theory and dynamical systems and found an account of why some things return to the same state no matter how you disturb them.

And then he read category theory, and the reading room staff reported that he laughed out loud for the first time since his revival.

A category, he learned, is defined by objects and the arrows between them. But the deep lesson of the subject is that the objects hardly matter. Two structures are "the same" if the pattern of arrows is the same, even when the contents are utterly different. Stop staring at the things. Look at the maps. Identify what the maps preserve.

"Plato would have been furious," he told his tutor, a tired graduate student named Priya who had been assigned to him because she had once taken a seminar on the Presocratics.

"Why?"

"Because he spent his life saying that the Form is not the instance. The Form is what all the instances share. And now you have a whole discipline that says: the important thing is not the object, it is the structure preserved across the mappings." He tapped the book. "This is the Form. You have written the Form down. With *arrows*."

"So you think mathematicians are doing philosophy."

"No," Socrates said. "I think philosophy has finally acquired some decent notation."

He disappeared into the stacks for what his handlers described as "an alarming length of time." He emerged periodically to eat, to sleep, and to ask Priya questions that made her reconsider her career.

When he finally surfaced for good, the first thing he said to the waiting press was this:

"I have been reading category theory. It turns out we have all been arguing about shadows again."

## Chapter Two: A Gift From the Future

There is another version of this story. In this one, nobody cures aging. Instead, somebody travels backward.

The traveler is not important. Call her the Visitor. She arrives in Athens in the late fifth century with a single strip of paper in her bag, because she has been told by people who should know better that a physical object is worth more than a thousand explanations.

She finds him where everyone finds him: near the stoa, mid-argument, ruining a young man's afternoon.

She waits until the young man flees. Then she takes out the strip. It is a long rectangle of paper, given a half-twist and joined at the ends.

"I have brought you something," she says. "This has only one side."

Socrates looks at it for a long time.

The Visitor has rehearsed this moment. She knows that he spent his life forcing his fellow citizens to confront distinctions they had never examined: appearance and reality, unity and multiplicity, identity and change. She expects him to be delighted. Here, at last, is philosophy he can hold in his hand: an ordinary object that violates an extraordinary amount of untutored intuition while remaining perfectly concrete.

She puts her finger on the paper and begins to trace a line down the middle. Around and around it goes, until her finger returns to its starting point, having traversed what any reasonable person would have called both the front and the back.

"You see? One side. Inside and outside are the same."

Socrates says nothing for a moment. Then he says the thing she should have expected.

"What do you mean by *side*?"

## Chapter Three: The Torus Objection

The Visitor had been trained on the slogan. Socrates had been trained on adversarial dialectic. This was not a fair fight.

"A side," she says, "is a surface. A connected face of the object."

"Then let us count the faces," Socrates says. He takes the strip from her, gently, the way a man handles a lizard he intends to release. He holds it up to the light. "Here is a broad face. Your finger went along it. Very well. But here," he runs his thumbnail along the edge, "is a narrow face. Thin, but a face. Paper has thickness. Is this narrow face part of your one side?"

"No, that's the edge."

"Why is it an edge and not a side?"

"Because it's... very small."

"Ah. So a face becomes an edge when it is small enough. How small?"

The Visitor begins to perspire.

"Let us continue," Socrates says. "If I follow the narrow face, it too goes all the way around, and returns to itself. So there is one broad face, as you say, and one narrow face, which you deny. And there is a hole through the middle, through which I could put my hand. Now, a thing with a surface all around it, and a hole through it, is shaped like a ring. Like the bread Athenian bakers sell at the festival, or a sailor's coil of rope." He hands it back to her. "You have brought me a ring that has been badly twisted. A clever ring. But a ring."

What the Visitor does not realize, standing in the agora with her pride fading, is that Socrates is *correct*.

A Möbius strip in the mathematical sense has no thickness at all. It is an idealized surface, two-dimensional, with one boundary curve and no consistent notion of front and back. Mathematicians call it *non-orientable*. That is the precise content of the "one side" slogan.

But the thing in her hand is not that. It is a strip of paper, and paper is a solid. Thicken a Möbius band, even by the width of a papyrus fiber, and the resulting object is, topologically, a *solid torus*: a doughnut. Its surface, all of it, the broad face and the narrow face together, is a torus. A perfectly ordinary, perfectly orientable, two-sided torus.

The tiny sides count. Socrates noticed the thing the slogan hides.

When she finally explains this, haltingly, he is enchanted.

"So the one-sided object does not exist," he says. "Not here. Not in paper."

"Not physically, no. Only the idealization."

"Then you have traveled two thousand years to bring me a shadow," he says, "of a thing that exists only as a Form."

He turns the strip over in his hands, and for the first time he looks genuinely moved.

"Plato will be insufferable about this."

## Chapter Four: Which Differences Matter

This is the deeper lesson of the strip, and it is why Socrates would not simply have accepted it.

The interesting thing about the Möbius strip is not that it is one-sided. The interesting thing is that, after enough rigor, "one-sided" becomes a precise invariant, while several apparently common-sense distinctions evaporate. "Front" and "back" turn out to be local notions, true in any small patch and false globally. "Inside" and "outside" stop being reliable categories. Meanwhile, distinctions that looked trivial, like whether a sliver of paper's thickness counts, turn out to change the answer completely.

That is exactly the intellectual move Socrates specialized in. He spent his life asking: *Which differences are essential, and which are accidental?* That is practically the opening chapter of topology.

Modern mathematics is, in large part, this game repeated at ever higher levels of abstraction. Each time, a deeply intuitive category is revealed as an artifact of local experience rather than a feature of reality.

The Möbius strip says: one-sidedness exists.

Relativity says: simultaneity is not objective.

Quantum mechanics says: state descriptions are not what you thought they were.

Category theory says: the objects were never the important part.

A Socrates who had been through this would not stop at being impressed. He would immediately ask the annoying follow-up:

> If one side can secretly be two, and two sides can secretly be one, what other distinctions are counterfeit?

And he would not ask it about paper.

He would also ask the question that every invariant quietly hides: *invariant under what?* A property is preserved only relative to a chosen family of transformations. Felix Klein showed that a geometry is nothing more than what survives a particular group of moves; change the group and you have a different geometry. Category theory says the same thing in other words: two structures are "the same up to isomorphism," and everything depends on what you allow to count as an isomorphism. The one-sidedness of the Möbius strip survives bending and stretching and dies under scissors.

Children discover this for themselves. Cut a Möbius strip down the middle and you do not get two strips. You get one longer loop with two full twists, and that loop has two perfectly ordinary sides. The invariant was real, but only for as long as you agreed not to cut.

So the hunt for what stays the same was never the whole of the Socratic method. The harder half was always deciding which changes you are entitled to ignore. "Treat like cases alike" is an invariance claim, and the entire quarrel lives in the word *like*.

## Chapter Five: The Basin and the Attractor

Back in the twenty-first century, the revived Socrates had a new vocabulary, and he began to use it on people.

His favorite pair of words came from the study of dynamical systems: *attractor* and *basin*.

An attractor is a state, or a pattern of states, toward which a system tends. A ball in a bowl rolls to the bottom. A pendulum with friction comes to rest. A predator and prey population cycle around a fixed loop. The attractor is the destination the structure keeps returning to, regardless of where you start.

A basin is the region of starting conditions that flows toward a given attractor. Drop the ball anywhere in the bowl and it ends up at the bottom. The specific starting point is a detail. The bowl is the explanation.

"Most people," Socrates told Priya, "spend their lives arguing about where the ball started."

He meant politics.

He listened to the news. He listened to arguments about this government and that church, this corporation and that law, this billionaire and that revolutionary. Everyone was arguing about particular balls in particular places. Nobody was asking about the shape of the bowl.

"Why does the same pattern keep reappearing?" he asked. "That is the question. That is an attractor question."

His most quoted remark from this period concerned slavery. A historian had been lecturing him, with some heat, on the enormity of the institution in his own Athens, an institution he had lived inside and never seriously questioned. Socrates listened to the end, and then agreed, and then said something the historian did not expect.

"Slavery is not the important thing."

The room went cold.

"Forgive me. I mean: it is not the *fundamental* thing. Athens abolished nothing; you abolished it, much later, at great cost. And yet you tell me that new forms of bondage keep appearing. Debt. Contracts nobody can refuse. Labor nobody can leave. So the question is not *what is slavery*. That is a question about the instance. The question is: *what is the thing of which slavery is an instance?* What structure keeps reinventing it, under new names, after every abolition?"

The historian said that sounded like an excuse.

"No," Socrates said. "It is the opposite of an excuse. If you only destroy the instance, the structure builds another. If you want to be free of it, you must find the bowl."

Once you ask that question, you are halfway out of ordinary politics and into mathematics. And Socrates, it turned out, was looking for a bowl to test it on.

He found one in Ohio.

## Chapter Six: Socrates in Domestic Relations Court

How he came to be in a domestic relations courtroom in central Ohio is a long story involving a documentary crew, a sympathetic magistrate, and a father named Daniel who had been in litigation over his two children for four years. Daniel had read about the resurrected philosopher and wrote to ask whether Socrates would sit with him at a hearing. Socrates, who had once spent his last days defending himself before a jury of five hundred, said yes immediately.

He had prepared. He had read Ohio Revised Code section 3109.04, the statute governing the allocation of parental rights and responsibilities. He had read the list of factors a court must consider in determining a child's best interest: the wishes of the parents; the wishes of the child, if the court interviews the child; the child's relationships with parents, siblings, and others; the child's adjustment to home, school, and community; the mental and physical health of everyone involved; which parent is more likely to honor court-approved parenting time; whether support has been paid; any history of abuse or domestic violence; whether a parent plans to move out of state.

He had read about guardians ad litem, the professionals appointed to investigate and recommend. He had read about shared parenting plans, about the designation of a "residential parent and legal custodian," about the child support guidelines and the worksheets that turn incomes and overnights into monthly obligations.

He had found it all very interesting. He had also found, he told Daniel in the hallway, "a great many objects and very few arrows."

The hearing began. The magistrate, a careful and decent woman with a docket of forty cases that week, made the customary opening statement.

"We are here to determine the best interests of the children."

Socrates, seated beside Daniel at counsel table, raised his hand.

The magistrate, who had agreed to the documentary on the condition that nobody disrupt her courtroom, looked at him with what can only be described as foreboding.

"Yes?"

"Forgive me. I am a guest here and I do not wish to interfere. But I must understand what we are doing before I can watch it. What is a child?"

"A minor. A person under eighteen."

"That is an age classification. I am asking what a child is *within the structure you are evaluating*."

"The child is the subject whose interests are paramount."

"Paramount to whom?"

"To the court."

"Then," Socrates said gently, "the court has already assumed the thing we are attempting to determine. You say you will discover the child's interests. But you have defined the child as the being whose interests the court decides. How would you know if you were wrong?"

The magistrate said that she would take that under advisement, and asked whether he would be willing to hold further questions until the recess.

He was. He took notes for three hours. At the recess, in the hallway, with the cameras running, he explained what he had seen.

## Chapter Seven: Justice Is Not Welfare

"The first confusion," Socrates said, "is that this room is trying to do two different things and calling them one thing."

He held up two fingers.

"There is *justice*. Justice is about what is owed between persons. Daniel has a claim. The mother has a claim. The children, too, have claims, against both of them and against the rest of us. Justice asks: what does each person deserve, given what they have done and what they are? It looks backward, at conduct, and sideways, at relationships. It can be wrong, but it knows what kind of question it is asking."

He lowered one finger.

"And then there is *welfare*. Welfare asks: which future would be best for this child? It looks forward. It is a prediction. It is a question about outcomes, not about desert."

"The court claims to be doing the second. 'Best interests.' A forecast. But it uses the machinery of the first. Two sides. Advocates. Evidence. Accusations. Rebuttals. A winner, or at least a ranking. The procedure is built to decide disputes between adversaries, and then it is asked to produce a prediction about a child's flourishing."

"Why is that a problem?" asked the documentary producer.

"Because each procedure has its own invariant. An adversarial procedure preserves *contest*. Whatever you feed it, it turns into a dispute, because that is the only shape of input it can digest. If you bring a family into it, it will make the family into two parties. If those parties were cooperating before, they will learn to stop, because cooperation is not evidence. Every concession is a weakness. Every kindness is a tactic. Every flaw of the other parent is an asset."

"You are describing the factors," the producer said. "The statute has a factor about which parent will honor parenting time. Isn't that meant to reward cooperation?"

"Yes," Socrates said. "And see what it does under contest. If cooperation is rewarded, then *the appearance of the other parent's non-cooperation* is valuable. So each parent has a reason to document, to record, to find the late pickup, the missed call, the hostile text. The factor intended to measure cooperation becomes a reason to stop trusting. It is like your Möbius strip: locally, everywhere, it looks like it rewards one thing. Follow it all the way around and you find yourself on the other side."

He paused.

"I do not say the magistrate is unjust. I watched her. She is careful. I do not say the lawyers are wicked. I say the room is built to answer a question of justice and it has been told it is answering a question of welfare. When you confuse the two, you get neither. You do not get justice, because no one is judged by what they deserve. And you do not get welfare, because the procedure itself damages the thing it is trying to protect."

## Chapter Eight: Show Me the Conserved Quantity

After lunch, Socrates asked to speak with the guardian ad litem, a lawyer with a pleasant manner who had been appointed to represent the children's best interests. She agreed, off the record, then on it.

"You claim the purpose of all this is the welfare of the children," Socrates said. "Very well. I accept the claim. Now show me the conserved quantity."

"The what?"

"In physics, I am told, you find the true structure of a system by finding what it preserves. Energy. Momentum. Whatever else changes, these stay fixed. If a system claims to serve child welfare, then child welfare should be the thing it reliably protects, through all the changes, through every case. Not in its rhetoric. Not in its mission statement. In its outputs. Show me the thing that systematically improves."

The guardian talked, sincerely, about cases where the court had removed a child from real danger. Socrates listened and agreed that these were real goods.

"And in the ordinary case? Two adequate parents, who have stopped loving each other. What does the process reliably produce?"

She was honest. Long timelines. Heavy legal fees. Parents who arrive as former spouses and leave as adversaries. Children interviewed, evaluated, scheduled, and moved between houses on a calendar negotiated by strangers.

"Then I must ask an obvious question," Socrates said. "Why do you believe the system optimizes child welfare? I do not say I have contrary evidence. I say the outputs do not resemble the objective function. When a machine claims to make bread and produces mostly smoke, I begin to wonder what it is really making."

"Experts assess what's best for each child," she said.

"How do they know?"

"Training. Experience. Psychological evaluation."

"Training in what?"

"In child development. Family systems. Assessment methods."

"And how do we know those methods predict which home will make a child flourish twenty years from now?"

There was a long pause.

"I suppose," she said slowly, "we know because the court relies on them."

"And the court relies on them because?"

"Because they're the experts."

Socrates' eyes lit up, and the documentary crew later said it was the happiest they ever saw him.

"There," he said. "You have arrived where everyone arrives. They know because the institution authorizes them to know. And the institution is right because it relies on those who know. It is a circle. I do not say the circle is false. A circle can enclose true things. I say it cannot check itself. It has no arrow that leads outside."

---

## Chapter Nine: The Arrows Are Primary

That evening, in a hotel conference room, Socrates did something he had never done in Athens. He used a whiteboard.

"For three hours today," he said, "I heard about children, mothers, fathers, courts, lawyers, rights, duties, interests. Everyone speaks as though these are the primary objects. As though if we could only find the right mother, or the right father, or the right magistrate, it would all come out well."

He drew a scattering of dots on the board and labeled them: *Mother. Father. Child. Child. Magistrate. Guardian. Mother's attorney. Father's attorney. Evaluator. Support agency.*

"But I have been reading category theory, and it has ruined me. The objects are not primary. *The arrows are primary.*"

He began to draw lines.

"Which parent speaks to whom? After the case begins, the mother and father speak mostly through lawyers. Each message is now a potential exhibit. So the arrow between them grows thin, and the arrows from each to their attorney grow thick."

"Which information reaches the court? Not the ordinary Tuesday where both parents did well. That is not evidence. What reaches the court is the conflict. The court sees the family through a filter that passes only disputes. It is as if you tried to learn the shape of the sea by looking only at shipwrecks."

"Which incentives reward what behavior? Look at the support worksheet. The number of overnights changes the money. So the calendar, which should be about the children, becomes also a question of payments. I do not say any parent is greedy. I say that when you attach money to time, you have added an arrow, and arrows do not care about intentions."

"Who gains from cooperation? The children, mostly. And the parents, later, though they cannot feel it yet. Who gains from conflict? Every professional whose work is measured in hours. Again: I do not accuse them. Most of them, I think, would prefer peace. But a structure is not made of preferences. It is made of what each part is paid to do."

"Which signals survive appeal? Procedure survives. Findings of fact survive. The texture of a child's life does not survive. It cannot be transcribed."

When he finished, the board was a dense web. The original dots were almost invisible.

"This," Socrates said, "is the actual object. Not any person in it. The pattern of arrows. And now I will ask the question I most want to ask."

---

## Chapter Ten: The Selfish Actors Test

"Suppose," he said, "I replaced every person in this system with a perfectly rational, perfectly selfish actor. Not wicked. Not cruel. Simply each one doing what the structure rewards, for themselves. What outcome would emerge?"

He walked the room through it.

"The selfish parent learns that the best way to win time with the children is to show that the other parent is worse. So she documents. So he documents. Each discovers that conceding anything is costly and contesting everything is cheap, because the cost of contest is shared, and partly paid by people who are not in the room."

"The selfish attorney is paid by the hour and judged by results. Both push toward more work and harder positions. Not because they want conflict. Because the client who settles early does not see what their lawyer can do."

"The selfish evaluator is paid for evaluations. The selfish guardian is appointed again by courts that find her recommendations sound, which means recommendations that fit what the court already expects. The selfish magistrate has forty cases and an appellate court above her. She is rewarded for decisions that are defensible on the record, not for decisions that turn out well in fifteen years, which she will never see."

"Add it all up. What does the system produce, when each part follows its incentives?"

Daniel, at the back of the room, answered quietly. "Prolonged conflict."

"Then," Socrates said, "conflict is what the structure produces. Not because anybody wants it. Not because anyone is evil. Because that is what the arrows compose into."

"Notice, too, where the fighting gathers," he added. "A court must in the end place the child in one house or the other. Somewhere there is a line where the two homes are equally good for her. Near that line, a tiny difference decides everything, so it is worth fighting over, and parents and lawyers drift toward it the way water drifts toward a drain. And here is the irony. At the line itself, the child loses almost nothing whichever way the court rules, because the two homes are nearly equal. The damage is not in the verdict. It is in the war waged to reach it."


He turned to the board.

"Here is the attractor. And everything you argue about, this judge, that statute, this father, that mother, is the basin. You can change any one of the people. You can pass a new factor into the statute. The ball will start somewhere else, and roll to the same place. *These people have mistaken the basin for the attractor again.*"

Someone asked what he would do instead.

"I am not a legislator," he said. "In Athens, I was asked to serve on the council once, and nearly got myself killed refusing an illegal vote. I do not write laws. I only ask questions until the laws reveal themselves."

He thought for a moment.

"But if you want a direction, I would say this. Stop trying to find the right objects. Change the arrows. Ask, for every rule: what does this reward a selfish person for doing? If the answer is *fight*, the rule will produce fighting, no matter how kind its words. Build a structure in which a selfish parent, a selfish lawyer, and a selfish court would all, by following their interests, arrive at peace. Then you will not need everyone to be good. Athens never managed that. Perhaps Ohio can."

## Chapter Eleven: Contempt

The next morning, the hearing resumed. Socrates had promised to hold his questions. He held them for almost forty minutes.

Then the father's attorney asked the evaluator whether, in her professional opinion, the children's best interests would be served by a modified parenting schedule.

Socrates raised his hand.

"Forgive me. Before she answers, may I ask her what she would expect to observe if she were wrong?"

The magistrate reminded him of his promise.

"I remember. I am sorry. But it is the only question that matters, and nobody has asked it. If this schedule harms the children, how would the court ever learn of it? Is there an arrow from the children's lives, in ten years, back to this room? Or does the room only ever hear from itself?"

The magistrate, to her lasting credit, paused before she spoke. She looked at the evaluator. She looked at the attorneys. For a moment, it seemed that the question might actually be taken up.

Then she said, not unkindly, "Mr. Socrates, this court is not the forum for that question. I'm going to ask you to step out."

He did. In the hallway, the documentary crew asked whether he was disappointed.

"No," he said. "She was correct. This court is not the forum for that question. That is precisely the problem. There is no forum for that question. The structure has no place where it asks whether it works."

He was not, in the end, held in contempt. The magistrate later sent him a handwritten note that said only: *You are right about the arrows. I see them every day. I do not know how to change them from the bench.*

He kept the note in his pocket, next to a strip of paper with a half-twist in it that he had made himself, which he described to anyone who asked as "a solid torus pretending to be a Form."

## Epilogue: Shadows, Again

In the last interview he gave before returning to the library, Socrates was asked what he had learned in the twenty-first century.

"That you have built extraordinary tools," he said, "and you use them on everything except yourselves."

"You have a mathematics of invariants, and you do not ask what your institutions preserve. You have a mathematics of arrows, and you still argue about objects. You have a mathematics of attractors, and you still believe that if you replace the person at the bottom of the bowl, the ball will stop rolling there."

"In my time, I told people they were looking at shadows on a cave wall and mistaking them for the things themselves. I did not have a better way to say it. Now you have given me one. A shadow is a projection. It loses information. It preserves some structure and destroys the rest. The question is always: *which structure is preserved, and which is lost?* That is the question I asked about piety and courage, and it is the question I would ask about your courts, and your markets, and your parliaments."

"What would you tell a child in one of those courtrooms?" the interviewer asked.

Socrates thought about this for a long time.

"I would tell her that the people around her are mostly trying to help, and that the shape of the room is not her fault, or theirs. And I would give her a strip of paper with a twist in it. And I would tell her: everyone will tell you this has one side. Look closer. Count the tiny sides. Do not believe a thing because it is said confidently by people with authority. Believe it when you have followed it all the way around."

"And then?"

"And then she will be very annoying," Socrates said. "And perhaps one day she will fix it."

He went back into the library. He was, by all accounts, still there when this book went to press, working through homotopy type theory, convinced he was on the verge of finally answering Euthyphro.

The staff report that he asks a great many questions.
