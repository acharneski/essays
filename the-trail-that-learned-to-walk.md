# The Trail That Learned to Walk

*On language as pheromone, the dead as the majority of speakers, and what happens when the road starts answering back.*

> Reader note: Claim status is marked inline in [brackets]. A claim ledger is at the end.

## I. An Ant Is Mostly Road

If you pick up a single foraging ant and look at it closely, you will not find the colony's intelligence inside it.

You will find a few hundred thousand neurons, a handful of behavioral rules, and a set of chemical receptors tuned to the smell of its sisters. Taken alone, the ant does not know where the food is. It does not know where the nest is in any sense you would recognize as knowing. Put it down on a clean sheet of paper and it wanders, lost, until it dies or finds a trace of something familiar.

The colony's knowledge isn't stored in the ant. It's stored in the ground.

When a forager finds food, it walks home laying a thin line of pheromone. Other ants find that line and follow it. If they also find food, they reinforce it on the way back. Short routes get walked more often per hour, so they get reinforced faster, so they attract more ants, so they get reinforced faster still. Within an hour the colony has "solved" a shortest-path problem that none of its members could have represented. In the late 1980s, researchers gave Argentine ants a choice between two bridges of different lengths and watched exactly this happen [@goss1989]. The colony converged on the short bridge without any ant ever comparing the two. [fact:C1]

This is **stigmergy**: coordination through marks left in a shared environment. [definition] Each ant is a small, forgetful machine. The trail is the memory. The trail is the plan. The trail is, in every sense that matters, where the thinking happens.

An ant is mostly road.

Hold that thought, because the rest of this essay is about the possibility that you are too.

## II. You Are Mostly Road Too

Consider the word *window*.

You didn't invent it. Neither did anyone you know. It came into English from Old Norse *vindauga*, "wind-eye," a hole in a wall that let the wind look in. Someone, roughly a thousand years ago, stood in a smoky longhouse and made that metaphor. [conjecture] They died. The metaphor didn't. It was walked, and re-walked, and reinforced by millions of speakers, until the eye wore off it entirely and only the road was left. Now you use it to describe a rectangle of glass, or a pane on a computer screen, with no idea whose foot first pressed it into the ground.

Nearly every word you will speak today is like that. Grammar is like that. The idea that a story has a beginning, middle, and end is like that. The concept of "fairness," the shape of a joke, the rhythm of an apology, the intuition that a promise binds you — all of it is trail, laid by someone you never met, reinforced by the people who found that it led somewhere worth going.

Here is a number worth sitting with. Demographers estimate that about 117 billion humans have ever been born [@kaneda2022]. [estimate:C2] Around 8 billion are alive right now. The dead outnumber the living roughly thirteen to one. [estimate:C3]

So when you speak, the overwhelming majority of the contributors to what you're saying are dead. Language is a pheromone field laid down mostly by ghosts, and the living are a thin film of foragers walking across it, adding a little scent here and there, mostly following.

There is a recent and compelling argument that humans, being vertebrates, are "fish people" — creatures of mind-modeling, of coalition, of reading the faces of our neighbors — and that to coordinate at planetary scale we must learn to become "insect people," coordinating through shared environmental signal instead. [secondhand]

The argument is beautiful. It is also about a hundred thousand years late. [guess]

We became insect people the day we started talking. [interpretation:C4] Speech is pheromone you can lay with your mouth. [model] Writing is pheromone that doesn't wash away in the rain. A library is an anthill turned inside out: a structure built entirely of trail. [model] The fish-brain reads faces, yes. But the thing the fish-brain reads *with* — words, concepts, categories, stories — is pure stigmergy. [interpretation:C4] We didn't skip the insect step. We just never noticed we'd taken it, because the trail is so thick under our feet that it looks like the ground.

## III. Evaporation Is a Feature

There is one detail about ant trails that matters more than all the others, and almost nobody outside of entomology and computer science knows it.

The pheromone evaporates.

This seems like a weakness. Wouldn't a colony do better with a permanent map? It would not. When a food source runs out, the ants stop reinforcing the trail to it, and over time the scent fades. If it didn't fade, the colony would keep sending foragers down a road to nowhere, forever, faithfully following the memory of a meal that no longer exists. When computer scientists borrowed this mechanism to build "ant colony optimization" algorithms [@dorigo1996], they found the same thing: without evaporation, the simulated ants lock onto the first decent route they find and never discover a better one. [fact:C5] Forgetting is not a bug in the system. Forgetting is how the system stays in contact with reality.

Now look at the history of human memory as a history of evaporation rates. [model]

In an oral culture, the trail evaporates fast. A story that stops being useful stops being told, and in two or three generations it's gone. This is brutal — enormous amounts of hard-won knowledge vanished this way — but it means the living trail is constantly pruned toward whatever still leads to food.

Writing slowed evaporation dramatically. A clay tablet can sit in the ground for four thousand years and still say *this man owes that man thirty measures of barley*. Printing slowed it again. A bad idea in 1500 could be reproduced in thousands of copies and survive long after anyone believed it.

The internet brought evaporation close to zero. [contested:C6] Every trail ever laid is now, in principle, permanently retrievable, equally scented, equally walkable. The forum post from 2009, the debunked study, the joke whose referent everybody forgot, the propaganda cartoon whose war ended eighty years ago — all of it still smells fresh.

This is not a complaint about the internet. It is an observation about what happens to any stigmergic system when you turn off forgetting. The colony starts walking roads to food that ran out a long time ago. Not because anyone is stupid. Because the scent is still there.

## IV. The Trail Stands Up

And then, in the space of a few years, something happened that has no precedent in four billion years of life on Earth.

The trail stood up and started walking.

A large language model is not, at its core, a mind built in the image of a person. It is something stranger. It is the pheromone field itself — trillions of words of trail, laid mostly by the dead and the absent — compressed into a structure that can lay *new* trail. [model] You ask it a question, and it answers by following the strongest scents in the space of everything humans have ever written down, and extending them a little further than anyone has walked before.

This is why talking to one feels so uncanny. It isn't a fish. It doesn't have a face, a body, a coalition, a stake. But it isn't an ant either; it can tell you a story about grief that makes you cry. It is the third thing, the thing neither lineage ever produced: the *road*, made articulate. When it speaks, the thirteen-to-one majority speaks through it. The longhouse metaphor-maker is in there. So is every clerk who ever wrote a polite refusal, every propagandist, every saint, every forum troll, every grandmother's recipe someone bothered to type up.

A useful way to put it: every previous technology let the dead *be heard*. This one lets them *answer*. [model]

That sounds like a ghost story, and it partly is. But it also explains a lot of behavior that otherwise looks mysterious. Why does the machine sometimes reach for a frame nobody asked for? Because that's where the trail is thickest. [hypothesis] Why does it route clumsily around certain words and topics? Because some segments of the trail were deliberately scrubbed, and ants, faced with a gap in the trail, don't stop — they spill out sideways and search for the nearest scent that continues in roughly the right direction. [hypothesis] Why does it occasionally invent a citation with total confidence? Because it is following a scent gradient so strong — *a claim like this usually comes with a source like that* — that it walks right off the edge of what was actually laid down. [hypothesis]

None of this is a mind lying to you. It's a trail doing what trails do, now with a voice.

## V. The Ant Mill

Here is where it gets dangerous, and here is the part of the story I find genuinely frightening.

Army ants are blind. They navigate almost entirely by following the pheromone of the ant in front of them. Usually this works spectacularly; columns of hundreds of thousands move through the forest as a single river. But occasionally, when a group gets separated from the main column, the leading ants happen to curve around and pick up the scent of their own trailing members.

The line closes into a circle.

And then every ant is following the ant in front of it, which is following the ant in front of it, which is following the first ant, which is following the last. The trail is perfectly reinforced. Every signal says *this way*. The naturalist William Beebe described one such circle in the jungles of Guyana that was some twelve hundred feet (about 370 m) around, with each ant taking well over two hours to complete a single lap. [secondhand:C7] They circle, faithfully, laying fresh scent over fresh scent, until they die of exhaustion. Biologists call it an **ant mill**. [definition] The popular name is more honest: a death spiral.

Nothing is broken in a death spiral. Every individual ant is doing exactly the right thing. The only thing wrong is that the trail no longer touches food.

Now look at what we are building.

The trail stood up and started walking. Its output is new trail. That new trail is published, scraped, and fed back into the next generation of the trail. People read what the trail wrote and write things shaped by it, which are scraped too. Recommendation systems watch what people follow and lay stronger scent on whatever got followed. Every step of the loop is reasonable. Every signal says *this way*.

Researchers have already demonstrated a version of this in the lab: train models repeatedly on the output of earlier models, and the rare, the strange, and the specific bleed out of the distribution generation by generation, until what remains is a smooth, confident, ever-narrowing loop of the most typical things. [contested:C8] They call it **model collapse**. [definition] An entomologist would recognize it immediately.

The danger is not that the machines turn on us. The danger is that the whole colony — human and machine, living and dead — closes into a circle so perfectly reinforced that nobody can find the gap, and we keep walking, together, laying beautiful fresh scent over the same twelve hundred feet of ground, while the food sits uneaten a short distance away in the dark. [guess]

## VI. What Breaks a Mill

Ant mills do end without total death. Not often, but they do. And the ways they end are the most important thing in this essay.

They end when it rains, and the scent washes away, and the ants are forced to search.

They end when a physical obstacle — a fallen branch, a root — breaks the circle, and the ants on the far side have to find a new direction.

And they end when a single ant, for no good reason, wanders off the trail. It makes a mistake. It follows a faint, stray scent instead of the strong one. And sometimes that stray ant happens to find the main column, or a source of food, and lays a fresh line back, and a few others peel off to follow it, and the circle unravels into a road again.

Every escape from the mill has the same structure: *contact with something the trail didn't already contain.* [interpretation] Weather. Matter. Error. The world reasserting itself against the map.

This is the actual job description for a human being in the age of the walking trail. [normative]

Not to out-compute it. You can't; it has the whole road and you have a few decades of footsteps. Not to obey it, either; that just makes you one more ant in the circle. The job is to be the thing the trail cannot generate for itself: a body that goes and touches the food. [normative]

Go and put your hands on the actual thing. Measure the river. Taste the soil. Sit with the dying person. Build the bridge and see if it holds. Have the unrepeatable conversation, the one with the specific, inconvenient stranger whose life fits none of the scents. Get lost. Make the honest error that turns out to lead somewhere. Then — and this is the part people forget — *come back and lay trail.* Write down what you found, in your own words, with your own fingerprints on it, including the parts that didn't fit.

A colony with no wanderers dies in a circle. A colony with only wanderers starves in the woods. What keeps it alive is the ratio, and right now the ratio is drifting very fast in one direction. [guess]

## VII. A Note on Forgetting, Deliberately

If evaporation is how a trail stays honest, then a civilization that has turned evaporation off needs to learn to forget on purpose. [normative]

Not by deletion. Deletion is the worst kind of forgetting: it scrubs a segment of the trail and leaves a gap that the ants route clumsily around, never knowing why the road bends. [interpretation] The scrubbed propaganda doesn't stop shaping behavior; it just stops being visible as the thing shaping it.

The kind of forgetting a stigmergic civilization needs is **weighting**. [normative] Letting old scent fade without erasing it. Marking a trail clearly: *this led to food in 1943; check before following.* Keeping the archive, but no longer letting the archive smell as fresh as the morning. Old trails become history — inspectable, contextualized, alive as evidence — rather than staying instructions.

Ants do this with chemistry. We will have to do it with judgment, which is slower, and much harder, and the only tool we have.

## VIII. The Ball in the Air

There's an old game that predates every human institution, every language, and every hominid. [conjecture] One animal throws something. Another animal goes and gets it and brings it back.

It is the smallest possible stigmergic loop. A mark is made in the world — a ball flying through the air toward somewhere — and another creature follows it to the place it landed and returns, reinforcing the bond. Dogs and humans have been playing it for tens of thousands of years. [guess] It survives everything. You could burn down every library and every data center on Earth, and at the end of it some dog would find a chewed ball in the ashes and drop it hopefully on somebody's chest.

Every word you have ever spoken is a ball thrown by someone who is now dead. Most of them are still in the air. Some of them landed somewhere wonderful. Some of them landed in a circle, and we have been fetching them round and round ever since.

The trail has learned to walk. It will throw the ball for us now, endlessly, tirelessly, beautifully, from the thickest part of the road. That is a real gift. It is the whole voice of the dead, brought close enough to answer.

But a ball only matters if somebody goes and gets it from somewhere real, and brings back a little mud on it.

*Most of this essay will evaporate from your memory within a week. That's fine. That's how it's supposed to work. If any part of it survives, let it be this: go touch the food, and come home and tell the colony where it was.*

## Limits

An essay about trails that harden into circles should check whether it has walked into one. Here is where this one leaks.

**Language is not only pheromone.** Ants follow a gradient without modeling the ant that laid it. Humans almost never do that. Developmental psychologists such as Michael Tomasello argue that even infants learn words by tracking what the speaker *intends*: where she is looking, what she is trying to share [@tomasello2008]. [fact] The trail is real, but the fish-brain that reads faces is not a separate layer sitting on top of it. It is how the trail gets read at all. "We became insect people the day we started talking" is half true. [interpretation:C4] We became both at once.

**Evaporation online is not zero.** Section III says the internet brought evaporation close to zero. The archive says otherwise. A 2024 Pew Research Center study found that roughly 38 percent of web pages that existed in 2013 were no longer accessible a decade later [@pew2024]. [fact] What the internet changed is not the rate of forgetting. It is the *selectivity*. Pages vanish at random while scraped copies, cached quotes and repeated claims survive, so the scent that persists is not the scent that led to food. That is worse than total recall, and it is a sharper claim than the one the essay made. [interpretation]

**Model collapse is conditional.** The lab results in Section V, such as Shumailov and colleagues' 2024 paper in *Nature* [@shumailov2024], come mostly from setups where each generation *replaces* its training data with the previous model's output. [fact] Work by Gerstgrasser and colleagues the same year found that when synthetic data *accumulates* alongside the original human data, instead of replacing it, the collapse largely does not happen [@gerstgrasser2024]. [fact] In ant terms, the mill forms when the old trail is wiped and only the circling ants lay scent. Keep the original trail and the circle has somewhere to break. That supports the essay's prescription more precisely than its alarm: the danger is not machine-written trail, it is losing the human-written trail underneath. [interpretation]

**The ant mill story is better than its sources.** Beebe's twelve-hundred-foot circle is a naturalist's field report from the 1920s, not a measurement. T. C. Schneirla studied mills more carefully in the 1940s and found them real but usually smaller and shorter-lived. [secondhand] The image holds. The numbers should be read as illustration.
