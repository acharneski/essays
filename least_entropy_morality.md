# The Alignment Manifesto

## Toward a Politics of Institutional Coherence

---

## Preamble

Every political tradition begins by telling us what we ought to want. The faithful say we should want holiness, the nationalist glory, the revolutionary justice through the abolition of classes, and the technocrat efficiency. Each claims special access to the Good, and each has built institutions on that claim and demanded obedience in its name.

We begin somewhere humbler and, we believe, firmer.

**People have interests, and people respond to incentives.**

That is the descriptive whole of our starting point. We do not assume that people are good or that they are wicked. We do not assume that they are equal in talent, wealth, or virtue. We assume only that each person is an agent who prefers some outcomes to others and acts, within the limits of their knowledge and power, to bring those outcomes about. To this we add one further commitment, which we state plainly in Section III instead of hiding: that each person's interests count, and count roughly equally. Many will read this as a moral axiom. It is compatible with one, but we hold it for a harder-headed reason. It is the least-entropy model of a society. It is the only accounting in which no agent's pressure goes unrecorded, and so the only one that does not have to be propped up by force.

From this modest premise, we argue, a surprising amount of political wisdom can be reconstructed. It is the wisdom of building a society in which the pursuit of one person's interest tends to serve the interests of others, rather than defeat them.

This is our manifesto. It is not a program for a party or a plan for a revolution. It is a standard against which any program, party, or revolution may be measured.

---

## I. The Central Claim

**A society is healthy to the degree that its institutions make each person's success depend on helping others succeed, and sick to the degree that they make it depend on blocking others from pursuing their interests.**

We call this the principle of *incentive alignment*. It is not a theory of what people should want. It is a theory of how a society should be organized, given that people want different things.

Institutions shape the landscape on which agents optimize. On some landscapes, optimization produces cooperation: the baker prospers by feeding the town, the doctor by healing it, and the inventor by making the work of others easier. On other landscapes, optimization produces predation: the official prospers by extracting bribes, the monopolist by excluding rivals, and the master by suppressing the servant.

Agents are much the same across these landscapes. The terrain differs. The terrain also shapes what agents come to want and to expect, a complication we take up in Section III. Where we find a society full of predators, we should first suspect the terrain rather than the people.

Political questions should therefore be asked in a new order. We should stop beginning with "Who is virtuous?" or "Who has the right to rule?" and begin with a different one: **what are the incentive gradients here, and do they point in the same direction or in opposite ones?**

## II. Why We Start Here: The Case of Slavery

The strongest test of any political foundation is whether it can condemn the worst things without smuggling in assumptions it cannot defend. Slavery is the oldest such test.

We do not need a vote to condemn slavery. We do not need a full theory of equality, or of natural law, or a revealed commandment. We need only notice the structure, and grant one thin premise: that the slave's interests count.

The slave has interests. The slave prefers better conditions to worse, autonomy to coercion, the fruit of their own labor to its confiscation, and flourishing to mere survival. These preferences exist whether or not the slave can vote, speak, or be heard by any institution.

The master's gain depends on suppressing those preferences. Every improvement in the slave's condition is a cost to the master, and every increase in the master's profit is a harm to the slave. The relationship is **adversarial by construction**: one agent's optimization process is the other's obstacle.

That is why slavery is condemned by nearly every moral system that has survived contact with reality. Those systems disagree about *why* it is wrong, but they are all detecting the same structural fact, a persistent and massive divergence of incentives, held in place by force, and the damage that this force does to everyone around it.

Notice what this argument does not require. It does not require the enslaved to have the franchise before their situation counts as a wrong. Voting has not yet entered the picture. Representation is one way in which misalignment becomes visible to institutions, but the misalignment exists whether or not anyone is counting.

### The whip does not stay with the slaves

It is tempting to think the argument stops here, so that slavery fails our test only because we choose to count the enslaved. A slave economy can look superbly aligned from the master's side: the overseer is rewarded for the whip, and the enslaved are driven by it to comply. On that reading, a society that simply declined to count its slaves would pass.

It would not, and seeing why is the core of our thesis. Suppression is not a closed transaction between master and slave. It is a practice, and practices spread.

Consider what a slave society must build to keep its slaves.

- **Patrols.** Patrols need men, and in the American South those men were drawn largely from poor whites who owned no one.
- **Silence.** The enslaved must not learn that escape or revolt is thinkable, so the society must police what *free* people say and read. Southern states kept abolitionist literature out of the mails, and the House of Representatives refused to hear antislavery petitions for eight years. In 1857 Hinton Rowan Helper, a white Southerner, argued that slavery impoverished the white men who held no slaves. His book was banned, and men were prosecuted for circulating it.
- **A doctrine that some people may be owned by others.** That doctrine does not respect the line it was invented to guard. George Fitzhugh, slavery's most consistent defender, followed it to its end and argued that free laborers would be better off enslaved too.
- **A household run on command.** The household that holds slaves organizes itself around command, and the wife, the children, and the servants live under the same logic as the field.

The whip that begins with the slave moves on to the poor, to women, to children, and to neighbors.

Coercion is also habit-forming in the person who practices it. Jefferson, himself a slaveholder, saw this plainly. He wrote that the commerce between master and slave is "a perpetual exercise of the most boisterous passions," and that "our children see this, and learn to imitate it." The whip is a drug. A master who can command need never learn to persuade, bargain, invent, or earn cooperation, and those capacities wither in him, in his heirs, and in the institutions built around him. The slaveholder is not merely wicked. He is dysfunctional, and his dysfunction is contagious.

Then there is the cost in energy. An enslaved population is an optimizing force that has been driven underground, not removed. It works slowly, breaks tools, runs away, and sometimes rises in revolt. Every unit of labor taken from it must be paid for in surveillance and force, and the enforcement grows with the gap it holds open.

Sparta is the clearest case. To hold the helots down, its citizens turned themselves into a permanent garrison. Their magistrates declared war on the helots every year, they trained their young men to hunt helots, and they lived under a regime of mutual surveillance hardly gentler than the one they imposed. The citizen body shrank, and the army could seldom be risked far from home. When Thebes freed Messenia after Leuctra, the Spartan order never recovered, and later kings, Cleomenes III and Nabis, freed helots in order to rebuild an army. Sparta learned, too late, that the gap it had maintained had consumed it.

Britain's abolition is usually told as a story of Christian conscience. Conscience was present, and the abolitionists deserve their honor. But conscience had been present for generations without ending anything. Britain did not free its slaves because Christian morality finally prevailed. It freed them when the system had become dysfunctional on terms the empire itself could read:

- repeated revolts, culminating in the Jamaican rising of 1831–32 and the reprisals that followed it;
- the expense of garrisons;
- a growing sense, later argued by Eric Williams, that free labor and open trade served Britain better than the plantation monopoly.

The settlement is telling. The Act of 1833 paid twenty million pounds in compensation, all of it to the owners and none to the enslaved. That is not how a moral reckoning is settled. It is how a failing investment is bought out.

Historians still dispute the weight of each factor. Seymour Drescher has shown that the plantations remained profitable on the owners' books when abolition came. But the owners' books are exactly the narrow accounting our thesis rejects. The question is not whether slavery paid the slaveholder. It is what slavery cost the society that hosted it.

So the equal counting of interests is not a moral premise that we add to the analysis to get the right answer about slavery. It is what the analysis finds when the books are kept honestly. A society that discounts some of its members does not make their interests disappear. It moves them off the ledger, where they keep acting as unpriced forces. The society then pays to suppress them: in coercion, in surveillance, in lost capacity, and eventually in the liberty of the people who thought they were exempt. Counting everyone at roughly face value is the configuration that takes the least energy to hold together. That is what we mean by calling it the least-entropy model.

This is the first strength of our position. It identifies injustice from the geometry of the relationship, and from the dynamics that geometry sets in motion, rather than from the outcome of a poll or the verdict of a creed.

## III. What We Presuppose

A manifesto that claims to start from little should say exactly what the little is.

Alignment is not a one-place property. To say that an arrangement aligns incentives is to say that it makes agents' self-interested action converge on some *target*, across some *population*. Without a target and a population the word has no content. Selecting them looks like a purely normative act, and in part it is. But the population, at least, is far less ours to choose than it seems, for reasons we give below. Alignment is therefore a criterion of institutional *competence*, conditional on a stated target. It does real work by exposing proposals that sound good but cannot survive the incentives of the people who must carry them out. But it cannot, by itself, tell us what is worth carrying out.

### The one premise

Our target is stated in a single sentence:

> **Every person's interests count, they count roughly equally, and they are not wholly constituted by the institutions being judged.**

This is not a theory of the Good. It does not say what lives people should lead. It is, rather, a thin conception of the Right, and we do not claim it is a precondition of every possible evaluation, or that it is neutral. A committed traditionalist or a Platonic guardian can reject it, and we have not refuted them merely by declining to address them.

Our defense is more modest. The premise is (a) widely shared across otherwise conflicting views, (b) needed to avoid a circularity we describe below, and (c) minimally committal about what lives people should lead. Whether that makes it thin enough is a matter of judgment, not proof. We are also unsure how much weight (a) can bear, since "widely shared" may only mean shared among those who have been asked.

The word *interest* itself deserves a note. To call something an interest, rather than a passing desire, is already to distinguish what serves a person from what they happen to want in the moment. That brings in a thin theory of agency, and we accept it.

### Three ways to name the target

There are at least three ways to say whose interests the alignment is with, each reducing what must be assumed and each leaving a residue.

**Mutual advantage.** The target is the interests of the participants themselves: an institution is better if it makes cooperation individually rational and mutually improving. This avoids an external Good. But it presupposes that persons, not groups or traditions, are the unit of concern, that the consent or advantage of actual people is what matters, and a baseline. Improvement relative to what? A baseline of existing holdings may smuggle in a view of what people are owed, so we state our baselines as choices, not discoveries.

**Accountability.** The target is the interests of the governed, and institutions are judged by how closely they tie rulers' incentives to citizens'. This presupposes who counts as principal, which is a question of membership and standing. Our answer, "each person affected," is the one in the premise above. The least-entropy argument explains why the answer cannot be narrower: anyone who is affected but not counted remains a force acting on the system.

**Robustness.** Design institutions to function even if the agents in them are self-interested, as Hume advised in his maxim of the knave. This is a feasibility constraint and not a standard of goodness. It tells us what will not collapse, not what is worth building. We use it as a discipline on proposals, never as a justification of them.

### Interests made by institutions

The hardest problem for any theory that starts from interests is that institutions shape the interests they are then judged against. Those who are oppressed learn to lower their expectations, and a thoroughly bad arrangement can look well-aligned because its victims have stopped hoping for better. If we judged institutions by the preferences people currently express, we would be moving in a circle.

We therefore do not treat every expressed preference as evidence of an interest. We treat a preference as evidence only if it was formed under conditions the person would not reject on reflection: without manipulation, with adequate information, with exposure to real alternatives, and without fear. This is the diagnostic that Sen and Elster developed for adaptive preference, or "sour grapes." It does not say what people should want. It says only what must hold for their wants to count. It filters inputs and leaves open how the filtered evidence is weighed.

Where a procedural filter is too thin, a second resource is the set of all-purpose conditions that nearly any life plan requires: health, bodily integrity, association, education. This is the move of Rawls and Nussbaum. It is more substantive, but it was designed to be compatible with many conceptions of the good.

A third resource is negative. Instead of building a positive theory of flourishing, we can identify clear *bads* (cruelty, domination, deprivation) and rank institutions comparatively by how much of them they produce. People disagree more about the good life than about the badness of being terrorized, so this approach needs less agreement. It is not free of the problem, since adaptive preference distorts even reports of suffering, and since bads conflict, as when security trades against liberty. We use it as a check, not as a foundation.

### What kind of thinness this is

"Thin" is not a yes-or-no property, and we claim it along three dimensions only.

- **Content.** The filter does not rank ways of life. It ranks only the conditions under which a person chooses among them.
- **Justificatory base.** It can be accepted from many standpoints for different reasons. A commitment against cruelty and for the conditions of real choice is plausibly shared widely, though not universally. Some traditions reject autonomy as a standard of life. We claim broad overlap, not neutrality.
- **Role.** It determines no outcomes. It decides which preferences count as evidence.

What remains is a minimal core. It is grounded mostly in function and partly in judgment, and it is not a neutral precondition. We prefer to name it than to be caught carrying it.

## IV. Democracy Is an Instrument, Not a Foundation

We do not oppose democracy. We are among its most committed defenders. But we defend it for a reason that differs from the usual one.

Many theories hold that legitimacy flows from the will of the majority. This leaves them with an uncomfortable question: if a majority votes to enslave a minority, on what ground is that choice illegitimate? Their answers tend to be awkward, appealing to rights that their own foundations cannot generate.

Our framework has no such difficulty. A policy that enslaves a minority fails the alignment test **regardless of how many people voted for it**. It fails the majority too. As Section II showed, the machinery built to hold down the minority will not stay pointed at them. The test is not who decided but what incentive structure the decision creates.

Democracy, on this view, is an **epistemic tool**. It helps reveal interests, detect misalignments, and update institutions when they fail. Elections, free assemblies, a free press, and open courts are valuable because they carry information about where incentives have come into conflict, and they carry it faster and more cheaply than any alternative we know.

This also means the framework is, in principle, indifferent to the label on the regime. A benevolent monarchy, a technocracy, a direct democracy, and a federation of local councils can all be evaluated by the same measure: **does the resulting system align the optimization of individuals, families, communities, and society, or does it place them in structural conflict?**

In practice, we expect democratic institutions to score well, because power that cannot be corrected tends to drift toward serving itself. But the reason is empirical and structural, not sacred.

## V. Rights as Discoveries

We hold that rights are not best understood as axioms handed down from above. They are better understood as **alignment technologies**: solutions that societies have repeatedly discovered because they reduce conflict and lower the cost of cooperation.

| Institution | What it does for alignment |
| :---- | :---- |
| Property rights | Reduces conflict over scarce resources |
| Freedom of speech | Preserves the information flow needed to detect misalignment |
| Contract law | Makes cooperative incentives predictable |
| Due process | Prevents arbitrary distortions of incentives by those in power |
| Freedom of movement | Lets agents leave poor environments and seek better ones |
| Abolition of slavery | Eliminates a fundamentally adversarial incentive relation |

Our approach changes the justification for these institutions but not their survival. The claim is no longer that "free speech is an inherent right," which a skeptic can simply deny. The claim is that suppressing speech blinds the society to its own errors, corrupting the signals it needs to stay coherent. A skeptic must then argue against the mechanism, not merely against an assertion.

A foundation of this kind has a further virtue: it explains why rights fail when they are merely declared. A right that cannot be enforced, or that is routinely outweighed by the incentives of those who administer it, is not an aligned institution. It is a decoration.

There is a circularity here that we accept. Rights are tools for implementing a target, and they also help define the target, as limits on what any acceptable outcome may involve. We do not think the circle is vicious, but it means that the vocabulary of evaluation and the vocabulary of engineering cannot be cleanly separated.

## VI. What This Manifesto Replaces, and What It Does Not

We are not proposing a complete theory of government. It would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. Incentive alignment is a criterion for evaluating institutional structures, not a full account of political life.

### What it replaces

It replaces **theories of legitimacy based on special access to the Good.** A government need not be righteous, historically destined, or the voice of the nation. It must be a mechanism that turns private optimization into cooperative outcomes.

It also replaces much of **government as moral arbitration**, the habit of states deciding which lifestyles are desirable, which beliefs acceptable, and which virtues compulsory. The proper role of the state, on our account, is closer to that of a mechanism designer:

1. Detect incentive misalignments.
2. Internalize externalities, so that those who impose costs bear them.
3. Dismantle exploitative equilibria such as fraud, corruption, rent-seeking, and regulatory capture.
4. Facilitate positive-sum cooperation.
5. Protect the information channels through which misalignments are discovered.

### What it does not replace

It does **not** replace the formation of values. Someone must still decide whether to prize growth or sustainability, security or liberty, cultural preservation or openness, the present or future generations. Apart from the thin core of Section III, our theory is silent on these questions. In engineering terms, it is an *optimizer*, not an *objective function*. It can say: given these values, this institutional design aligns incentives better than that one. It cannot say whether the values are right.

It does **not** replace courts. Disputes persist even in a perfectly aligned society: Was the contract valid? Did this person commit the act? Does this land belong to A or to B? These are questions of adjudication, not alignment.

It does **not** replace defense. A society may be internally coherent and still face external threats, and coordinated force remains necessary against those who stand outside the alignment.

It does **not** replace aggregation. Interests conflict, so "the interests of the people" requires a rule for weighing them, and the impossibility results of social choice theory warn us that no such rule is a neutral technical step. Our test can tell a society that an arrangement sets its members against one another. It cannot tell the society how to split what is scarce.

The analogy we find most useful is thermodynamics. Thermodynamics does not tell you whether to build a car, a rocket, or a toaster. It tells you which designs will waste energy. Likewise, our theory does not tell a society what to want. It tells a society that, whatever it wants, some institutional arrangements convert conflicting interests into cooperation more efficiently than others.

## VII. The Articles

From these premises we derive the following commitments.

**Article 1. No institution shall depend on the systematic suppression of another person's interests.**
Slavery, serfdom, debt bondage, and every disguised relative of them are condemned by this article, whatever name they go by and whatever majority supports them. We condemn them not only for what they do to the bound but for what they do to everyone else. Coercion learned on one class does not stay with that class.

**Article 2. Those who impose costs shall bear them.**
Where a person can profit by pushing the costs of their activity onto others, the gradients diverge. Institutions must be designed so that private gain does not require public loss.

**Article 3. Power shall not be able to insulate itself from correction.**
Every office, public or private, must be answerable to channels that can reveal its failures: free speech, a free press, transparent accounts, independent courts, and the freedom to exit. This applies to the system as a whole and not to every institution within it. A society may contain bodies whose commitments are irrevocable, provided it remains one in which leaving is possible and the conditions of correction stay intact (see Section IX).

**Article 4. Rules shall be predictable.**
Cooperation requires that agents can anticipate the consequences of their actions. Arbitrary power, retroactive punishment, and capricious enforcement distort incentives as surely as theft.

**Article 5. Rents shall be distrusted.**
Wherever income flows from position rather than contribution, from the capture of a regulator, the control of a chokepoint, or the manufacture of scarcity, the person's interest has been decoupled from the interests of others. Such arrangements must be justified, bounded, and regularly reviewed.

**Article 6. Exit shall be real.**
The freedom to leave a bad arrangement, whether an employer, a town, a contract, or a jurisdiction, is the most powerful alignment mechanism there is, because it forces institutions to compete to be worth staying in. Real means more than formal. A door that can be opened only at the price of one's children, one's livelihood, and everyone one loves is not a door, and costs imposed by the institution itself count against it.

**Article 7. Institutions shall be tested, not worshipped.**
Every arrangement, including those we propose here, is a hypothesis about incentive structure. Those that fail must be changed, and changing them must be possible. What may be entrenched is the machinery of revision (free speech, elections, amendment procedures), not first-order outcomes removed from revision.

## VIII. The Test We Propose

We offer one question, to be asked of every law, institution, and policy:

> **Given a population of agents with diverse goals, does this arrangement make each person's success depend on others' success, or on others' frustration?**

The closer a society gets to "your success depends on others succeeding," the more aligned it is. The closer it gets to "your success depends on blocking others from pursuing their interests," the more pathological it is.

This is a test of **coupling structure**, not of ultimate values. It lets us condemn fraud, corruption, regulatory capture, and exploitation without first resolving our disagreements over religion, equality, nationalism, or the good life. It also lets people who disagree about all of those things agree on the shape of the institutions they will share.

## IX. Hard Cases: Binding, Shunning, and the Closed Community

Articles 3 and 6 are the ones most likely to be turned against ways of life we do not share, so they deserve to be tested against the hardest cases. We take three: people who bind themselves and their successors, groups whose members shun those who leave, and children.

### Binding oneself and one's successors

Suppose people who have had real exit, voice, and exposure to alternatives reflectively bind themselves to an order that forecloses future contestation: an irrevocable vow, an entrenched clause. Does our test count their informed endorsement?

We say it counts as authoritative evidence of *their* interests, and not of their successors'. The test certifies preferences formed with exit, voice, and alternatives. The founders met those conditions. The children born into an entrenched order, with no exit, did not. So the priority we give to revisability is not a priority over present informed choice as such. It is a priority over one party's choice on behalf of another.

Two distinctions soften the case further.

- **Contestability belongs to the ecology, not to each institution.** A monastery with irrevocable vows can sit inside a society where leaving remains possible, where the state will not compel return, and where children can learn of alternatives. The vow forecloses nothing at the system level, and our criterion applies to the system.
- **Entrenchment can serve revisability.** Eternity clauses that protect free speech, elections, and amendment procedures are Ulysses-style precommitments that keep the conditions of correction intact. The test is whether the entrenched content protects the machinery of revision or removes first-order outcomes from it. The first is compatible with our criterion, and the second is not.

What, then, grounds the priority? A commitment that future persons have standing as evaluators of their own lives, and that their interests are not exhausted by what their predecessors judged good for them. We offer three reasons for it:

- **Epistemic.** Institutions shape the preferences of those raised within them, so the successors' endorsement is exactly the evidence the institution can corrupt.
- **Irreversibility.** Foreclosure destroys option value under uncertainty about what people will come to need.
- **Consistency.** Those who bind their successors typically claim that their successors' interests matter, that the order will be good *for* them. That claim carries the standing we are invoking.

This is a commitment to persons as continuing judges of their lives, not to autonomy as an ideal of how to live. It is still a normative commitment, and a thoroughgoing traditionalist can reject it. Nor do we distrust endorsement as such. We distrust *uncheckable* endorsement. If members could leave at tolerable cost, know the alternatives, and stay, that is Hirschman's loyalty, and it is good evidence. If endorsement is produced by the institution and exit is closed by threat, ignorance, or the loss of family and community, the evidence is compromised.

The hardest case is a single-generation, fully informed, unanimous, irrevocable vow with no successors. Our criterion is institutional and licenses no intervention. But it does give reason against *legally enforcing* irrevocability, as with Mill's argument that freedom does not include the freedom to sell oneself into slavery. That rests on a substantive commitment to autonomy, and we say so.

### Shunning

Shunning is both the members' exercise of their freedom of association and the very thing that makes exit costly. Our criterion does not forbid any member from refusing contact with anyone. It says that endorsement produced under institutionally imposed exit costs carries less evidential weight. That is a claim about evidence.

But a discount has consequences for what the state enforces or privileges: custody rules that let a group's shunning decide a parent's access, legal recognition of internal penalties, exemptions that leave members with no recourse. So it does burden the group's capacity to reproduce itself through exit costs, and we concede that.

What ranks the two liberties? The group's associational right is derivative of its members' interest in free association, so it cannot be used to defeat the voluntariness that grounds it. Two further points help.

- **The symmetry is only apparent.** Withdrawing club goods the group itself supplies is a cost incidental to membership. Withholding goods that are not the group's own to withhold, such as a parent's relationship with their children, kin outside the group, and a livelihood, uses third-party relationships as leverage. Organized shunning also constrains the members who would otherwise keep contact, so the group's liberty against the ex-member is partly purchased at the expense of its own members' liberty.
- **A purpose test, with limits.** We can ask whether the cost is designed to deter exit or merely follows from the group's nature. But expulsion deters exit in every club, so the test only separates cases at the margin. Kukathas would say that formal exit is enough and that shunning is the price of dissent. We reject that, and the rejection rests on a substantive claim about what counts as real exit. We cannot make that claim neutral.

### Children

Children are the main case where consent is impossible. Does the exposure and capacity-building we owe them collapse into the perfectionism we claimed to avoid? Partly. The difference is real in justification and thin in consequence.

It differs in kind in three ways.

- **Ground.** Perfectionism justifies action by the value of a life shaped by autonomy. Our view justifies it by what is owed to the child as a future evaluator, whatever she concludes. Feinberg's "right to an open future" is the model.
- **Success criterion.** Our view is outcome-indifferent. It is met if the informed adult stays. A perfectionist counts the autonomous life as better.
- **Stopping rule.** Sufficiency rather than promotion. The requirements are knowing that alternatives exist, being able to reason about them, and having some contact with outsiders. They do not include being raised to prize autonomy, equal exposure to every way of life, or a ban on religious formation. Capacity is required, not disposition.

It differs only in degree in three others. Specifying the threshold of capacities needs some view of which capacities matter, which is thicker than we would like. The distinction between aim and effect is fragile: exposure and reasoning predictably raise exit rates, so the state foresees the effect even if it does not intend it, and cases like *Yoder* show that the burden falls disproportionately on the group. And Raz's autonomy-perfectionism and our view prescribe nearly the same provision. They diverge in ground and in where they stop, not in what the school does on Monday.

We think the difference in justification still matters. It determines what can be said to a dissenting parent ("this is owed to her whatever she decides," rather than "autonomy is better"), it supplies limits a perfectionist rationale lacks, and it keeps the state's claim modest.

### What counts as evidence

How would anyone know that a community is closing off revision? Not from exit rates, dissent, or perceived cost. Low exit is exactly what we would see both where members endorse and where exit is foreclosed, so any test that discounts endorsement on the basis of exit rates is circular. A threshold that tracked such outcomes would be useless, and one that tracked a judgment about a community's character would be the targeted scrutiny we reject.

There is a third category of evidence, which concerns the rules and the state's own involvement. We look for three things that can be observed without knowing what members want:

- Whether the group's sanctions attach to exit or to questioning, as a matter of stated policy.
- Whether the group controls goods that are not its own to withhold: custody, housing, immigration sponsorship, wages, and credentials.
- Whether it restricts access to information, such as literacy, an outside language, or contact with non-members.

These attach to *practices*, not to communities, so any group adopting the practice triggers them. And the threshold varies with what the state is asked to underwrite. A group that asks the state to lend its power to its exit costs, through exemptions, custody deference, or enforcement of internal penalties, bears a burden of justification that a group operating privately does not.

A further source is elasticity. If the state offers a universal exit-support package, such as housing assistance, legal aid, and a route to contact with children, then the change in exit as costs fall tells us something. Low exit that persists makes endorsement more plausible, and a jump suggests foreclosure was doing the work. But elasticity is slow and noisy. Adaptive preferences can survive cost reductions for years, and people may distrust a support package offered by a state they regard as hostile. Evidence of this kind is defeasible, never decisive.

### What this costs

We state the cost plainly.

- **Information-control rules touch doctrine.** If avoiding outside information is part of a group's religion, then judging that rule as a threat to revision is a judgment about the practice's content. "Structural" and "doctrinal" are not cleanly separable here.
- **Practice-targeting has disparate impact.** Shunning or information-restriction rules cluster in particular communities, so the burden falls unevenly even when the rule is facially general. We would defend this only if the trigger is tied to the practice and to the state's role in it.
- **Individual standing leaves silent members unprotected.** We prefer to let members who say they cannot leave bring their own claims, rather than have the state assess whole communities. But this misses members who do not complain, and that gap is exactly what the ambiguity between endorsement and foreclosure predicts.
- **Informal coercion that never touches state power escapes.** If the threshold rises only when the state is asked to act, our criterion protects chiefly against harms the state could cause or underwrite. The rest falls to civil society and the other Articles.

The resulting position is this. The threshold is fixed at the level of principle: an informed adult must be able to stay or leave. It varies at the level of application, with rule features and the state's involvement, not with outcomes or community identity. The state is still making judgments, but about which *rules* are compatible with revision, not which *communities* are acceptable. That distinction is real, though not clean. It limits scrutiny to what can be stated, contested, and reviewed in advance. Some groups will be over-scrutinized because they share a practice with a coercive one, and some under-scrutinized because their exit costs are informal. We accept that as the price of public criteria over case-by-case verdicts on ways of life.

---

## X. Where We Stand Among the Traditions

We do not claim to have invented a new ideology out of nothing. This is better understood as a meta-theory that evaluates ideologies from the outside, and it borrows from many of them.

**Liberalism.** We share its insight that people generally know their own interests better than rulers do. We differ in treating rights as alignment technologies rather than foundations. The liberal says free speech is good because people have a right to speak. We say it is good because suppressing information causes systemic failure.

**Utilitarianism.** Utilitarianism asks what maximizes welfare. We ask what aligns optimization gradients. A utilitarian can, in principle, justify large coercion if the arithmetic comes out favorably. We distrust coercive structures because coercion is often the visible sign of underlying divergence. Our target is the structure of the optimization, not the sum of its outputs.

**Social contract theory.** Hobbes, Locke, and Rousseau asked why rational individuals would agree to live together. We offer a mechanistic rewrite: the contract is less a philosophical agreement than an emergent equilibrium, reached when institutions reduce conflict enough for cooperation to be self-reinforcing.

**Public choice theory.** This is our closest intellectual cousin. It treats politicians, bureaucrats, voters, and lobbyists as self-interested agents rather than saints. But public choice is largely diagnostic: it explains how government fails. We are normative: we describe what a successful arrangement looks like.

**Libertarianism.** Libertarians often treat voluntary exchange as inherently legitimate. We ask a further question. A transaction may be voluntary and still create a harmful incentive structure downstream. The question is not only "was it voluntary?" but "what does it set in motion?"

**Marxism.** Marx also analyzed incentive structures and the divergent interests of capital and labor, and we agree that this is an alignment question. But we have no need for historical destiny, a privileged class, or a revolutionary conclusion. We can describe the conflict without presuming which side is morally anointed.

**Cybernetics and systems theory.** Philosophically, this is where we belong. The basic unit of analysis is neither the individual nor the state. It is the coupling between agents.

## XI. Elinor Ostrom and the Evidence from the Commons

Our nearest empirical ally is Elinor Ostrom, and the comparison deserves to be made carefully, both because she supports us and because she disciplines us.

Ostrom studied communities that managed shared resources, such as Alpine meadows in Switzerland, irrigation systems in Spain and the Philippines, forests in Japan, and fisheries in Maine. Conventional theory predicted that such commons would collapse in a tragedy of overuse, each user rationally depleting what all depended on. Some did collapse. Many did not, and had survived for centuries.

Her explanation was not that these people were unusually virtuous. It was that they had built **institutions that aligned individual incentives with the health of the resource**. Her well-known design principles include clearly defined boundaries, rules matched to local conditions, participation of those affected in changing the rules, monitoring by people accountable to the users, graduated sanctions, accessible conflict resolution, recognition of the community's right to organize, and, for larger systems, nested layers of governance.

Every one of these can be read as an alignment device. Monitoring makes defection visible. Graduated sanctions make it costly without making the system brittle. Participation in rulemaking keeps the rules in line with the knowledge and interests of those who live under them.

**Where we agree with Ostrom.** The tragedy of the commons is not a law of nature but a property of a particular incentive landscape. Change the landscape and the outcome changes. Neither state control nor privatization is the only escape. Governance can be polycentric, many-layered, and local. And institutions should be judged by how they perform, not by which ideology they flatter.

**Where we go further.** Ostrom worked inductively, studying cases of common-pool resources and extracting design principles from the cases that succeeded. We attempt to generalize the underlying logic: the same coupling structure that makes a fishery sustainable also helps explain why fraud, slavery, rent-seeking, and capture are pathologies. Her principles are findings about one class of problems. Our claim is that they are instances of a broader criterion applicable to every scale of social organization, from the household to the state. That claim is a conjecture, since her evidence comes from small groups with repeated interaction.

**Where Ostrom disciplines us.** She reminds us that alignment is not designed from a desk. The rules that worked were built, tested, and revised by the people who lived with them, using local knowledge that no central planner possessed. Any alignment theorist who believes he can compute the correct incentive structure for a society from first principles has misunderstood the lesson. Our theory supplies a question, and the answer must come from experiment, feedback, and the participation of those affected.

## XII. Our Original Contribution

If there is something new in this manifesto, it is this: **we separate three questions that most political theories fuse.**

1. *What do people want?* The domain of culture, religion, personal choice, markets, and democratic expression.
2. *How should those wants be aggregated?* The domain of voting, markets, deliberation, and hierarchy.
3. *How should institutions shape incentives?* The domain of alignment.

Most political philosophies try to answer all three at once, which is why they quarrel so bitterly: a disagreement about values becomes a disagreement about the legitimacy of the whole structure. We refuse to answer the first, offer only partial answers to the second, and specialize in the third.

This specialization has three consequences.

First, **it gives us a standard that does not depend on prior agreement about values.** People who hold radically different conceptions of the good can still agree that a system in which success requires harming others is worse than one in which it requires helping them. It depends on one thin premise, stated openly in Section III and defended there on functional grounds, and not on agreement about the good life.

Second, **it relocates the moral weight from persons to structures.** We ask less often "who is to blame?" and more often "what arrangement made this behavior profitable?" That does not abolish responsibility, but it directs reform toward the thing that can actually be changed.

Third, **it reconstructs, rather than postulates, a surprising range of commitments.** Anti-slavery, due process, free expression, property, and open courts can be rebuilt from a premise about agents and gradients, not taken from a catalog of sacred axioms. The reconstruction is a stress test, not a proof, and the lineage of these ideas is long. What we add is a single coupling criterion applied across scales. A foundation whose assumptions are few, and stated, has fewer places to hide.

## XIII. Objections We Welcome

A manifesto that cannot withstand its critics is not worth signing. We state the two strongest lines of attack in their best form and answer each.

### The Marxist objection

*"You have described the surface and missed the depth. Incentives are not free-floating; they are produced by the material relations of production. To speak of 'aligning' the interests of capital and labor within the existing order is to ask the wolf and the lamb to cooperate. Where classes exist, misalignment is not a bug to be engineered away but the structure itself, and your technocratic vocabulary serves to pacify the exploited."*

We take this seriously, because it contains a genuine truth: structural divergence of interests is real, and it can be hidden behind the language of harmony. Our criterion does not deny it. It names it. An employment relationship in which workers have no exit, no information, and no voice is, by our own test, misaligned, and we say so without needing a theory of history.

But we part ways on three points. First, we do not accept that the divergence is total or inevitable. Wage relations vary enormously, from conditions near bondage to arrangements in which the employee's prosperity and the firm's are tightly joined, and the variation is itself evidence that institutions matter. Second, we do not accept that the remedy must be the abolition of an entire class and the concentration of power in those who claim to speak for it, because that concentration is exactly the sort of unchecked power that produces its own incentive divergence, as the twentieth century demonstrated. Third, we do not need a teleology. The test applies to the owners of capital and to the commissars alike.

If the Marxist response is that our test is too weak to discipline the powerful, we invite them to apply it without mercy. We believe it will condemn far more than its critics expect.

### The liberal objection

*"You have dissolved the individual into a system. Rights are not engineering conveniences; they are claims that persons hold against any arrangement, however efficient. If your justification for freedom of speech is that it improves information flow, then you have conceded that where suppressing speech improves the system, suppression is permitted. You have swapped an inviolable principle for a calculation."*

This is the stronger objection, and it deserves a direct answer. We concede that grounding rights in alignment makes them contingent on an argument, rather than immune from argument. We believe this is a virtue in explanation and a risk in practice.

The virtue is that we can say *why* the rights matter and so defend them against those who deny their sanctity. The risk is real: a clever planner may claim that this particular suppression, in this particular crisis, improves alignment.

Our response has three parts. First, the alignment argument is stricter than it looks. Suppression of information is the paradigm case of an arrangement that cuts a society off from its own error signals, and the planner who proposes it is always the party whose interests are served by the silence. Under our framework, that is a flagged conflict of interest, not a justification. Second, we hold that the argument must be conducted at the level of institutional *rules*, not case-by-case calculation. A rule that permits exceptions whenever the powerful judge them useful provides no protection at all, and this is itself a result of the alignment analysis. Third, we welcome the liberal's insistence that individuals not be reduced to inputs. Our whole starting point is that each agent has interests that count, and a theory that begins there cannot coherently treat persons as raw material. Section IX shows where we accept that this premise carries a substantive view of persons as continuing judges of their own lives.

The liberal and the aligner will usually arrive at the same institutions. When they do not, that is a disagreement worth having in public.

### A note on what neither objection can reach

Both critics assume that our near-silence on values is an evasion. It is a discipline. We believe the quarrels over the good life are the proper business of free people in free societies, and the task of institutions is to let that argument continue without any party capturing the machinery to end it.

## XIV. Limits and Honesty

We close our argument by stating our own weaknesses, because a theory that cannot name them is likely a creed.

- **Alignment is not measurable in any simple way.** We offer a question, not an index. Reasonable people will disagree about whether a given arrangement passes.
- **Some conflicts are not misalignments but genuine scarcities.** Not every disagreement can be dissolved by better design. Sometimes there is one seat and two claimants, and the weighting of competing interests is a choice that no technical rule makes neutral.
- **Alignment can be purchased at the price of conformity.** A society in which everyone's incentives point the same way because dissent has been made unthinkable has not been aligned. It has been silenced. This is why Article 3 and Article 6 are not optional.
- **Our core is thin, not neutral.** The premise that persons' interests count, and count roughly equally, is a normative commitment. So is the filter we apply to adaptive preferences, and so is the claim about what counts as real exit. Some traditions reject autonomy as a standard, and for them we offer overlap at best. Whether "widely shared" amounts to more than "shared by those who were asked" we do not know.
- **Every baseline is a choice.** To say an arrangement improves things is to say relative to what. No baseline is free of a view about what people are owed, and we have tried to state ours rather than conceal them.
- **The comparative standard is safer, not safe.** Ranking institutions by how much cruelty, domination, and deprivation they produce needs less agreement than a theory of flourishing, but adaptive preference distorts reports of suffering too, and bads conflict.
- **Endorsement and foreclosure can look identical.** Where exit is low, we cannot always tell whether members are content or confined. Our reliance on rules and on the state's involvement, instead of outcomes, reduces this problem without removing it.
- **The theory is mostly silent on ends.** A perfectly coherent society could pursue ends we find appalling. We do not claim our test is sufficient for a good society, only that it is necessary for a functioning one.

---

## Closing

We do not ask the reader to abandon their faith, their party, or their vision of the good. We ask something smaller and, we think, harder.

Whatever you want, ask whether the institutions you propose to build make the success of each person depend on the success of others. Ask who profits when things go wrong, and who is told to be silent. Ask what a rational agent in the system would do and where that behavior leads. Ask whether the whip you are willing to use on others will stay where you put it. And if the answer is that the arrangement works only by setting people against one another and holding them down, then build something else.

Agents optimize. Institutions shape the landscape on which they do it. Some landscapes produce cooperation, and some produce predation.

**Build the first kind.**
