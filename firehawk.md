# The Fire Hawk Problem: Beneficiaries, Causes, and the Management of a Burning Forest

There is a familiar ritual in public life. A group of people refuses a vaccine, or doubts an election, or rejects an official finding, and another group responds with mockery. Screenshots circulate. Someone compiles the dumbest quotes. The laughter feels like a defense of truth, a small act of civic hygiene. Then everyone is surprised when the doubters do not come back, and dig in instead.

The usual explanation for this pattern is polarization, understood as a collective-action failure. Everyone would be better off if trust were higher, arguments were calmer, and institutions were believed when they deserved to be. But no individual can produce that outcome alone, and each person pays a cost for unilaterally lowering their guard. So everyone defects a little, and the commons of shared credibility erodes. On this view, polarization is a tragedy: a symmetric prisoner's dilemma, bad for nearly everyone, sustained by nobody's intention.

That account is true as far as it goes. It is also incomplete in a way that matters. It assumes the payoff matrix is roughly symmetric, that the dysfunction hurts everyone. It does not. The game is variable-sum, but its gains and losses are distributed unevenly. Doubters, defenders of consensus, and the institutions themselves all lose as the commons degrades. Some people, however, profit from the chaos, and profit more as it deepens. Once you see who they are and how they profit, the problem changes shape. Not into a conspiracy, which would be the easy and wrong conclusion, but into something stranger and harder to fix: an ecology.

## Who Wins When Trust Collapses

Consider three archetypes. None of them is a single person. Each is a strategy, a role that various actors can occupy.

**The Corrupt** wins when generalized distrust makes specific, provable misconduct harder to distinguish from the ambient roar of accusations. In a high-trust environment, a documented case of bribery stands out. It is an anomaly, and anomalies get investigated. In a low-trust environment, where everyone already assumes everyone is on the take, the same documented case is just one more noise among a thousand. If everything is supposedly corrupt, actual corruption gets camouflage. The Corrupt's calculation is simple: illicit gain, minus the probability of being caught times the penalty. As ambient distrust rises, specific evidence drowns in general accusation, and the probability of detection falls toward zero. The real offense disappears into the background of imagined ones.

**The Monopolist** wins when trust collapses as a shared commons. When people can no longer rely on common reference points, verification becomes expensive. Someone has to tell you what is real, who is qualified, which product is safe, which source is honest. Whoever controls a dominant channel of information, credentialing, distribution, or access can then charge rent on the replacement. That rent grows with the level of distrust and shrinks with the availability of independent ways to check. The destruction of a public good creates a market for a private substitute, and markets for scarce substitutes favor concentration.

**The Demagogue** wins by converting uncertainty into identity. The demagogue does not need to restore credibility in institutions, and has no interest in doing so. What the demagogue needs is for followers to *transfer* credibility from institutions to the person. "You can't trust them" is only the first half of the pitch. The second half is "but you can trust me." Distrust is the raw material; allegiance is the product.

These three roles are not merely parallel. They are complementary, and they chain together:

> Corruption destroys credibility → monopoly concentrates the alternatives → demagoguery monetizes the resulting distrust.

The chain usually begins somewhere more mundane than any of the three. Institutions make ordinary mistakes, and then, to avoid short-term embarrassment, defend or conceal them. When the concealed mistakes surface, they spark reasonable doubt. That doubt is the first stage of the opening: the Corrupt discovers that misconduct now blends into general suspicion. As concealed misconduct proliferates, shared trust collapses further, verification costs spike, and the Monopolist finds it profitable to enclose the channels people turn to. Finally, sincere skeptics question the enclosed, error-prone institutions; they are met with ridicule; and the Demagogue, arriving last, harvests the combination.

Each stage prepares the ground for the next. Real misconduct makes skepticism reasonable. Reasonable skepticism fragments shared sources of truth and pushes people toward whoever offers a reliable-seeming channel. And a population that has learned to distrust institutions while craving something solid is exactly the audience a demagogue needs. None of this requires the actors at each stage to know one another. Each is responding independently to the condition the previous stage left behind.

## How the Mockers Feed the Fire

This is where the mockery returns. The people laughing at vaccine skeptics are usually not corrupt, not monopolists, and not demagogues. Many of them are sincerely trying to defend truth. But sincerity does not determine effect.

Every "look how stupid those people are" supplies evidence for a proposition far more powerful than any argument about vaccine efficacy: *They despise you; I am on your side.* The demagogue does not need to win the debate about the science. The demagogue only needs to win the debate about loyalty, and the mockers are handing over the evidence for free. Each viral sneer is a recruitment poster printed at someone else's expense.

Why do they keep printing? Because the incentives facing an individual mocker are lopsided. A mocker wants two things: immediate standing within their own group, and, in the long run, a world where truth is broadly believed. Ridicule delivers the first right away and cheaply. Respectful correction costs far more effort and delivers no applause. Meanwhile, the long-run payoff, a healthier commons, is the same either way from the perspective of any single person, because one sneer has a negligible effect on society-wide distrust. So mocking dominates for each individual in the short run, even though when nearly everyone mocks, distrust rises toward its ceiling and destroys the very truth payoff the mockers cared about. Call it the tragedy of the mockers: the original collective-action story, alive and well, but now feeding a predator.

The Demagogue's take is best understood as a product rather than a sum. It depends on ambient distrust, on the share of mockers supplying free evidence of elite contempt, and on the absence of legitimate channels for dissent, all multiplied together. That multiplicative structure matters later, because it means removing any one factor starves the whole operation.

Look at the same scene from the skeptic's side. A sincere skeptic wants accurate beliefs and wants to belong without being humiliated. Staying skeptical *within* the system pays off in truth and dignity, minus whatever humiliation the mockers dish out. Joining a counter-tribe pays off in belonging and in protection from that humiliation, a protection the Demagogue is happy to provide. The skeptic is also working with poor information. From the outside, an honest institutional mistake, a deliberate cover-up, and outright malfeasance can look nearly identical. When the institutions conceal errors and treat questions as acts of war, and the mockers answer with ridicule, the payoff for staying inside collapses. The skeptic is not so much seduced into the counter-tribe as pushed.

So the well-meaning defender of consensus and the cynical exploiter of distrust end up in an odd partnership. Neither intends it. Both contribute to it. The asymmetry here is between intent and effect: the mockers mean to defend truth, and what they actually produce functions as a subsidy to the person most eager to destroy it.

## Beneficiary Is Not Cause

At this point the conspiratorial reading becomes tempting. If certain actors benefit so consistently from the breakdown of trust, surely they arranged it? Surely the corrupt, the monopolists, and the demagogues are coordinating?

Sometimes, perhaps. But that is the exception, and treating it as the rule actively obscures what is going on. The key distinction is simple:

- A beneficiary is not necessarily a cause.
- A beneficiary is not necessarily an architect.
- But a beneficiary *does* acquire an incentive to preserve and amplify the condition it benefits from.

In game-theoretic terms, this is a non-cooperative evolutionary game. Nobody signs a binding agreement or meets in a smoke-filled room. Strategies are simply selected over time according to how well they do in the current environment, and they spread or wither accordingly.

The better archetype here is the fire hawk. In northern Australia, several raptor species, including black kites, whistling kites, and brown falcons, have long been reported by Aboriginal observers, and more recently documented by researchers, to pick up burning sticks and drop them in unburned grass. The fire flushes out insects, lizards, and small mammals, which the birds then catch.

The fire hawk did not invent fire. Lightning did, or people did, or a hundred mundane accidents did. But once fire exists, it alters the opportunity landscape, and behavior that exploits the disturbance gets rewarded. A bird that hunts at the fire line eats well. A bird that learns to extend the fire line eats better.

This is the right model for social dysfunction. A credibility crisis can emerge from entirely ordinary mechanisms: institutional mistakes, conflicting incentives, information overload, asymmetric accountability, repeated overclaiming by experts, and the plain tribal wiring of human cognition. No mastermind is required. Nobody had to sit in a room and plan the erosion of trust.

Then the ecology changes:

- The Corrupt discovers that noise provides concealment.
- The Monopolist discovers that disorder weakens competitors and raises the value of controlled channels.
- The Demagogue discovers that distrust can be converted into allegiance.

Eventually, some of them may begin fanning the flames deliberately. But that is a second-order development, a learned behavior that arises *because* the fire was already there and already paying. Treating these actors as the original cause gets the sequence backward and hides the more interesting phenomenon: spontaneously generated dysfunction creates ecological niches for actors adapted to dysfunction.

## The Feedback Loop

Once fire-adapted actors are established, the system acquires a feedback loop:

> Accidental fire → opportunistic exploitation → advantage to fire-adapted actors → incentive to prolong and amplify the fire → more fire.

A one-shot analysis misses this entirely. The game is played over and over, and between rounds it carries forward a state: the level of ambient credibility. Each round of opportunistic exploitation lowers that level a little, and a lower level raises the payoff for fire-fanning in the next round. Concealed misconduct, enclosed verification, and loyalty-harvesting do not just profit from low trust; they actively degrade it further, dropping burning sticks into unburned grass and locking the low-trust state in place.

This is considerably nastier than a conspiracy theory, precisely because it requires no conspiracy. A conspiracy can be exposed. Its members can be named, prosecuted, voted out, or discredited. An ecology cannot be arrested. It is evolutionary selection operating on institutions and strategies. Remove one fire hawk and another occupies the niche, because the niche is what rewards the behavior, not any particular bird.

A rough illustration makes the point concrete. Imagine an unreformed forest, one where institutions defend and conceal their mistakes, running at high combustibility. Assign illustrative payoffs to each role's two options. The Corrupt who conceals misconduct in the noise might earn 8, against 2 for the honest baseline. The Monopolist who entrenches gatekeeping earns 9, against 3 for competing in the open. The Demagogue who fans distrust earns 10, against 1 for abstaining, because there is no audience for calm. The mocker earns 4 from ridicule, mostly status, against 0 for patient correction. And the skeptic earns 3 in the counter-tribe against −3 for staying inside the system, mocked and with no room to dissent. The numbers are invented; the ordering is the point. In every population, the fire strategy beats the commons strategy when everyone else is playing fire. No lone deviator can invade. That is what it means for the fire-hawk regime to be an evolutionarily stable strategy, and because the hawks' payoffs keep rising as distrust rises, the system shows increasing returns to fire-fanning. This is why taking out an individual demagogue or corrupt official accomplishes so little: selection fills the vacancy with a new bird.

It also explains why the problem persists despite widespread agreement that it is bad. The collective-action framing assumed everyone was losing. The ecological framing shows that a minority is winning, and winning more the worse things get. Those actors face increasing returns as social credibility decreases. The system is not merely failing to correct itself; parts of it are actively selected for keeping it uncorrected.

So the central question is no longer *who started the fire?* It is: **what makes being a fire hawk an evolutionarily stable strategy here?**

## Ignore the Woodland Critter Drama

If the goal is better forest management, much of what dominates public attention turns out to be beside the point. Most political discourse is woodland-critter drama: which squirrel is a hypocrite, which badger said something outrageous, which owl is secretly in league with the foxes.

It helps to sort the questions by how much they matter:

- **"Which critter is a jerk?"** Mostly noise. There will always be jerks.
- **"Which critter started this particular fire?"** Occasionally important, for accountability in specific cases.
- **"Which organisms thrive specifically because the forest burns?"** Useful, but it tells you about feedback loops, not necessarily about causes.
- **"Why does this forest repeatedly become combustible?"** This is the management question.
- **"Which interventions reduce combustibility without requiring every critter to become wise and reasonable?"** This is the engineering question.

The last question is the crucial one. A robust social epistemic system cannot depend on humans ceasing to be tribal, vain, frightened, opportunistic, or occasionally spectacularly stupid. Those are not bugs to be patched out of the population. They are environmental constants, as fixed for the purpose of design as rainfall and wind. Any plan that begins with "if only people would be more reasonable" has already failed. Mockers will not spontaneously become saints, and skeptics will not become omniscient.

Not all the players in this game are critters, though. Credibility institutions occupy a different position. The other populations respond to the local incentive topology; the institutions help *set* it. They are architectural players, the ones whose choices determine how dry the underbrush is. They also face their own version of the mocker's temptation. Concealing an error avoids the immediate embarrassment of admitting it, and that saving is paid today, in full. The cost, accumulated fuel that eventually produces a far larger collapse, arrives later and is easy to discount, especially for the official who will have moved on by the time it ignites.

Real forest managers do not lecture the wildlife. They manage fuel loads, build firebreaks, limit how ignitions propagate, plan for recovery, and shape which species the landscape favors. The social equivalent is managing the incentive topology: the structure of rewards that determines which strategies flourish.

## Managing the Forest, Not the Squirrels

Return to vaccines. The target is not to make vaccine skeptics smarter, and it is not to make vaccine advocates nicer, though neither would hurt. Both of those are squirrel-optimization projects, and both rely on changing human nature at scale. The target is to design credibility institutions with properties like these:

- **Mistakes are exposed and corrected cheaply.** Institutions that cannot admit error accumulate hidden fuel. Every concealed or defended mistake becomes kindling for the next accusation, and when it finally ignites, it takes legitimate claims down with it. Cheap, routine correction keeps the fuel load low, discharging honest mistakes before they can detonate. In the illustrative model, this alone brings the forest's combustibility down from high to low.

- **Conflicts of interest are legible.** Distrust thrives on suspicion of hidden motives. When funding, affiliations, and incentives are visible by default, the Corrupt loses the camouflage of ambient suspicion, because real conflicts can be pointed to and imagined ones can be checked and dismissed. Even if someone screams that everyone is corrupt, the records are there to audit. Detection stays likely no matter how loud the noise gets, and concealment turns from the best option into a losing one: in the illustration, from 8 down to −4, well below the honest 2.

- **Claims can be independently checked.** If verification is possible without going through a single gatekeeper, the Monopolist's rent disappears. Distributed, reproducible verification is a firebreak against concentration. Gatekeeping that once paid 9 now pays about 1, less than the 3 available from competing in the open.

- **Dissent does not require joining a counter-tribe.** If the only way to voice doubt is to adopt an entire hostile identity, every doubter becomes a recruit for the Demagogue. Institutions that make room for skepticism inside the system, that treat questions as questions rather than as declarations of war, deny the Demagogue the conversion of uncertainty into allegiance. For the skeptic, staying inside rises from −3 to something like 5, now better than the counter-tribe's 3. And here the multiplicative structure of the Demagogue's payoff pays off for the reformer. Even if the mockers keep mocking, because vanity really is an environmental constant, the Demagogue can no longer convert their sneers into followers, since the skeptics already have a dignified place to go. Fanning distrust collapses from 10 to roughly nothing, below the modest return from abstaining. The mockers are not reformed; they are simply neutralized. Their ridicule no longer sets anything alight.

- **Fire-hawk strategies face diminishing rather than increasing returns.** This is the summary condition. The test of any reform is whether it makes exploiting dysfunction less profitable the more it is attempted. A system where each additional act of fanning the flames yields less reward is one in which fire hawks become a marginal curiosity instead of a dominant species.

None of these requires identifying villains. None requires winning an argument with any particular person. They work on the conditions, not on the characters. Each one targets a specific link in the chain: legibility breaks the step from corruption to collapsed trust, open verification breaks the step from collapsed trust to monopoly, and internal dissent breaks the step from monopoly and mockery to demagoguery.

It is worth being honest about one consequence. Moving from a burning forest to a managed one is not a change that leaves everyone better off. The doubters, the defenders of consensus, and the institutions gain enormously. The fire hawks lose the rents that made the fire worth tending. That is why appeals to "lower the temperature" and "find common ground" so reliably fail: they are addressed to everyone as though everyone were trapped, when some of the players are not trapped at all. They are home.

## Conclusion

The instinct to find the villain is strong, and the existence of real beneficiaries makes it stronger. The corrupt, the monopolist, and the demagogue are real, and they really do profit from the collapse of shared trust. But noticing who profits from a fire is not the same as discovering who lit it, and confusing the two leads to a politics of endless blame that, ironically, adds more fuel.

The more accurate picture is ecological. Ordinary failures produce combustible conditions. Combustible conditions create niches. Niches select for strategies that exploit them, and those strategies learn to keep the forest burning. The mockers, the skeptics, the officials, and the opportunists are all critters in that forest, behaving more or less as critters do.

Arguing about which of them is worst will not change the fire season. Changing the forest will.

Stop optimizing the squirrels. Optimize the forest.
