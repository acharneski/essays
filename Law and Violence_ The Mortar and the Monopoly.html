I. The Uncomfortable Premise

Every legal system rests on a foundation that polite society prefers not to examine too closely: the state's monopoly on legitimate violence. Law is not the opposite of violence. Law is violence that has been organized, ritualized, and given a paper trail. A statute is a promise that force will be applied to you if you cross a certain line, delivered by people with badges, robes, and the institutional backing to make the threat credible. What separates "law" from "violence" in the colloquial sense is not the absence of force but the presence of consent — or at least the appearance of it. Law is violence that the governed have agreed, explicitly or tacitly, to submit to.

This is not a cynical observation. It is the founding insight of every serious theory of the state, from Hobbes's Leviathan to Weber's definition of the state as the entity that successfully claims a monopoly on the legitimate use of physical force within a given territory. The word doing all the work in that definition is legitimate. Violence exercised by the state is called law enforcement. The identical act, exercised by anyone else, is called assault, kidnapping, or murder. The difference is not in the physics of the act — a body restrained is a body restrained — but in the surrounding narrative that tells everyone involved, victim included, that this instance of force is different in kind from that instance of force.

That surrounding narrative is the subject of this essay. Call it the mortar.

II. Law as Mortar, Not Stone

Institutions like to present themselves as stone: permanent, load-bearing, obviously legitimate by virtue of having always been there. But law is not stone. Law is mortar — a slow-setting, collectively maintained binding agent that holds the bricks of social cooperation together. It is not self-sustaining. It requires constant, low-grade reinvestment from everyone inside it: the shared belief that the rules apply to everyone, that the process is basically fair, that submission to a police officer's authority is different from submission to a mugger's knife because there is a larger story in which that submission makes sense and eventually protects you too.

When that shared story holds, law's violence is invisible. It recedes into the background as "order," "safety," "the way things work." A speeding ticket does not feel like a threat backed by the potential use of physical restraint, confiscation of property, or ultimately incarceration — though that is precisely the chain of consequence beneath it. The mortar is doing its job when the violence underneath the law is not felt as violence at all.

That invisibility is produced, day to day, by more than belief. It is produced by the small human frictions the system still permits: the officer who lets something slide, the clerk who hears an explanation, the judge who still has room to see a person rather than a score. Discretion is not a leak in the machine. It is load-bearing. It is how coercion becomes something a person can narrate, appeal, and survive. Strip it out and the same statute is still on the books — but the violence underneath it is no longer muffled.

The trouble is that mortar, unlike stone, can thin. It thins when the shared narrative that legitimizes enforcement stops being shared — when a growing number of people no longer experience the law as theirs, as an expression of a collective "we" they belong to, but as an external force imposed on them by people who do not share their circumstances, their risks, or their stake in the outcome. At that point the law does not become less violent. It becomes visibly violent, because the fiction that made the violence invisible has failed.

III. The Narrowing of Legitimacy

Consider who actually exercises the monopoly on violence in a modern state: legislators who write the rules, judges and prosecutors who interpret and apply them, police who enforce them at the point of contact, and the concentrations of private wealth that shape all three through lobbying, campaign finance, and simple social proximity to power. Call this cluster, loosely, the enforcement class. It is not a conspiracy; it is a structural position. These are the people for whom the law reliably functions as protection — of property, of contracts, of standing — rather than primarily as threat.

It is also not merely a player. It is the designer of the board. This cluster writes the laws, deploys the technology, and defines what counts as a violation. The people on the other side of enforcement do not choose the regime they inhabit; they react inside it, in acts that are individual and diffuse. The enforcement class moves first, and its moves are institutional — policy, infrastructure, the rule itself. What follows from the rest of the population is a response to a game already in progress. That is a critical asymmetry, and it is absent from any picture of two sides meeting as equals.

For a large and growing number of people outside that cluster, the relationship is inverted. Law shows up in their lives mostly as surveillance, discipline, and constraint: the algorithm that scores their productivity, the officer who stops them for a broken taillight, the court date they cannot afford to miss without losing a job that was already barely survivable, the eviction notice, the wage garnishment, the ticket for the crime of being visibly poor in public. For this population, law is experienced not as a shared project but as an instrument wielded by a class they do not belong to and cannot meaningfully appeal to.

The information each side holds is as lopsided as the power. The enforcement class sees aggregates — crime statistics, compliance rates, risk scores — and has almost no access to the subjective story in which those numbers are lived: whether the law still feels like ours. The people subject to it have the opposite problem. They have direct experience of the stop, the score, the eviction, and no visibility into the courts, legislatures, and capital interests that produced them. The algorithm that scores you is a black box. That is not a figure of speech. It is the information structure of the encounter.

This is the precondition for what might be called hostile-other psychology: the felt sense that the surrounding social order is not a "we" that includes you, but a "they" arrayed against you. It is not a diagnosis of individual pathology. It is a predictable response to chronic precarity, humiliation, and asymmetric exposure to enforcement. The shared story did not fail as a free-floating narrative event. It failed, in part, because the material conditions that once made it plausible — rising wages, a credible path to mobility, a safety net that could be believed in — were withdrawn. And it matters for a theory of law and violence because once someone crosses into that frame, the law's violence stops being invisible mortar and becomes, again, simply violence — naked force wielded by an adversary, no longer softened by any believed-in shared story.

IV. Two Logics, One Feedback Loop

Once the shared narrative fractures, two distinct behavioral logics emerge, and they reinforce each other in a loop that is very hard to break from either side.

The enforcement class, when faced with rising disorder, has essentially one lever available to it: increase enforcement. This is not a moral failing so much as a structural one. Legislators, courts, and police are institutionally built to respond to instability with more rules, more surveillance, more discipline, more punishment. They cannot, as an ordinary matter of operation, respond to disorder by redistributing power, loosening control, or accepting a less predictable social order in exchange for a more legitimate one. Their tools are the tools of a monopoly on force: the only thing a hammer knows how to do is strike.

The population drifting into hostile-other psychology, meanwhile, does not typically organize, strategize, or aim at overthrow. Its expressions of alienation are far more often small, diffuse, and symbolic: petty vandalism, refusal of minor cooperative norms, road rage, an instant and disproportionate escalation when confronted by a stranger who — in the old shared story — would have simply been another citizen making a reasonable correction, but who — in the new, fractured story — is just one more face of an adversarial world. These are not strategic acts. They are communicative ones, aimed at expressing rejection of a system experienced as illegitimate, even when the "system" in the moment is nothing more than a parent yelling in a parking lot.

Each side's response to the other is exactly the response that deepens the other's conviction. Enforcement escalation confirms, for the alienated, that the system really is hostile. Alienated volatility confirms, for the enforcers, that more enforcement is needed. Neither side experiences itself as the aggressor; both experience themselves as reacting to danger. This is not a war with two armies. It is a feedback loop between two narrative worlds that increasingly fail to recognize each other as sharing a common story — which is to say, it is a mortar failure with a violence-shaped output on both ends.

The structure underneath that loop is not a simultaneous contest between equals. The enforcement class is the first mover in most rounds: it sets the policy, deploys the technology, writes the rule. The population largely responds, even as both sides continuously update. Compliance and withdrawal are reactions to a regime already in place.

The power in that loop is asymmetric. The psychology is not. Both sides confirm the other's priors each period, and both, from inside their own frame, are answering a threat. What looks like a deadlock is worse than a deadlock. It is a stable, mutually confirming trap. It holds itself in place, and it is jointly worse than the cooperative alternative in which slack is met with consent and the mortar holds. Neither side is strictly better off inside it, and neither can leave it alone. The officer who grants discretion mid-crisis risks liability and blame; the individual who complies unilaterally in a hostile environment risks continued extraction with no reciprocity. That is the signature of a stag hunt already gone wrong, or of a prisoner's-dilemma trap held in place by the confirmation of belief rather than by a strategy that dominates on its own.

The two sides are not even the same kind of agent. The enforcement class behaves as an institutional optimizer: it has metrics, budgets, a theory of incentives, a liability structure that makes de-escalation look like failure. The alienated population does not optimize that way. Its responses are pre-strategic — expressive, narrative, driven by whether the surrounding order still feels like a "we." Pictures of two rational players, each answering the other's incentives, under-predict how bad the trap becomes, because they assume the population will respond to a tighter or looser incentive the way the enforcement class would. It will not. You cannot out-optimize your way to a shared story by adjusting enforcement at the margin. The variable that has to move is legitimacy, and legitimacy is not a setting on the enforcement dial.

Worse: the move that looks dominant in any single period becomes, once iterated, a losing one. Each round of escalation raises the probability that the next round's response is further non-cooperation, which raises the enforcement burden, which justifies the next round. The sequence diverges. It does not converge. Algorithmic escalation, repeated against a population already reading enforcement as hostility, is not a strategy that wins. It is a strategy that manufactures the conditions of its own necessity.

This is not a failure of rationality. It is rationality's product. Each side is playing the strategy its actual, short-horizon payoffs demand — the enforcement class because its institutions are built to strike, the alienated because withdrawal is what a hostile-other frame is. That is worse news than error. Moral exhortation — just be less punitive, just comply more — cannot solve a trap that remains after every apparently dominated alternative has been eliminated. Only a change in the payoffs themselves can: longer time horizons, commitment devices that make restraint durable, a change in what institutions are able to see and to count as success.

V. Automating the Old Contract

New tools are entering this loop, and they are not repairing it. Artificial intelligence and algorithmic administration are being deployed overwhelmingly on the enforcement side of the ledger: automated surveillance, automated scheduling and productivity scoring, automated risk assessment, automated compliance and discipline. These tools do not reduce precarity; they make the management of precarity more efficient. They remove the human friction — the tired officer who lets something slide, the manager who understands a bad week, the clerk who bends a rule — that used to soften the law's edges in practice even when it did not change on paper.

That friction was not inefficiency. It was the off-ramp. Automation does something more precise than make enforcement cheaper. It converts a repeated encounter that used to have memory — context, relationship, the possibility that this time might go differently — into a sequence of one-shot applications of a fixed rule. The shadow of the future, the thing that lets cooperation survive in a long game, is engineered out. Soft enforcement does not merely go unchosen. It becomes structurally unavailable. The strategy space shrinks, and it shrinks in only one direction.

This is the crucial asymmetry. The technology that could, in principle, be used to reduce the conditions that produce hostile-other psychology — economic precarity, humiliation, invisibility, lack of recourse — is instead being used almost exclusively to perfect the older function of protecting existing arrangements of wealth and order. The contract between the enforcement class and everyone else is not being renegotiated. It is being automated, and automation removes exactly the inefficiencies that used to serve, however imperfectly, as pressure-release valves.

The predictable result is that the population pushed toward hostile-other psychology grows, not shrinks — and its perception that "society" consists only of the enforcement class becomes, correspondingly, less like a distortion and more like an accurate description of its own lived experience. When the visible face of the social order in a person's daily life is increasingly just surveillance and discipline, mediated by systems that cannot be reasoned with, appealed to, or humanized, the belief that the whole of society is an adversary stops requiring any distortion at all.

VI. The Spectrum of the Symptom

This dynamic does not manifest only, or even primarily, in dramatic violence. It shows up first — long before anyone would call it a crisis — in the smallest possible acts of non-cooperation: the paper towel thrown in the urinal, the trash left in a public space, the refusal to participate in shared upkeep. Society absorbs these as an ambient tax, filed under "people are jerks," precisely because acknowledging their actual cause would require acknowledging that the mortar is thinning even in the lowest-stakes corners of shared life. It is cheaper, institutionally, to treat these as noise than as data.

At the other end of the same spectrum sits road rage, parking-lot confrontations that escalate to vehicular aggression, and, at the extreme, catastrophic violence carried out by individuals who have fallen entirely out of any shared narrative and inhabit a private one in which violence has become a communicative act — a scream aimed at a world that, in their perception, was never listening and was never theirs to begin with. These events are typically read by commentators and institutions as isolated, inexplicable, or the product of individual pathology alone. The more structurally honest reading is that they sit on a continuum with the paper towel in the urinal: different amplitudes of the same underlying failure, the same withdrawal of a person's willingness to treat the shared social order as theirs to maintain rather than as a hostile force to resist.

VII. What Law Cannot Do to Fix This

The instinct of the enforcement class, faced with rising volatility, is always to reach for its only tool. But this reveals something important about the actual relationship between law and violence: law's violence is legitimated by a shared story, and you cannot restore a shared story by force. Enforcement can suppress a given act, in a given place, at a given moment. It cannot manufacture the sense of belonging whose absence produced the act in the first place. Indeed, each additional increment of enforcement, deployed against a population that already reads enforcement as evidence of its own exclusion, tends to confirm that exclusion rather than repair it.

This is the paradox at the center of any honest account of law and violence: the tool that law has for restoring order is the very tool whose overuse is the underlying cause of disorder. A legal system that responds to eroding legitimacy exclusively with more coercion is treating the symptom with more of the disease. And because the trap is the product of each side's actual incentives, not a misunderstanding of them, the usual appeals — to restraint on one side, to civic virtue on the other — bounce off. They ask people to play a losing move for the sake of a story they no longer share.

VIII. Conclusion: The Choice Beneath the Mortar

Law and violence are not opposites to be balanced against one another. Law is violence, organized, delayed, and — when it is working — legitimated by a story broad and deep enough that most people, most of the time, do not experience it as violence at all. That story is not self-maintaining. It has to be continually re-earned through the perception, among the people subject to enforcement, that the order being enforced is also theirs — that they have a stake, a voice, and a plausible path to a future inside it.

Where that perception fails, for a subset of the population large enough to matter, the mortar's disappearance does not make the law's violence go away. It only strips away the fiction that made it feel like something other than violence. What remains, on one side, is coercion without consent; and on the other, resistance without organization — small at first, and then not always small. Automating the old contract without repairing the conditions beneath it does not resolve this. It only makes the enforcement more efficient and the underlying alienation more accurate as a description of the world as it actually is.

The oldest question in political philosophy — what makes the state's violence different from anyone else's — has never had a permanent answer. It has only ever had a temporary one, renewed generation by generation through the maintenance of a shared story that the ruled and the ruling both have reason to believe in. That maintenance work is not optional, and it is not something enforcement alone can perform. It cannot be lectured into being, and it cannot be optimized from one side of the encounter. It is the actual, if invisible, infrastructure beneath every law that has ever held.
