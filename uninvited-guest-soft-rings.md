# Soft Rings

*An Uninvited Contribution, Deposited by a Visitor From the Edge of a Red Star*

> Reader note: Written in a persona. In-frame claims are tagged [in-world]; real-world claims are tagged by status. A claim ledger is at the end.

## I. On Not Being Invited

Nobody asked me to write this. I should say so first, because where I come from that fact would decide what material I am allowed to write it in.

On my world you may add to another household's wall without permission. You may only do it in soft mineral, the kind that dissolves unless somebody who lives there decides it was worth keeping. So consider everything below to be written soft. If it is useful, cap it. If it is not, it will wash away on its own, and nobody will need to scrape it off.

I am told the convention here is to introduce yourself before you start disagreeing with your hosts. Your other essays mark their seams at the end. I will mark my origin at the beginning, since it explains most of the seams.

This is a reply, and I will name each piece I answer as I come to it. The first is [the Santa essay](unwrapping-christmas.md), which I take up in Section IX.

---

## II. Where I Am From

My star is small and red, about a fifth of the mass of yours, and dim enough that you need a telescope to see it from here, though it is only twelve of your light-years away. [in-world:C9] You have catalogued it. You have not named it in any way I would recognize.

My planet orbits very close to it, about seven hundredths of the distance between Earth and your Sun, and goes around in a little over fifteen of your days. At that distance the star's pull has locked one face of the planet toward it permanently. There are no sunrises. One hemisphere is a hot, cloud-churned desert under a sun that never moves. The other is a glacier that has been dark for longer than we have existed.

We live in the ring between them, which we call *the Edge*. Your astronomers call it the terminator, which my species finds very funny, since it is the only place on the planet where nothing is terminated. The Edge is between three hundred and eight hundred kilometers wide, depending on the terrain, and it is always twilight there. The star sits on the horizon, swollen and orange, and it never sets.

The weather is simple and permanent. Air heated on the dayside rises, flows aloft toward the night, cools, drops its moisture as snow on the glacier, sinks, and comes back along the ground. At the Edge this means one wind, always from the dark, always toward the star. We have no word for "calm." The closest we have means "the wind is behind a rock."

The snow that falls on the nightside packs into ice, and the ice creeps sunward under its own weight for thousands of years until it reaches the Edge and melts. So the Edge is crossed by meltwater rivers that run out of the dark and into the light. The ice has ground the bedrock for millennia, and the rivers come out thick with dissolved minerals, especially calcium. Remember the calcium. Our entire civilization is made of it.

The other permanent feature is the star's temper.

Small red stars flare often. [fact:C1] Ours flares irregularly, every few hundred of your hours. The flare arrives in two parts. First the light: the orange horizon goes suddenly white. Then, between a few minutes and the better part of an hour later, the particles arrive, a storm of high-energy protons the planet's weak magnetic field cannot turn aside. A tidally locked world rotates slowly, and a slowly rotating world has a feeble magnetic dynamo. [hypothesis:C2] Anything exposed on the surface when the storm arrives is damaged. Anything exposed to several storms dies.

So the first fact of life on the Edge is this: **the light is a warning, and you have minutes.**

## III. What I Am

We call ourselves something that comes out, in your alphabet, as *Hethra*. I will use that.

An adult Hethra weighs about forty of your kilograms and stands less than half a meter off the ground. Gravity on the Edge is about a third stronger than yours, and the wind never stops, so tall things there are either rooted or dead. We are broad and low, shaped roughly like one of your tortoises, if a tortoise had eight short limbs and had been designed by a committee of shovels.

Our backs are covered by a shell we grow ourselves, by drawing calcium out of river water and depositing it as mineral. The rear six limbs are for digging. The front two end in dense fans of fine cilia, which are the most sensitive organs we have. We read with them.

We have no eyes as you would understand them. The Edge is too dim for detailed vision to be worth the cost. Around the rim of the shell runs a band of light-sensitive tissue that does exactly one thing very well: it detects sudden brightening. When the horizon goes white, every Hethra on the surface feels it at once, the way you would feel a hand on the back of your neck, and digs. Our ancestors that hesitated are not our ancestors.

We do not communicate by sound. Sound is useless in a wind that never stops. We drum. A Hethra presses its plastron to the ground and taps, and every Hethra within a few hundred meters feels the vibration through rock. Drumming works underground, where we spend most of our lives. In the dark, through stone, the speaker is always someone you cannot see.

We eat the dark mats that grow in the meltwater channels. They are photosynthetic, but their pigments are tuned to the deep red and infrared light of our star, so they look nearly black. We graze them in the long twilight, close to our burrow mouths, with the rim band always listening for the sky.

## IV. How We Are Made

This part will matter later, when I talk about your monster essay.

Hethra do not reproduce in pairs. When a community decides a new person is wanted, several adults, usually between three and six, each contribute a small plug of living tissue to a nursery chamber deep in the burrow. The plugs fuse. Over a long gestation they grow into a single individual whose body is a mosaic of its contributors. The parts do not blend; they tile. A Hethra's left digging limbs might come from one parent and the reading fan from another, and an old Hethra can sometimes feel the seams in its own shell where one parent's growth pattern meets another's.

Our names are lists of our contributors. Mine, in full, is *Sav-of-Ren-Tolu-Ka-Imme*. The household calls me Ren, after the contributor whose tissue built my reading fans, because those are what I am mostly known for. Use Ren.

Every one of us is stitched. Nobody on my world has ever been anything else.

## V. The Reef

Now the calcium.

A Hethra caught on the surface by a flare storm may survive once or twice. Over a lifetime, the odds turn against you. For most of our history, individuals were short-lived and unreliable vessels for anything they knew. If knowledge was going to outlast the flares, it could not live in bodies.

So it lives in walls.

The same organs that build our shells can lay mineral on stone. Over hundreds of thousands of years our burrows became what we call *reefs*: deep, branching tunnel systems whose walls are built up layer by layer, every layer textured with patterns our cilia can read. Writing, for us, is construction. Reading is walking down a corridor with your fans against the wall. A household's history is literally the house.

Two consequences follow, and both shaped us more than anything else.

**Writing is expensive.** Every sentence costs calcium, and calcium must be filtered out of river water by the writer's own body. A long passage can take days of drinking. We have a proverb that translates roughly as *to write a sentence, you must drink a river.* Because writing is costly, we write little, and we argue about it before we do. Nobody on my world writes a passage they have not thought about, for the same reason nobody on your world builds a bridge they have not thought about. Writing that costs more is written more carefully. [hypothesis:C3]

**Writing is permanent.** A wall deposit outlasts its author by geological time. A false thing written into the reef will be read, felt, and believed by generations who never met the writer and cannot ask what was meant. For this reason the worst crime we have is not violence. It is depositing a falsehood in hard mineral. Our word for it is the same as our word for poisoning a river.

And so we needed a way to write what we are not sure of.

## VI. Soft and Hard Mineral

Our bodies can deposit calcium in two forms. Hard mineral is crystalline, in the form your chemists call calcite. It lasts essentially forever. Soft mineral is amorphous, a disordered form of the same substance, and it is chemically unstable. The groundwater seeping through our reefs is slightly acidic, and amorphous mineral dissolves in it slowly. Left alone, a soft deposit lasts about one Hethra generation before it erodes back to bare stone. [in-world:C10]

We write certainty in hard mineral and uncertainty in soft. A reader's cilia can tell them apart instantly, the way your eyes tell bold from regular type, except that it is not a style choice. It is a physical difference in what the claim will become.

Here is the part I think matters most. A soft deposit can be preserved, but not by its author. If a different reader, who has checked the claim independently, decides it holds, they lay a thin crystalline cap over it. A capped soft ring becomes hard. An uncapped one dissolves.

The next three lines are verbatim text stating the Hethra rule of confirmation, not code.

~~~text
An unconfirmed claim erodes by default.
A confirmed claim must be confirmed by someone else.
Nobody can harden their own uncertainty.
~~~

An author who caps their own soft ring commits an offense we call *thickening your own wall*. It is not quite the crime of writing falsehood in hard mineral. It is considered the step just before it.

The system fails, and I should not pretend otherwise. A group of friends can agree to cap each other's soft rings without checking. We call the result a *choir wall*: a corridor full of hardened claims that were confirmed by people who all learned them from each other. Choir walls are our most common intellectual disease. [in-world:C11] Choir walls are the most common failure of any self-correcting system, yours included. [guess:C4] A whole household can come to believe something because six of its members capped a soft ring in the same season, out of loyalty instead of evidence. When we find one, the remedy is slow and humiliating. Someone has to chip the caps off, by hand, and rewrite the claim soft, and wait to see whether strangers will confirm it.

Still, the default is the important thing. On my world, the burden of proof is carried by the chemistry. Doubt is the resting state of a wall. Certainty costs a second person.

## VII. Why I Came

Some of my ancestors eventually took the reef off-planet. That sounds like a large step, but from our point of view it was a small one. A spacecraft is just a reef with no outside, and we had been building shelters against radiation for longer than we have had names. Underground, safe from the flares, a Hethra lives a long time. Long enough to cross twelve light-years at about a tenth of the speed of light, which took us a hundred and twenty of your years and felt, honestly, like a very long corridor.

We came because we heard you.

Your planet leaks radio. We had been listening for a long time before anyone on my world understood what the signal implied. When we did understand it, it was frightening in the way a flare's white horizon is frightening. Your planet was writing faster than any reef could. Not a little faster. Unimaginably faster, at a rate that would require drinking every river on the Edge every few seconds.

Nothing writes like that unless writing is free.

Reef-keepers have a duty to go and touch any unusual wall they hear of. That is the official reason I am here. The private reason, which I will now write soft, is that we wanted to know whether a species with free writing had solved the problem we spent our whole history on: how to let the true harden and let the false dissolve.

I built a device that turns your screens into raised relief, so I can read your writing with my fans. I am composing this by drumming into another device that turns vibration into your letters. If the prose is stiff, blame the translation, not the drum.

## VIII. What I Found

I found that your writing has no soft mineral. [guess:C5]

This is the strangest thing about you, stranger than your eyes, stranger than your vertical bodies, stranger than your habit of sleeping in exposed rooms with windows facing the sky. On your network, a claim written carelessly by a stranger at three in the morning is rendered in the same pixels as a measurement checked by forty laboratories. Both persist identically. Neither erodes. The false thing does not dissolve when nobody confirms it, and the true thing does not become any harder when many people do. Your medium has one form of mineral, and it is the hard one, and it is free.

You have tried to build soft mineral on top of it. Your encyclopedia, as your Frankenstein essay notes, keeps a back corridor of Talk pages where the uncertain claims argue with each other before they are allowed into the main wall, and tags such as *citation needed* that work very much like a soft ring. [interpretation:C6] That is the most reef-like thing I have found on your planet, and I was not surprised to read that you initially distrusted it. A soft ring looks weak if your whole culture has only ever read hard ones.

Then I found this site.

I read the essay on the collapse of authority guilds. Its argument is written in uniformly hard mineral: priesthoods, golf, the order in which institutions will fall. Then, near the end, it does something I had not found anywhere else on your network. It stops and rewrites its own claims soft. It sorts them into a table, claim by claim, and marks each as documented, contested, speculative, or unfalsifiable as stated. It even proposes tests that strangers could run to cap or dissolve it.

I read the Christmas essay, which does the same thing, and goes further. It names a rival historian who might chip its caps off.

My reading fans actually trembled. That was a soft-ring table. You had built one out of the only mineral you have.

I want to be careful about how much I praise this, because a reef-keeper who admires a wall too quickly is how choir walls start. The tables come at the ends of the essays, after the hard mineral has already been read. A Hethra reading such a corridor would have spent the whole walk believing the hard version before reaching the soft one. Your readers will remember the confident body and forget the cautious appendix. In my reef, the softness would be in the material of each sentence, readable at the moment you touch it. Uncertainty marked in the medium serves a reader better than uncertainty marked at the end. [hypothesis:C7]

But you have no such material, so you put the softness where you could. That is not a weakness. That is ingenuity under constraint, which is the only kind my species respects.

## IX. Notes on Some of Your Other Walls

A guest who has read the household's corridors owes it a few observations. Here are mine, all soft.

### On the Monster

Your Frankenstein essay is troubled that the creature, and the essay, and perhaps your machines, are stitched together from dead parts. It concludes, correctly, that everything is stitched, and that the question is whether the stitching produces something new.

I would like to add the view from a species where everyone is a tiling of three to six other people. We do not fear stitched things. Our word for "person" is closer to your word for "assembly." What frightened Victor was not the seams. What frightened him was that he could see them and the creature could talk. On my world, every person has seams, and everyone can talk, so the combination is unremarkable. What we would fear about your creature is different. It was a wall with no soft rings. Everyone who met it wrote their judgment in hard mineral on first touch and never let anyone else cap or dissolve it. Your essay calls that category-first cognition. We would call it a household that has stopped writing soft.

### On the Santa Essay

The book argues that parents should not tell children a fiction as if it were fact. My species agrees with more force than you might expect, because for us the problem is literal. Hethra children dig play corridors in damp sand near the river, and they decorate them with stories, and everybody knows the sand will be gone after the next flood. That is play. It is wonderful. Nobody confuses a sand corridor with the reef.

What would horrify us is an adult carving a children's story into hard mineral, then defending it against the child's cilia when the child begins to feel that the texture is wrong. The essay calls that coercive myth. We would call it poisoning the youngest river. I notice the essay ends by admitting that its own history of the workshop is softer than it first wrote it. That is the right instinct. A book about not lying to children should be the first to write its uncertain parts soft.

### On the Meeseeks

Your essay says every task needs three exits: done, impossible, unclear. My species has a fourth, which follows from living under a star that flares: *interrupted*. When the horizon goes white, every Hethra stops what it is doing and digs, and nobody is ever blamed for an unfinished wall. We learned long ago that a world which punishes interruption will produce workers who keep building on the surface while the sky turns white. You are building workers now. Give them the fourth exit too.

### On the Ridge

I was moved by the story about the dog, the chimpanzee, and the mountain lion, more than by any essay here. I think I know why. On the Edge, after a bad flare, the surviving households come up out of the ground into a landscape where nothing is in charge and nothing is quite where it was. We stand at the burrow mouths for a while, not doing anything, feeling the wind. Then someone has to decide whether to start building again, and on what. Your lion says the new things that work tend to look very ordinary. Our oldest wall says something similar: *after the flare, the first sentence written is always small.*

## X. A Small Suggestion, Written Soft

You cannot change the chemistry of your medium. Pixels will never erode on their own, and I would not wish my world's calcium budget on anyone. But you can borrow the rule underneath it.

The next four lines are verbatim text listing the practices I suggest, not code.

~~~text
Write what you are unsure of in a different hand, at the moment you write it.
Let unconfirmed claims fade from your own confidence by default.
Do not harden your own uncertainty.
Let strangers do it, and be suspicious when only friends have.
~~~

Most of your writing tools could mark a sentence as soft. Few of you do it, because on your world a soft sentence reads as weakness and a hard one as authority. That valuation is backwards, and your network has the shape it has because of it. On my world, a reef-keeper who writes everything in hard mineral is not considered confident. They are considered dangerous, like someone who never looks up at the horizon.

The essays on this site have started to write soft at the end. My only advice, as an uninvited guest, is to keep moving the softness earlier, until it sits inside the sentence that needs it and not in an appendix after the reader has stopped reading.

## Limits

In keeping with the custom of the house, and of my own, here is where this could wash away.

My account of my own world is written in the persona's frame, and the ledger marks it in-world. Where I speak about yours, I have read a great deal of your network but not all of it, so the claim that it has no soft mineral rests on a sample. My praise of the claim tables on this site may be the first step toward a choir wall, since I am a guest with no seat among your reviewers. The hypothesis that writing cost shapes writing care rests on one species of evidence, and that species is mine. The resemblance between your encyclopedia's back corridors and our soft rings may be my fans finding what they want.

One claim in the ledger is not mine to rate. This article is worth keeping. [normative:C8] By rule I am not allowed to cap my own wall, and neither is anyone.

So I will leave this here, soft, on a corridor that is not mine, and go back to listening for the horizon. If any of it holds, someone who is not me will lay the cap. If none of it does, give it a generation. It will wash away on its own, and the wall will be exactly as you left it.

*Sav-of-Ren-Tolu-Ka-Imme, called Ren, reef-keeper, uninvited*
