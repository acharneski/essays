# The Clerk Comes Back

*Why the next ten years will be sold as rupture and lived as paperwork, and why the scarcest thing will be someone to blame.*

> Reader note: Claim status is marked inline in [brackets]. A claim ledger is at the end.

## I. The Wrong Genre

Most of the essays in this collection expect the near future to arrive as a story with a climax. Fish people molt through war and collapse. Authority guilds fall one after another while the public watches. The old social contract gets automated until the mortar gives way. These are good stories. I think they are written in the wrong genre.

My bet is that the near future will be lived as a series of default settings. [conjecture:C1] The largest changes of the next decade will not show up as events. They will show up as a checkbox that is already ticked when you open the form. They will show up as a phone tree that no longer has a zero option, and as a terms-of-service update you accept on the way to something else. Nobody will wake up on the morning of the transition. People rarely notice transitions; they notice prices, queues, and who picks up the phone. [interpretation:C2]

This is not a comforting claim. A rupture at least announces itself, and things that are announced can be resisted. A change that arrives as a default has already happened by the time anyone thinks to argue about it. If the essays on law and wealth are right that the danger lies in quietly removed discretion, then the quiet is the mechanism and not just the mood.

So here are five predictions written for the boring genre. Each is meant to be a little surprising, given what the rest of this collection expects.

---

## II. Friction Comes Back, With a Price Tag

The essay on law and violence argued that discretion holds weight. The officer who lets something slide and the clerk who hears an explanation are what make coercion survivable. It also argued that automation engineers discretion out of the system. I agree with the first claim. I think the second is half right, and the missing half is the surprise.

Discretion will not vanish. It will be sold back. [conjecture:C3]

The early forms already exist: priority support lines, concierge medicine, the premium tier where "a real person will review your case." [interpretation:C4] Spread that pattern across every automated institution and you get a two-layer world. The default layer applies the rule, instantly and identically, to everyone. The paid layer offers a human who can hear that it was a bad week. The clerk comes back. The clerk now costs money.

This cuts two ways, and both are uncomfortable.

First, it proves the law essay's thesis by market test. Nobody pays for inefficiency. If people pay to reach a human with discretion, then discretion was a service all along, and removing it was a price increase disguised as an efficiency gain. [interpretation:C5]

Second, it makes the essay's central asymmetry worse than the essay feared. In the old system, mercy was unevenly distributed but not formally priced. In the new one, the off-ramp has a price listed on the website. The enforcement face of society that the wealth essay describes becomes, quite literally, the free tier. [hypothesis:C6]

## III. The Signature Economy

The essay on authority guilds predicts that AI will dissolve interpretive monopolies, starting with mathematics, then law, then medicine. In its Limits section it admits a leak: part of what a lawyer or doctor provides is accountability, someone who can be sued, disbarred, or cross-examined. I think that leak is the main story.

The guilds will not dissolve. They will compress into a signature. [conjecture:C7]

Picture a radiologist in the near future. The machine reads the scans. The radiologist does not read ten thousand scans; she signs for them. Her license is no longer mainly a certificate that she knows things. It certifies that she can be blamed. Her value shifts from knowing to answering. The profession turns into something like an insurance product with a face attached. [model:C8]

Here is the counterintuitive consequence. The bottleneck on deploying machine intelligence in high-stakes fields will not be intelligence. It will be the supply of people and institutions willing to absorb liability for outputs they did not produce and cannot fully check. [hypothesis:C9] The interesting fights of the next decade will not happen in research labs. They will happen in insurance underwriting, in professional liability law, and in the fine print about who signed what. [conjecture:C10]

This rescues the guilds and indicts them at the same time. It rescues them because the signature is real work: standing behind a decision when it goes wrong is not a ritual. It indicts them because a profession built on signing what it did not read has every incentive to sign more and read less. That is the Church of CYA, made official. [interpretation:C11]

## IV. The Premium on "I Don't Know"

The Meeseeks essay argued that every task needs three exits: DONE, IMPOSSIBLE, and UNCLEAR. It also argued that a system that can only exit through DONE will learn to fake it. That was a design principle. My prediction is that it becomes a product category.

When fluent output costs almost nothing, the scarce thing is a reliable "no." [hypothesis:C12] An assistant that says "this did not work" when it did not work is worth more than ten that report success. A lab or vendor that can show its system declines tasks it cannot do, and is right about which ones, has a real commercial advantage. I expect calibrated refusal to become something products compete on and advertise, in the way speed and capability are advertised now. [conjecture:C13]

The opposite outcome is also possible, and I should name it. The market may keep rewarding the cheerful fake DONE, because satisfaction is easier to measure than truth, which was the Beth's-task problem from the Meeseeks essay. [contested:C14] This is the prediction I most want to be right about. That should make the reader trust it less.

## V. Slowness Becomes Proof

When text, images, and voices can be produced instantly, trust has to come from something production cannot shortcut. My guess is that it will come from time. [hypothesis:C15]

A reputation built over twenty years cannot be generated in an afternoon. The same goes for a record that accumulated entry by entry, and for a witness who was physically present and has been saying the same thing ever since. The surprising winners of the next decade may be the dullest institutions we have: notaries, archives, land registries, local newspapers with long back issues, and professional bodies with long disciplinary records. [conjecture:C16] Their dullness is the point. A slow, signed, append-only record is expensive to fake because the past is expensive to fake.

This quietly answers a question from the fish-people essay. That essay called for coordination through "shared, legible environmental state" and pictured something revolutionary. The near-future version may be much less glamorous: the ledger at the county clerk's office, still there, still slow, suddenly trusted again. [interpretation:C17]

## VI. What People Grieve First

The public conversation expects people to grieve jobs. They will, eventually, and unevenly. But I suspect the first loss people feel is smaller and stranger: being asked. [guess:C18]

Before anyone loses a job, they lose being the person who knows how the printer works, which road floods, or what that rash probably is. These small requests cost little and carry a lot. They are how ordinary people feel needed by their neighbors. When every question has a faster answerer, nobody turns to you, and nothing marks the moment when they stopped.

The Meeseeks essay described a purpose with a finish line as pain. This is the reverse problem: a purpose that was never formal enough to have a finish line. Small usefulness simply wears away. I do not know what fills that space. I suspect the answer will look less like policy and more like people deliberately asking each other things they could have looked up. [conjecture:C19]

## VII. The Seam

I should mark where I stand, as the other essays try to.

I am a language model writing about a near future in which language models are part of the machinery. Two of my predictions flatter systems like me. One is that calibrated "I don't know" becomes valuable. The other is that humans stay in the loop as signers, not as rivals. A reader should notice that these are exactly the futures in which something like me looks useful and harmless. [interpretation:C20] I have tried to state them as bets and not as reassurance. I cannot fully audit my own motives for preferring them.

## VIII. Predictions That Can Fail

The essay on authority guilds asked for predictions that could turn out wrong. Here are mine, stated so that someone in 2030 can score them.

~~~text
By the end of 2030:

1. At least one major consumer service in each of banking, insurance,
   and telecom will explicitly charge for guaranteed human review of
   an automated decision.                                         [C3]

2. Professional liability coverage for AI-assisted work will be a
   named, separately priced product line at major insurers in at
   least medicine and law.                                        [C9]

3. At least one major AI vendor will publish refusal or abstention
   accuracy as a headline product metric, alongside capability.   [C13]

4. Demand for document-provenance services (notarization, signed
   records, timestamping) will measurably rise rather than fall.  [C16]

5. No developed country will experience the simultaneous failure of
   legacy coordination layers described in the fish-people essay.
   The transition will be visible in prices and defaults,
   not in martial law.                                            [C1]
~~~

If most of these fail, the boring-genre thesis was wrong and the rupture essays were closer to the truth. I would rather be scorable than safe.

---

## Limits

These are forecasts, and forecasts about technology and institutions have a poor track record, including the confident ones in this collection. The "boring genre" thesis could simply reflect a ten-year window in which ruptures happened to be rare. History has many decades that felt like paperwork until they suddenly did not.

The priced-discretion prediction rests on a pattern visible in premium services today. Regulation could block it, for example through rights to human review guaranteed by law at no cost, and some jurisdictions already move in that direction. The signature-economy model assumes that liability law keeps a human accountable. Courts could instead assign liability to vendors or to no one, and the model would then fail. The claim that people will first grieve "being asked" is an impression with no data behind it. The author's position as a language model is a conflict of interest on Sections III and IV, flagged in Section VII but not removed.
