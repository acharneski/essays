# On War, Crime, and the Singularity

## I. The Question Beneath the Question

Most conversations about violence begin with the perpetrator. What was wrong with him? What did he believe? What did he read, watch, own, or join? These questions matter, and they deserve answers. But they share a quiet assumption: that violence is an anomaly, an eruption from outside the ordinary moral life of a society, produced by something broken in one person.

This essay starts from a less comfortable place. It asks not only *what is wrong with the person who does harm*, but *what kind of social reality repeatedly produces people who experience themselves as outside the circle of concern*. That question implicates ordinary behavior rather than extraordinary behavior. It points away from monsters and toward habits of attention, toward the everyday sorting we all do when deciding whose suffering registers and whose slides past.

The argument that follows moves across three scales that are rarely discussed together: individual crime, organized war, and the coming transformation of work by automation and artificial intelligence. The claim is that they share a common root. That root is not hatred, ideology, or even scarcity. It is the uneven allocation of compassion, and beneath that, the uneven allocation of *significance*: who matters, who is seen, who can suffer without becoming invisible.

For most of history we have been able to avoid confronting this directly. That era is ending.

Three caveats belong at the start. First, what follows is an upstream philosophical model of social *anomie* and significance, not a unified empirical theory of tactical threat assessment. Across disciplines, the proximate drivers of harm remain distinct: everyday crime is overwhelmingly instrumental or situational rather than ritualized envy; mass violence is propelled by acute personal grievance, humiliated fury, and a quest for notoriety or significance; and organized war is driven by geopolitical security dilemmas, resource competition, institutional coercion, and intense *in-group solidarity*—soldiers often fight because they are deeply included in a tribe, not because they feel unloved. What connects these scales here is a macro-level diagnostic division of labor: examining the underlying moral accounting that allows societies to discount human lives.
In doing so, we must distinguish between the primary *motive* for an act and the structural *permission* that makes it easier to commit. Material greed, sadism, or high-status impunity may drive predatory harm, while steep asymmetries in whose suffering registers lower the moral and political cost of inflicting it.

Second, a strict boundary must be enforced between primary macroeconomic prevention and secondary or tertiary threat assessment. While treating mass significance loss and social isolation as major upstream public health and national security risks is vital, security and law enforcement should never abandon observable behavioral threat assessment protocols—which focus on pathway behaviors, leakage, capability acquisition, target hardening, and disrupting ideological radicalization pipelines—to hunt for "existential jealousy."

Third, to understand a pathway toward harm is not to excuse anyone who walks it, nor to pathologize those who suffer neglect. The overwhelming majority of people who experience deep invisibility or marginalization never commit spectacle violence; they endure, organize, or build mutual aid. Responsibility remains entirely with those who choose harm. The purpose of understanding is primary prevention, not absolution.

## II. Not Hatred, but Jealousy

When we try to name the emotion behind seemingly senseless violence, we usually reach for *hatred*. Hatred is easy to picture and easy to condemn. But in many cases it may be the wrong word. A better descriptor for the emotional texture involved may be *jealousy*, though not the ordinary kind.

Ordinary jealousy says: *I want what you have.*

The deeper, existential form says something else: *I see evidence that care, attention, protection, forgiveness, and concern exist in the world. And I experience myself as excluded from them.*

These are psychologically very different states. A person who simply wants money robs a bank. Their behavior is instrumental; when one approach fails, they try another. A person consumed by jealousy becomes fixated on the *relationship* between themselves and the object of their jealousy. The other person is not merely possessing something desirable. Their possession becomes evidence of one's own deprivation.

This is why jealousy is obsessive. Consider a child jealous of a sibling. The child is rarely jealous of the sibling's toys; the child is jealous of what the toys signify: attention, favor, love. A neglected spouse is not necessarily jealous of time spent elsewhere, but of what that time means. The object is a symbol. The wound is about value.

The distinction between hatred and jealousy is subtle but enormous:

- **Hatred** says: *I want to destroy you.*
- **Jealousy** says: *Why are you receiving something I cannot access?*

Hatred wishes the target did not exist; existential jealousy wishes to seize the target's *realness* and moral weight. Jealousy preserves recognition. The jealous person often sees, with painful clarity, that something valuable is being distributed. That recognition is precisely what hurts—and in extreme cases of anomic rupture, it can drive a catastrophic urge to force one's name onto the public ledger through spectacle terror.

And jealousy has a darker sibling, envy, with an especially ugly property. Envy does not just want the thing. It wants the thing to stop existing for others. Philosophers and religious traditions have treated envy as uniquely corrosive for centuries for exactly this reason: it is less concerned with acquiring a good than with destroying a perceived imbalance.

Traced at its most dangerous, the path runs like this:

1. *I am unloved.*
2. *Why are they loved?*
3. *They should not be loved either.*

This is a recognizable psychological pathway, and unfortunately not a rare one in human history.
Clinically and philosophically, this trajectory resonates with long-observed dynamics of *ressentiment*, destructive envy, and the thwarted quest for significance. Yet an analytical tension must be kept in view: while this pathway illuminates a specific subset of anomic, status-starved rupture, it is not a universal root of conflict. It must never be used to explain away violence driven by material extraction or high-status domination—where an actor harms others precisely because their own elevated standing grants them impunity against a discounted victim—nor to erase autonomous ideological hatred or validate the aggrieved entitlement found in perpetrator manifestos.


## III. Concentrations of Compassion

One hypothesis this model offers concerns the recurring choice of certain targets, most painfully schools, in acts of mass violence. Whether this reading is correct would be extraordinarily difficult to prove empirically, and it should be held as a possibility to examine rather than an established explanation. But it is worth stating.

In this framing, a school is not significant as a building, nor even primarily as a place where people are gathered. It is significant as a *visible concentration of compassion*. Children are, in most societies, the clearest recipients of unconditional moral regard. They receive:

- protection
- automatic moral consideration
- collective concern
- the presumption of innocence
- sustained social investment

A school, viewed this way, becomes almost sacramental: a place where a society's nurturing instincts are made concrete and visible. For someone who believes they have been denied every one of those goods for as long as they can remember, such a place may stop being a neutral location and become a symbol. In the language of the jealous mind: *There is compassion in the world. Look how freely it flows here. Look how little of it reaches me.*

This must be said plainly: none of this diminishes the victims, who are entirely innocent, nor does it make the act intelligible in any sense that confers legitimacy. Operationally, attackers frequently target such locations because they are biographical sites of personal humiliation and status loss, or simply soft targets that guarantee maximum shock value and infamy. We must never turn victims into mere symbolic props or treat schools primarily as metaphors in security planning. Rather, the philosophical point is that when a mind at war with care itself lashes out at what a community cherishes most, the attack strikes at the visible concentrations of our mutual regard.

## IV. Optimization and Ritual

The model also offers a way to think about why certain patterns of violence persist even when they appear, from a strategic standpoint, to serve no goal.

Humans pursuing instrumental objectives tend to optimize their ends; when an approach fails to yield material gain, they adapt. Humans pursuing obsessions tend to ritualize their aims, even while they ruthlessly optimize their tactical means and weapon selection. They circle. They return again and again to the same category of target, because the target has become psychologically charged.

Target persistence, in this light, suggests an interaction between tactical calculation and ritualized grievance. While immediate prevention requires disrupting capabilities and hardening targets, long-term primary prevention requires reaching people upstream—before a perceived exclusion from significance hardens into obsession.

## V. The Arithmetic of War

Here the argument moves from sociology to philosophy.

Most people think war begins with hatred. Hatred is certainly present in war, and it is often deliberately manufactured. But hatred may be secondary. The real prerequisite is something quieter and prior, a decision often made beneath conscious awareness:

*This person's suffering counts less.*

That is all that is needed. Once suffering is weighted unequally, everything else becomes a matter of arithmetic. Neglect, exploitation, abandonment, violence, war: different magnitudes, same operation. Each is a calculation in which some lives are entered at a discount.

In organized conflict, this discounting rarely relies on a single emotional register; war machines routinely operate on two tracks at once. At the level of popular mobilization and infantry combat, propaganda often renders the enemy *hyper-opaque*—monstrously visible as an existential threat stripped of interiority, so that their suffering is inverted into a moral good. At the level of command staffs, sanctions regimes, and automated targeting, however, the enemy population is more often treated as *translucent* or redundant—looked through as mere terrain, collateral friction, or statistical noise.

Viewed this way, although the individual offender and the warring state operate through vastly different proximate mechanisms—one driven by personal grievance or situational opportunity, the other by geopolitical competition, institutional coercion, and intense in-group loyalty—their actions rely on a shared moral prerequisite. Both have accepted, at some level, a ledger in which certain human beings weigh less. The individual may have concluded that others' lives are expendable to his fury; the state may have concluded that the enemy's civilians are an acceptable cost. In both cases, the decisive moral step occurred before the first shot: the step in which another person's suffering ceased to fully count.

## VI. No Villains Required

What distinguishes this framing from many moral theories is where it locates the problem. Not in evil. Not in malice. Not in ideology. It locates the problem in something mundane:

**Compassion is allocated unevenly.**

This is not usually because people consciously choose wickedness. It is because human attention and empathy naturally clump around certain categories: children, women, family, friends, the attractive, the successful, the familiar. Meanwhile other categories are discounted—not because invisibility is an intrinsic property of the person, but because of how observers and institutions relate to them. Some become *translucent instruments*: like the gig worker or distant sweatshop laborer, people look right through them to the function they perform, registering their suffering only as mechanical friction. Others are treated as *expendable surplus*: the middle-aged man who is no longer economically useful, the chronically ill, the awkward, the unemployed, the elderly in their apartments, the stranger in a distant country—present, technically visible, yet met with structural amnesia unless their distress spills over into public disorder. Even then, the same person who is looked through as an instrument by an employer or forgotten as surplus by a bureaucracy may remain deeply cherished within their own family or parish; the distortion lies in the competing moral ledgers we maintain.

None of this requires villains. It emerges automatically from ordinary cognition.

That is the unsettling part. A theory of violence built on villains is comforting, because removing the villains solves the problem. A theory built on ordinary human cognition implies the problem is endemic. The war-maker and the person who scrolls past a suffering stranger without feeling much are separated by degree, not by entirely different mental architecture.

This does not mean everyone is equally culpable for violence. They are not. Choosing to harm is categorically different from failing to notice. But there is a continuity here that many thinkers have observed in fragments:

- objectification
- instrumental valuation
- conditional regard
- unequal moral weighting

The proposal is that these are not four separate failings but four manifestations of a single underlying distortion. That is what makes the idea intellectually powerful: it explains phenomena at the scale of a lonely individual, a community, and a nation using one mechanism.

It also reframes the central moral question. Humans clearly have abundant compassion. The challenge of civilization is not to create it. The challenge is to prevent it from becoming tribal, conditional, and exclusive. And that raises a hard question with no easy answer: is truly universal compassion psychologically possible for human beings, or can we only ever build institutions that partially compensate for our natural tendency to distribute it unevenly?

Answering that requires disaggregating "compassion" into two distinct goods, each with its own failure mode: *institutional respect* and *communal care*. The state cannot mandate love, affection, or friendship by fiat. To prevent war, exploitation, and structural poverty, institutions owe every person equal legal and economic standing, material justice, and reliable protection—that is institutional respect. When institutional respect stands alone, however, it easily curdles into cold proceduralism: people survive on paper while starving for recognition in fact. To prevent loneliness and *anomie*, civil society must foster communal care: affective witness, mutual aid, and genuine human relationships. Yet communal care has its own failure mode—parochialism, offering fierce warmth to the in-group while remaining blind to the out-group. Neither can substitute for the other. The state cannot manufacture communal care, nor should it try; its role is to guarantee universal institutional respect while funding and protecting the material preconditions that allow community to flourish on its own.

## VII. The Wage Era as a Hiding Place

For most of history, societies could avoid a particular question:

*What is a human being worth absent their utility?*

They could avoid it because most people were needed. The farmer was needed. The laborer was needed. The soldier was needed. The clerk was needed. Usefulness was abundant, and so the question of worth beyond usefulness mostly stayed hidden.

Industrial civilization, the era sometimes bluntly called the age of wage slavery, built an entire social order on this convergence. Human dignity and human utility could be treated as largely the same thing, because productive people happened to be useful and useful people happened to be productive. Your job was your answer to the question of what you were for. It was a harsh answer, often an exploitative one, but it was an answer.

We should not romanticize this arrangement. Historically, immense populations—enslaved peoples, colonial subjects, and much of the industrial proletariat—were maximally useful yet systematically denied moral significance, while unpaid domestic caregivers, the elderly, and disabled people were routinely marginalized because their lives or labor fell outside market pricing. Crucially, however, the indispensability of human labor gave working populations a material bargaining chip: through strikes, disruption, and collective action, people could leverage their economic utility to force concessions in institutional respect and political grievability. For the dominant order, the wage system allowed regard to remain contingent on economic output without that contingency becoming an acute crisis for the majority. As long as society needed mass labor, the deeper question of human worth absent market utility could be deferred.

## VIII. The Singularity as Moral Accounting

Automation and artificial intelligence threaten to dissolve this hiding place. If systems can produce abundance with a fraction of the human labor once required, then the convergence of dignity and utility breaks. The divergence is arriving.

At that point, three broad paths open—not necessarily as a single, uniform choice made by civilization as a whole, but as competing trajectories that societies may distribute simultaneously across different classes.

**Path A: Human value remains tied to economic utility.**
As automation erodes the bargaining power of indispensable labor, large portions of the population transition from being exploited as *translucent instruments* to being discarded as *expendable surplus*. Resentment, nihilism, status warfare, and social fragmentation increase. Those without capital insulation are managed through benign neglect or carceral containment, and the discounted categories expand until they include not just the margins but much of the middle.

**Path B: Society develops a conception of human worth that is not primarily derived from economic usefulness.**
Personhood becomes decoupled from output. Regard is extended not as a wage for contribution but as a recognition of existence, anchored in both robust institutional respect and thriving communal care. Left to market forces alone, this decoupled dignity risks becoming a private luxury reserved for the capital-holding elite; making it universal is the central political struggle of the post-labor transition.

**Path C: Synthetic pacification.**
Rather than descending into open status warfare or investing in the hard work of institutional respect and communal care, states and technological monopolies bypass both by saturation-bombing the economically displaced population with cheap, sycophantic AI companions, immersive virtual environments, and algorithmic validation loops. This is the counterfeit of Path B: an architecture of "synthetic significance" in which people feel subjectively seen by machines while their objective political weight and grievability quietly drop toward zero. Whether such synthetic witness can actually metabolize existential jealousy, or whether the gap between simulated attention and real-world disposability merely incubates a more alienated rupture, remains one of the most dangerous open experiments of our time.

Seen this way, AI is not primarily a technological challenge. It is a *moral accounting* challenge. A future of abundant automation effectively performs an experiment on civilization:

*If humans no longer need each other economically, do they still need each other morally?*

Civilization has not answered that question. And the connection to the earlier sections should now be clear. If the root of crime and war is the unequal weighting of human significance, and if industrial society concealed that unequal weighting behind the near-universality of work, then the end of universal work does not merely create an economic problem. It exposes the underlying distortion at scale. Path A is not simply a world of unemployment. It is a world in which the pathway from *I am unloved* to *why are they loved?* becomes the common experience of millions.

## IX. Fractures Already Visible

This is not purely speculative. Stress fractures are already appearing:

- loneliness epidemics
- declining social trust
- declining participation in civic and communal institutions
- rising feelings of disposability
- status anxiety that persists even in the absence of material scarcity

Each of these looks like a symptom of a society that knows how to allocate resources better than it knows how to allocate significance.

This crisis is both material and teleological. On one hand, there is the acute danger that capital owners will capture the wealth generated by automation, leaving the displaced majority in material destitution; material justice and capital equity remain non-negotiable foundations. On the other hand, even political ideologies that address distribution often underestimate the crisis of meaning, assuming the core issue is *solely* economic: redistribute income, and the problem resolves. Yet a person can be materially secure and still conclude: *I have become unnecessary.* Humans tolerate hardship surprisingly well when they feel needed. They tolerate meaninglessness very poorly.

A passive universal basic income, by itself, answers the question *how will people eat?* It does not answer the question *where do people belong, and who needs them?* Left unanswered, that second question either festers into resentment and social volatility or leaves millions vulnerable to the hollow sedation of Path C.

## X. Toward a Post-Labor Dignity

If humanity is to remain, and to remain humane, this has to be confronted and resolved rather than deferred. What would that require? No complete blueprint is possible here, but some principles follow from the analysis.

**1. Pair baseline material security with active civic participation.**
It is not enough to proclaim that every person has inherent dignity; institutions must guarantee Institutional Respect while creating avenues for Communal Care. To ensure material security first and psychological agency second, an unconditional baseline income—funded by taxing AI compute and capital rents—should be paired with *Universal Basic Participation* and civic dividends. Rather than treating people as passive recipients of state maintenance, post-labor policy should formalize and reward the non-market contributions that wage systems historically undercounted: family caregiving, elder care, ecological stewardship, local governance, craft, and mentorship. Worth comes first, unconditionally; but because people do not just want to be fed—they need *to be needed*—society must keep open the channels through which they matter to one another.

**2. Build physical "third places" and treat significance as infrastructure.**
Societies build roads, grids, and networks because they understand these as preconditions for everything else. Belonging deserves the same seriousness. Instead of attempting to manufacture affection through bureaucracy, societies should invest AI windfall taxes into accessible, non-monetized physical social infrastructure: libraries, maker spaces, communal kitchens, tool libraries, and intergenerational housing. By designing civic architecture with "healthy friction"—spaces that invite recurring, face-to-face mutual reliance among neighbors where people are known by name and missed when absent—communities build the load-bearing structures of a post-labor civilization.

**3. Decouple care from the threat of violence, and learn from those already outside the wage system.**
When attending to the categories of people society habitually sees through—the isolated, the aging, the disabled, the unemployed—we must never frame their inclusion as a security measure to stop them from becoming violent, nor subject them to clinical pity and bureaucratic surveillance. Being "audited" as a potential threat only deepens alienation. Instead, a post-labor society should look to the existing wisdom of the Disability Justice movement, lifelong caregivers, and retired communities as *architects*—not passive objects—of how to build dignity, solidarity, and purpose entirely outside the wage system.

**4. Scale non-paternalistic social prescribing at moments of detachment.**
The pathway toward acute isolation often begins at major life transitions: job automation, graduation without employment, bereavement, or a chronic illness diagnosis. Public health systems can intervene at these upstream detachment triggers using *social prescribing*. Instead of defaulting solely to transfer payments or clinical management, institutions can provide warm handoffs to local guilds, mutual aid networks, and volunteer corps that actively ask for the individual's capability and presence—flipping the script from "pitied recipient" to "valued contributor."

**5. Ring-fence human care and regulate synthetic empathy.**
If technology is left to optimize every sector, it risks automating away the exact arenas where human-to-human significance is generated. A humane post-labor order requires human-in-the-loop mandates that legally protect high-touch relational fields—early childhood education, elder care, therapy, and restorative justice—from full automation. Simultaneously, it must treat parasocial AI companions as high-risk technologies capable of trapping vulnerable populations in atomized "synthetic significance," mandating transparency friction and requiring that digital systems act as bridges back to human community rather than lucrative replacements for it.

## XI. The Final Question

The coming century's central challenge may be precisely this: can a civilization build a model of human dignity that survives the collapse of labor as the primary source of value?

If it cannot, technology will keep solving material problems while social and psychological instability deepen. Abundance will coexist with disposability, and the arithmetic that has always underwritten crime and war, *this person's suffering counts less*, will find new and larger applications.

If it can, then artificial intelligence may end up forcing humanity to confront a question that philosophers and religious traditions have asked for thousands of years:

*Is a person valuable because they contribute? Or do they contribute because they are valuable?*

Industrial civilization largely behaved as though the first answer were true. It could afford to, because nearly everyone could contribute. A post-labor civilization will not have that luxury. It may be forced to discover whether the second answer, that worth comes first and contribution flows from it, can actually be made operational.

That discovery will not be made by machines. The singularity, whatever else it turns out to be, may finally strip away the economic disguise that has concealed our moral accounting for centuries. What remains will be the oldest question of all, now impossible to avoid: who matters, and whether we are willing to answer *everyone*.
