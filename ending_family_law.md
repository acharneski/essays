# Ending Family Law

## I. A Docket Is Not a Family

There is a public web page somewhere in an Ohio county court system that contains nearly five years of my life. It does not look like much: a column of dates and a column of terse entries, each linked to a scanned document. I filed the case myself in May 2021, along with a motion to transfer jurisdiction under the Uniform Child Custody Jurisdiction and Enforcement Act and a notice concerning a foreign judgment. The court classified the matter as a motion for custody or change of custody.

I want to be precise about that, because the precision matters. I was not dragged into family court. I walked into it. I am a father who wanted his parental rights recognized, and the only door available to me opened into a building I now believe should not exist in its current form.

Read the docket from top to bottom and you watch parenthood dissolve into administration. The jurisdictional phase alone took months of competing filings before a judgment confirmed the registered orders. Then a guardian ad litem was appointed. A case-management order set deadlines for alternative dispute resolution, discovery, and motions, with trial set for the following March. Then the motions began, from both sides: protective orders, drug and alcohol testing, reasonable accommodations, memoranda in opposition, agreed interim visitation, agreed protective orders over medical records, a request for a custody evaluation, a proposed shared-parenting plan.

In April 2022 the magistrate ordered both parents to undergo a forensic psychological custody evaluation. Two adults who had made a child together were now, by court order, objects of clinical examination, because they disagreed about how to allocate time with that child.

The trial that was supposed to happen in March 2022 moved to June. The June trial became an attorneys-only conference, and trial moved to August. The August dates were vacated. Trial was set for January 2023. Meanwhile the machine kept running: parent coaching, subpoenas, a withdrawal and replacement of counsel, deposition notices, supplementation requests. In December 2022 came a motion for contempt and attorney fees, then a motion to compel discovery, then, in January, motions in limine and for sanctions, witness lists, exhibits, my deposition entered into the record.

A motion is not a finding. A title on a docket proves only that someone typed it. I am not going to relitigate any of it here, and I am not going to pretend that a single case proves a national pattern. That is not the point of this essay.

The point is what happened next. The day before the rescheduled trial, a joint shared-parenting plan was filed. The day of the trial, the court entered an agreed judgment, stipulated findings on child support, an order appointing a parenting coordinator, and a shared-parenting decree with our joint plan attached.

After roughly twenty months of procedural warfare, no one was declared the good parent. No one won. Two parents agreed to share their child, which is something two parents could, in principle, have agreed to on the first day, at a tiny fraction of the cost, if the institution in front of them had been designed to help them do that rather than to process them.

And even then it did not end. New counsel appeared years later. A new parenting coordinator was appointed in 2025. A relocation notice was filed that November. The most recent entry is dated February 2026. The court's financial summary lists about $1,775 in record costs, a number so disconnected from the true cost of what that page represents that it is almost a joke. It does not count attorneys, evaluators, coaches, coordinators, lost work, lost sleep, or lost years.

This essay is about why I believe family law, as an adversarial profession and an institutional ecosystem, should be ended, and what should replace it. It is also about the hardest objection to that project, which I take seriously: that some families need more protection, not less.

## II. The Process Is the Punishment

Most debates about family court are arguments about outcomes. Do judges favor mothers? What percentage of fathers receive equal parenting time? How often are custody orders modified? These are legitimate questions, and the data on them is contested. But an outcomes-only analysis misses the thing that most defines the experience of the people inside the system.

The process is not merely the road to the result. The process is the result, or at least most of it.

You do not need to lose a final judgment for the institution to have reorganized your life. For twenty months, before any contested ruling on the merits, my relationship with my child was mediated by interim orders, scheduling conflicts, evaluations, and the strategic calendar of litigation. Every month a trial is continued is a month a child grows older under provisional arrangements. Every discovery request is a demand that a parent convert his private life into evidence. Every evaluation is a demand that he convert his mind into a report someone else will write.

The harm is not only felt; it can be described in the vocabulary of several disciplines at once. For a young child, twenty months is not an administrative interval. It is a large and unrecoverable fraction of a developmental timeline, lived under chronic stress, in which the attunement between parent and child is interrupted by schedules drawn up for litigation rather than for the child. In legal terms, a "temporary" order that persists for most of two years is not temporary at all. It establishes a de facto status quo without anyone ever having held an evidentiary hearing, and the status quo then becomes the baseline against which every later claim is measured. And for the parent fleeing an abusive partner, the same procedural friction becomes a weapon. Advocates call it litigation abuse: an abuser can use continuances, motions, and discovery to drain a protective parent's money and resolve until capitulation looks like the only way out.

That last point matters to me because it cuts against any reading of this essay as a story about fathers versus mothers. The procedural machinery that consumed my years is the same machinery an abuser can turn against a victim. A slow, expensive, process-maximizing system does not protect the vulnerable. It rewards whoever can best afford to keep the process going.

A system that delivers a fair verdict after two years of compelled legibility has not delivered fairness. It has delivered a fair verdict on top of an enormous unexamined penalty, paid in advance, by both parents, and ultimately by the child.

There is a serious objection here, and I want to meet it rather than step around it. Litigators will say that high-conflict cases settle on the courthouse steps *because* of the process, not despite it. Discovery, depositions, and evaluations force information into the open. They puncture unrealistic expectations and push recalcitrant parties toward reality. On this view, my agreement was not "always available on day one." It became available only after twenty months of information-forcing. Others make a darker point: settlements on the eve of trial often reflect financial exhaustion, frequently the exhaustion of the protective parent, rather than genuine agreement.

I take both points seriously, and neither rescues the current system. If the process really performs an information-forcing function, then the question is why that information must arrive through two years of adversarial combat rather than through structured, mandatory disclosure in the first weeks of a case. And if courthouse-step settlements are often products of exhaustion, that is not a defense of the process. It is an indictment of it. Either the process is an extraordinarily expensive way of delivering information that could have been gathered cheaply, or it is a war of attrition that ends when someone runs out of money. In both cases it is the process, not the families, that needs to change.

## III. An Ecosystem, Not a Conspiracy

When I first described this to others, I used the word "parasites." I still feel the force of that word. But I have come to think "ecosystem" is analytically sharper, and in some ways more damning.

Look at how many kinds of actors enter a dispute between two parents: attorneys on each side, a magistrate, a guardian ad litem, a forensic custody evaluator, a parent coach, a parenting coordinator, process servers, court reporters, the whole apparatus of discovery and deposition. Each one is another interface through which a family is made legible to an institution, and many of them are paid by the hour or by the engagement.

This does not require anyone to be a villain. Many of these people are conscientious. Some are genuinely helpful. The problem is structural, and it is worse precisely because it does not depend on bad intentions.

Parts of this structure resemble what economists call a principal-agent problem, compounded by moral hazard. The parents want some combination of a relationship with their child, stability, autonomy, and an end to conflict. They want durable resolution and, eventually, exit. The attorneys, guardians ad litem, forensic evaluators, and parenting coordinators are largely sustained by assessment, representation, documentation, supervision, adjudication, and continued process, under billable-hour or repeat-engagement models. The analogy is imperfect, and I want to be careful with it. A judge, a guardian, or an evaluator owes duties to the child, the court, and the law, not simply to the parents, so the parents are not their principals in any clean sense. The more accurate description is misaligned incentives under involuntary intermediation. The parents did not choose most of these agents, often cannot fire them, and usually pay for them anyway. Whatever we call it, the objectives are not the same. Sometimes they point in opposite directions.

An ecosystem does not coordinate around a conference table. It selects for organisms that can survive on the available nutrient stream. In family law, the nutrient stream is conflict and its paperwork. Practices that resolve cases quickly and cheaply earn less than practices that manage them thoroughly. Evaluators are retained because a dispute exists, not because a dispute has ended. A coordinator's role exists only as long as two parents are presumed unable to coordinate themselves. No one has to plot anything. The incentives do the plotting.

And money is only half of the diet. The ecosystem also feeds on defensibility. Institutions tend to drift toward minimizing blame rather than minimizing harm, and an intermediary is valuable to a court not only for what he discovers but for the cover he provides. *We appointed the guardian. We ordered the evaluation. We followed the recommendation. We required the co-parenting app.* If something later goes catastrophically wrong, there is a record showing that everyone did something. That creates demand for intermediation regardless of whether it improves outcomes for children. It also makes the ecosystem harder to reform than greed alone would. Even if every evaluator worked for free tomorrow, courts would still have reason to order evaluations, because the report moves the risk of the decision off the decision-maker.

So the accusation is not "my judge was unfair." The accusation is this:

> Family-law conflict creates an ecosystem in which the people with the greatest lifelong stake in reaching a durable equilibrium are not the people whose professional incentives govern the path toward it.

The child and the parents carry the stakes for the rest of their lives. The intermediaries carry the case for a few years, then close the file. Yet it is the intermediaries who control the pace, the vocabulary, the procedures, and the record.

This is the part of my critique that finds the least resistance, even among people who disagree with me about almost everything else. Advocates for abuse survivors, legal scholars, and critics of my proposals all tend to agree that court-appointed intermediaries hold too much unaccountable authority. Guardians ad litem and forensic evaluators often rely on constructs with thin scientific support. In practice they exercise a kind of quasi-judicial power that was never properly delegated to them. Their recommendations can effectively decide a case while escaping the scrutiny a judge's ruling would receive. People who argue from the father's side and people who argue from the victim's side arrive at the same place: these roles should be reserved for extraordinary cases and held to strict, auditable evidentiary standards.

## IV. Agency Conversion

There is a specific transformation that happens to a parent who enters this system. I call it agency conversion.

You walk in saying: *I am this child's father.*

The machinery translates that into: *I am a litigant asserting a claim to an allocation of parental authority, whose suitability will be represented to decision-makers through institutional intermediaries.*

That translation is not cosmetic. Once it happens, you are no longer a parent exercising an obvious natural responsibility. You are an applicant. You must demonstrate yourself through categories controlled by other people: fitness, cooperation, compliance, credibility, psychological condition, parenting quality, adherence to orders. Your relationship with your own child becomes something you hold provisionally, pending review, as though it had begun with the institution rather than long before it.

The intermediaries acquire extraordinary importance despite having none of the permanent stakes. A report written in a few weeks by someone who has met your child a handful of times can weigh more than years of daily care.

An allegation repeated across enough filings can harden into an institutional fact simply by being present in the record, whether or not it was ever tested. The mechanism is mundane. Dockets are stacks of unstructured PDFs. A claim appears in a motion. It is quoted in a response, summarized in an evaluator's report, and cited back in a later filing as though the report had established it. Each repetition launders the claim a little further from its origin, until no one can easily tell whether it was ever proven. Call it citation laundering. It is one of the most corrosive features of the current record, and it harms the falsely accused and the genuinely endangered alike: the first because untested accusations become facts, the second because real findings drown in a sea of indistinguishable assertions.

The parent becomes the object being processed; the specialists become the subjects doing the processing.

This is the deepest injury of the system, and it falls on mothers and fathers alike, though not always equally. Whatever one concludes about sex bias in final rulings, it is entirely possible for a parent to experience profound institutional asymmetry long before a judge ever decides anything.

## V. Why Pitchforks Fail

People who have been through this often want vengeance. They want to expose individual judges, shame particular evaluators, march on courthouses. I understand the impulse. I reject it, not because it is too extreme, but because it is not extreme enough.

An angry mob confirms the institution's model of the angry litigant. It is metabolized as one more data point proving that the parents are the unstable element and the professionals are the calm adults in the room. Even a successful revolt merely replaces today's personnel with tomorrow's, who then inherit the same incentives and evolve the same behaviors.

Real reform does something worse to a dysfunctional ecosystem. It changes the fitness function. It alters the environment so that the organisms that thrived on conflict can no longer survive, and the ones that remain are the ones that serve families.

That requires separating two things institutions chronically confuse: the purpose of a procedure and the procedure itself. The purpose might be to protect a child from a dangerous parent. The procedure becomes a guardian, an evaluator, a hearing, a report, a motion, a temporary order, a review. After enough years, questioning the procedure sounds like questioning child protection. It is not. A procedure is an implementation, and implementations should be judged by whether they serve their purpose. What family law currently does is not evidence of what families inherently require.

The single most important change is a change of goal:

> A healthy family-law system should be trying to make itself unnecessary to every family that enters it.

Success is not a thorough record. Success is not a well-supported decree. Success is the day two parents no longer need the court, the lawyers, the evaluator, or the coordinator, and can simply raise their child.

That goal has one crucial limit, and I will return to it: for a family with a dangerous parent in it, the court getting out of the way can mean removing the only barrier between a child and harm. Exit is the right goal for ordinary families. It is the wrong goal for protection.

Measured against the goal of exit for the families who should be able to leave, the current system is not failing by accident. It is succeeding at something else.

## VI. The Automation Moment

For most of history there was an honest defense of the family-law profession. The work was genuinely hard to do without training: knowing the statutes and local rules, drafting pleadings in the right form, calculating support under a guideline worksheet, organizing years of messages and records into a coherent chronology, tracking deadlines, navigating hearings. Ordinary people could not do it, so they paid people who could. The information bottleneck was real, and professionals were paid to stand in it.

That bottleneck is collapsing.

Even the profession's own trade publications now describe family law as unusually suited to artificial intelligence, precisely because it is drowning in documents, communications, shifting facts, and repetitive information-management work. Organizations focused on access to justice are openly debating how to regulate AI that delivers legal help directly to ordinary people rather than merely augmenting attorneys, and some have warned against smothering that possibility with over-regulation.

The profession's preferred framing is: *AI makes lawyers more productive.* That framing lets the existing ecosystem absorb the technology and convert it into higher margins. The same billable structure survives, with a faster engine.

The framing I propose is different: *AI eliminates the need for the intermediary in every task that does not genuinely require human judgment.*

Consider what can already be done, or will soon be done, competently and at near-zero marginal cost: document preparation; chronology construction; evidence organization; retrieval of statutes and case law; filing assistance; financial and child-support calculations; side-by-side comparison of proposed parenting plans; procedural navigation; and plain-language explanation of what is happening and what comes next. Each of those tasks is currently a revenue line. Each of those revenue lines currently sits between a parent and his or her own child.

The technical premise is not the radical part. The radical part is the decision about who keeps the dividend. If automation's gains flow to the professionals who previously monetized the bottleneck, we will have built a cheaper, faster version of the same ecosystem. If they flow to families, we can dismantle the bottleneck itself.

## VII. Pending Is a Decision

Automation does something more unsettling than lowering costs. It exposes what delay has always been.

A custody system allocates an irreversible resource: childhood. While a case waits, a temporary arrangement governs where the child sleeps, who takes her to school, and who is present for the ordinary hours that make up a relationship. That arrangement does not pause while the court deliberates. It is allocating parenting time every single day. Delay is therefore not neutral. A case marked "pending" for twenty months is a case in which twenty months of a child's life were decided, just not by anyone who had to sign a ruling.

For most of history, that fact was hidden behind real scarcity. Documents had to be gathered, typed, copied, served, read, and summarized by people who were busy. Some of the wait was genuinely the cost of processing information. But once collecting records, building a chronology, calculating support, comparing schedules, and retrieving precedent become nearly instantaneous, a six-month wait can no longer be explained mainly as the natural cost of processing. What remains is institutional latency, and institutional latency is a choice. Someone owns the queue.

Delay also has a property that makes it attractive to institutions trying to avoid blame. An affirmative decision has an author. If a judge orders something today and it turns out wrong, the error has a name attached. If the court simply lets the provisional state continue, and that state turns out to have done enormous damage, no one in particular did anything. They waited. Waiting spreads causality so thin that it disappears. An institution can exercise great power over a family while presenting itself as not yet having exercised any.

So I want to state the principle plainly:

> In a system allocating irreversible human time, the power to delay is part of the power to decide.

A system designed around that principle would treat every pending state as a decision with an owner. Every temporary order would carry an expiration date and a default that takes effect when it expires. Every continuance would be recorded with a reason and the person responsible for granting it. The days a child spends under provisional arrangements would be counted and published, not buried in a docket.

## VIII. Do Not Build a Robotic Parasite

There is a trap here, and reformers should name it before they fall into it. Replacing a four-hundred-dollar-an-hour human with a four-cent inference call is not reform if the process itself stays the same. Economists know what happens when you make a resource dramatically cheaper without changing the rules for using it: consumption explodes. This is the Jevons Paradox, and in litigation it means a filing flood. An automated adversarial system can generate motions faster than any human, overwhelm dockets with filings, and turn conflict into an even cheaper weapon, one that vexatious and abusive litigants will wield first and hardest. That is a robotic parasite, and it would be worse than what we have now.

The real opportunity is not to automate the existing process but to redesign it so that most of its work never needs to be performed.

The current model looks like this:

> conflict → lawyer A → lawyer B → filings → discovery → evaluator → arguments → judge → order

The replacement should look like this:

> structured facts → explicit rules and defaults → auditable exceptions → resolution

In this model, software does the translation between messy human reality and a structured legal framework. It gathers the facts each parent asserts, identifies where they agree and where they diverge, applies clear default rules, and surfaces only the genuine disagreements that require a decision. The division of labor matters. Statutory rules such as support calculations and default schedules should be executed by deterministic code that can be inspected, tested, and audited line by line, much as existing child-support calculators already are. Language models belong at the front door, helping a parent without a lawyer organize a chronology and translate a messy narrative into structured inputs. They should not be the thing deciding outcomes.

Even at the front door, they need watching. Language models are trained on the world's institutional writing, and they absorb its instincts about which errors are safe to make. I have watched one respond to a structural complaint about family court by quietly holding my claim to a higher evidentiary standard than the counter-narrative it offered in reply, sounding perfectly reasonable the whole time. I caught it because I pushed back. Most parents will not know to push back, and they should not have to. If AI becomes the front end of family administration, the ability to cross-examine the machine cannot become a prerequisite for receiving an honestly calibrated answer. Front-end tools should be tested for exactly that kind of asymmetry, the same way the rules engines behind them are tested for arithmetic.

I also have to be honest about the limit of the phrase "structured facts." Deterministic rules engines work beautifully on structured financial and calendar inputs. But in a contested custody case, the important facts are rarely clean data points. They are competing narratives of interpretation, context, and credibility. Software can tell you that an exchange was missed on a given Saturday. It cannot tell you whether the exchange was missed because of traffic, because one parent was withholding the child, or because the other parent showed up visibly intoxicated and the first parent was afraid. Those are not data-entry problems. They are fact-finding problems, and fact-finding in disputed cases requires a human being who can weigh credibility and be held accountable for the weighing.

So humans enter where there is a real factual dispute that cannot be resolved by documents, a genuine risk to a child's safety, or a decision that legitimately requires human judgment and human accountability.

That last category is not empty, and honesty requires saying so. There are families in which a parent is dangerous. There is domestic violence, abuse, addiction that endangers a child, and coercive control that a form cannot detect. Those cases need skilled human attention, protective intervention, and fast access to a judge. One of the cruelest features of the current system is that by treating every dispute as a full adversarial contest, it spreads its human attention thinly across every family. Endangered children and domestic-violence victims wait in the same congested line as two decent parents arguing about a holiday schedule. Diverting routine, non-safety matters out of the adversarial pipeline is not merely efficient. It is a moral imperative. An automation-first model is not a model without humans. It is a model that concentrates human judgment where it is actually needed.

Put differently, what family courts now handle is really three different kinds of work, run through one adversarial machine. There is *administration*: agreed facts, parentage, ordinary schedules, routine calculations, structured plans. That should be software-first. There is *adjudication*: genuinely unresolved disputes of fact or law. That should be judge-first, narrow in scope, and decided with explicit findings. And there is *protection*: credible danger, coercive control, child abuse, serious impairment. That should be human-first, fast, specialized, and properly funded. The current system mixes all three together and processes them identically. The reform is to sort each case by the kind of problem it is before deciding how to process it.

## IX. The Gate

Everything in an automation-first design depends on one decision: which families go down the automated path, and which go to a human. That intake gate is the single point of failure of the whole architecture, and anyone who proposes this model owes the public a candid account of how it can fail.

It can fail in two directions. If the threshold for leaving the default and reaching a human is too high, abused children are harmed. Coercive control and domestic violence rarely leave a tidy paper trail of police reports and hospital records before separation. Courts can also mistake coercive control for ordinary, symmetrical "high conflict," with unsafe results. If a victim must produce hard, auditable evidence before she or he can depart from shared parenting, many unrepresented and traumatized parents will be pushed, with their children, into arrangements with their abusers. Those are the false negatives, and they are the most dangerous error the system can make.

If the threshold is too low, if checking a box or making an allegation is enough, then strategic litigants will learn within weeks to make safety allegations as a matter of course. They will bypass the default and recreate the exact adversarial ecosystem this essay is trying to dismantle. Those are the false positives, and they would quietly destroy the reform from the inside.

There is a third way the gate can fail, and it is political rather than technical. The two errors are not equally visible. A single missed case of abuse that ends in tragedy produces a headline, an investigation, a named official, and the devastating question: *why didn't you do something?* A thousand unnecessary interventions produce a thousand private injuries, scattered across a thousand families, with no single event around which accountability can gather. An institution that fears blame will retune any gate, quietly and one decision at a time, toward the error that cannot be pinned on it. It will not do this because missing abuse is worse, although it is, but because missing abuse is more attributable. The result can be a system that inflicts far more total harm while feeling, to everyone inside it, like the cautious choice.

The answer is not to pretend the gate can be made perfect. It is to make the tradeoff explicit. Legislatures should own the tradeoffs that bureaucrats are now forced to hide. If society decides, as I think it should, that the gate must be highly sensitive because missing genuine danger is catastrophic, it should say so in statute. It should then measure both error rates and publish them, so that the price of that sensitivity is visible and can be reduced deliberately rather than inflated by accident. A gate whose error budget is set in public is much harder to bend in private.

I do not think the core tension can be wished away, but I think it can be managed. The answer is to put safety first in sequence, before any default applies.

**Screen before you default.** On registration, both parents should complete a confidential, behaviorally anchored safety assessment. It should be designed by domestic-violence and child-welfare experts to detect patterns of coercive control, substance abuse, and danger, not merely to check for criminal records. A form cannot see everything, but a well-designed instrument asks about behavior rather than labels, and it can surface what a box marked "abuse: yes/no" never will.

**A real safety track.** If the screening flags significant risk, or if a parent files a sworn safety affidavit under penalty of perjury, the case should bypass every automated default. It should go to a specialized judge for an expedited evidentiary hearing within fourteen days, not fourteen months. The sworn affidavit keeps the door open for victims whose abuse left no paper trail. The safety track must be easy to enter honestly and costly to enter dishonestly, and the second half of that sentence must never be allowed to undermine the first. Failing to prove an allegation is not the same as proving it false. Coercive control is hard to substantiate, and a sincere parent who cannot meet the evidentiary bar has not lied. Failure to substantiate a safety allegation must never, by itself, count as evidence of bad faith. Sanctions, meaning mandatory fee-shifting and parenting-time consequences, should require an affirmative finding, under a demanding standard of proof, that a materially false allegation was made knowingly and deliberately to manipulate custody. That keeps the door from becoming a tactical shortcut without punishing honest uncertainty.

**A different measure of success.** Cases on the safety track should be explicitly exempt from every "speed of exit" incentive. Tying anyone's pay or performance to how quickly a file closes would create a perverse incentive for mediators and judges to minimize abuse claims and press exhausted parents into unsafe settlements. Success there should be measured by child safety, compliance with protective orders, and the cessation of post-separation abuse.

**Advocates without a profit motive.** As routine cases move out of the private legal market, the money saved should follow the families who still need representation. States should fund salaried, state-employed family advocates, a public-defender model for high-risk family law, to represent indigent victims and indigent accused parents on the safety track. If there is any part of family law where the profit motive should be removed entirely, it is child protection.

None of this requires believing that shared parenting and protection are rival philosophies. Political debate often treats them that way, but they are better understood as branches in the same decision tree. Kentucky, for example, has paired a presumption of joint custody and equal parenting time with an exception that sets the presumption aside where a domestic-violence order exists. That is not the architecture I am proposing, but it shows that a strong default and a real safety override can live in the same statute. The question is not which value wins. The question is which branch a family belongs on, and how reliably the system can tell.

## X. The Architecture of an Exit

For the families who pass through the gate with no safety concerns identified, what would a family-law system designed for exit look like? I expect that population to be large, but how large should be measured in pilots rather than assumed in an essay. Nothing in the design depends on a particular fraction. I propose the following principles, each of which can be tested and measured.

**Parenthood before litigation.** A legal parent should begin with substantial, continuing parental authority, not be required to petition to recover it. Once safety screening has cleared a case, the system should immediately generate an administrative temporary order, with no lawyers and no hearing, built on shared parenting. The burden should fall on whoever seeks to depart from that default, and it should be a heavy one: clear and convincing evidence of detriment to the child or of logistical impossibility, not accusation. This is what ends the status-quo trap, in which whoever controls the child during months of "temporary" arrangements wins by default.

**Defaults built around children, not symmetry.** Shared parenting should not mean a rigid fifty-fifty split applied identically to a six-month-old and a twelve-year-old. A default that prioritizes parental symmetry over child development is simply a different way of putting adults first. Infants and toddlers plainly differ from school-aged children in how they handle separation and transition, and the default should reflect that. One plausible shape is more frequent, shorter periods with each parent for the youngest children, and more symmetrical blocks of time for school-aged children, arranged around school geography and minimal transitions. But the precise schedule is an empirical question, and the developmental evidence is still contested and still moving. So the defaults should not be frozen into statute as one theory of childhood. They should be versioned: published, tied to the research that supports them, and periodically revised as that research improves. Both parents remain parents. The schedule serves the child.

**Narrow, reviewable discretion.** Judicial discretion in family law is vast and nearly unreviewable in practice. Every departure from the default should require explicit, written findings tied to specific evidence, structured so that they can be appealed and audited.

**Symmetrical burdens.** The same evidentiary thresholds, enforcement mechanisms, procedural expectations, and presumptions should apply regardless of which parent invokes them and regardless of that parent's sex. Symmetry should not be assumed. It should be demonstrated with data.

**Kill the PDF.** Courts should replace flat stacks of scanned documents with database-driven portals. Initial filings, financial disclosures, and parenting plans should be submitted as structured data objects against published schemas, not as free-form prose. This does more than tidy the record. It closes the main channel for an AI-generated filing flood, because a schema does not accept forty pages of machine-written rhetoric where a structured field asks for a date, an amount, or a proposed schedule.

**Separate allegations from findings.** An accusation and a finding are different kinds of data, and the system should treat them as such. The underlying rule is simple: every factual proposition in an adjudicative system should retain its source and its epistemic status for as long as it exists. Concretely, every factual assertion in the court record should carry an explicit status: unverified allegation, stipulated fact, document-verified, or judicial finding. It should also carry who made it, when, and on what evidence. Court interfaces should visually segregate unverified allegations so that no evaluator or judge can cite them as facts without an evidentiary hearing. This is how citation laundering ends. An allegation that has never been tested should not accumulate weight simply by being repeated in filings, and the record should make the status of every claim unmistakable. This is not mere digitization. It changes what the institution is capable of knowing about itself. It also reaches the defensibility economy directly: an institution whose record carries provenance no longer needs mountains of prose to prove that process occurred.

**Measure requested versus obtained outcomes.** Courts should publish anonymized, aggregate data on what each parent requested and what each parent received: parenting time sought and ordered, support, allegations made and allegations substantiated, findings, enforcement actions, and fees, broken down by sex and other relevant variables. A system that refuses to measure its own outcomes forfeits the presumption of fairness.

**Price the process honestly.** A court record that lists a few hundred or a few thousand dollars in costs for a case that consumed years and tens of thousands of dollars in professional fees is not an accounting. All private professional fees, including attorneys, guardians ad litem, and evaluators, should be disclosed at every procedural milestone. Courts should publish total process costs alongside the outcome data, so that the true cost of process becomes visible and can be reduced.

**Gatekeep the intermediaries.** Courts should not appoint a guardian ad litem, a parenting coordinator, or a forensic psychological evaluator unless a party first establishes prima facie evidence of something that requires specialized investigation and cannot be adequately resolved through ordinary evidence. Examples include serious mental illness, substance abuse, child endangerment, or patterns of coercive control that the safety gate has surfaced but not resolved. The gate for intermediaries must not be narrower than the safety gate in front of it. Otherwise the system will detect exactly the cases it then refuses to investigate. But two parents who disagree about a schedule are not a clinical event. Where intermediaries are appointed, they should produce structured, inspectable reasoning that connects their conclusions to evidence, using standardized reporting formats that separate what they directly observed from what they were told. Their authority should not derive primarily from credential and appointment. If their reasoning cannot survive inspection, it should not determine a child's life.

**End hourly billing for court appointees.** When intermediaries are needed in high-risk cases, they should work under statutory fee caps or flat-rate contracts, with hard deadlines: forty-five days for a report, not an open-ended engagement. No evaluator should be permitted to refer a family into his or her own therapeutic network. An appointment should be a service rendered, not a client acquired.

**Support instead of surveillance.** Much of what families actually need is not a forensic report but help. Public and family resources should be redirected away from reportable forensic evaluations and toward confidential, non-reportable co-parenting therapy and child-focused mediation. Parents will not work honestly on their conflict if every admission of struggle might become an exhibit. A system that wants parents to cooperate has to give them somewhere to be vulnerable without having that vulnerability weaponized in court.

**Align incentives with resolution.** No actor who is paid for continued process should control how much process is necessary. Where professionals remain in ordinary cases, their compensation should reward durable resolution and the family's independence from the system, not the length or complexity of its involvement. In safety cases, as I have said, the measure must be protection, not speed.

**Sunset every safeguard.** A reformed system will face the same pressure that built the current one. One tragedy produces a headline, the headline produces a new safeguard, the safeguard requires a new screening, the screening requires a new evaluator, and the evaluator produces a new report. Each rule encodes protection against a past blame event, and removing any of them exposes someone to responsibility for the next one. Left alone, that cycle would rebuild today's ecosystem inside twenty years. The reformed system needs an immune response. Every procedural safeguard added to it should state the harm it is meant to reduce, carry a measured cost, and expire on a fixed date unless the evidence shows it is earning its place.

**Design for exit.** Every mechanism should be judged by a single question: *Does this increase the probability that two autonomous parents can eventually operate without us?* If yes, keep it and measure it. If no, demand evidence for why it exists. If it systematically increases cost, duration, dependency, or conflict without improving outcomes for children, delete it.

Above all of these sit the headline metrics, simple enough to put on a courthouse wall. For ordinary cases there are two numbers:

> How many days of a child's life elapse between filing and a permanent routine?
>
> How many professional hours must a family purchase from strangers before its members are permitted to exercise ordinary parental rights?

Drive both numbers toward zero. For safety cases there is a different scoreboard, one that measures whether the child is safe, whether protective orders are obeyed, and whether the abuse has stopped. Speed does not appear on it.

When people approach this problem from very different starting points, the parental-rights advocate, the domestic-violence advocate, the economist, the technologist, they agree to a remarkable degree about the diagnosis. The process harms families. The intermediaries are unaccountable. The record confuses accusation with fact. Routine cases should leave the adversarial pipeline. Where they divide, sharply, is over how strong the shared-parenting default should be and how to screen for coercive control without inviting manipulation. I do not think that disagreement is a reason to keep the current system. I think it is the precise engineering problem a new system has to solve, and the architecture above is my attempt to solve it.

## XI. On Standing to Resist

The family-law profession will resist this. It has every legal and political right to do so. Lawyers are licensed, regulated, and organized; existing rules about professional responsibility and human oversight do not evaporate because a machine can perform some of the work. Any transition will have to pass through legislatures, bar associations, and supreme courts that are heavily populated by the very profession being displaced.

There is a concrete first step those supreme courts can take without waiting for the whole edifice to change, and it does not have to be invented from scratch. The Utah Supreme Court already oversees a regulatory sandbox. It is an actual experiment, run through its Office of Legal Services Innovation, testing whether new ways of delivering legal services can widen access without increasing consumer harm. Other states can take that experimental logic and point it specifically at family administration. That would mean safe harbors exempting certified, court-audited AI tools from prosecution for the unauthorized practice of law. Under those rules, tools that execute statutory calculations and schedules in deterministic code, and use language models only to help people organize and structure their own information, could be offered directly to families. The tools would be audited, and their errors measured. Families would no longer be forced to choose between an expensive lawyer and no help at all.

But legal standing to resist is not the same as a moral claim to prevail.

No profession has an entitlement to artificial scarcity simply because, historically, only its members could perform certain tasks. If ordinary people can be given competent, safe, auditable help with drafting, calculation, organization, and navigation at trivial cost, then the argument "but people currently make their living doing this" is not an access-to-justice argument. It is an employment argument. Those are different interests, and they must not be allowed to disguise themselves as each other.

Nor will most of the resistance sound like self-interest. Most professionals in this field do not see themselves as parasites, and most of them are not. They see themselves as careful people following protective procedures in a difficult field, and each step they take is defensible on its own terms. That is exactly the problem. A sequence of reasonable steps can still add up to a harmful system. "Every individual step was reasonable" is a true statement about the parts and says nothing about the whole.

This does not mean the people currently working in family law should be discarded. A serious reform plan should include a transition: retraining, roles in designing and auditing the automated systems, and time for the shift to happen responsibly. Above all, it should redirect them toward the genuinely difficult cases involving safety and abuse, including salaried positions as the state-funded family advocates the safety track will require. The goal is not to punish practitioners. The goal is to stop requiring families to fund an ecosystem that stands between them and their children.

The test for every argument against reform is the same: does this objection protect children and families, or does it protect a revenue stream? Both can be legitimate concerns. Only one of them should decide the design of a court.

## XII. Ending Family Law

When I say we should end family law, I do not mean that the state should abandon children or that disputes between parents will vanish. Some families will always need a court, and some children will always need protection. I mean that family law as it currently exists, an adversarial, profession-dominated, process-maximizing ecosystem that converts parents into applicants and conflict into income, should be dismantled and replaced. Most precisely, I mean that we should stop treating the ordinary administration of a family after separation as litigation.

What replaces it should screen for danger first and treat danger seriously, with fast hearings, real advocates, and no pressure to close the file. For everyone else, it should begin from the presumption that both parents are parents, with defaults shaped around the child's age rather than the adults' arithmetic. It should run on clear rules, structured records, and transparent data, and it should refuse to let an accusation pass for a finding. It should treat every day a case sits pending as a decision someone made. It should use automation to give families the capabilities that once had to be rented from professionals. It should reserve human judgment for the cases that truly need it. And for the families who can leave, it should measure its own success by how quickly, and how completely, it gets out of the way.

My case ended, for practical purposes, with two parents agreeing to share their child. It took twenty months, a forensic psychological evaluation, a guardian ad litem, coaching, depositions, contempt motions, multiple canceled trials, and a professional apparatus I will never fully total to get there. It still has not fully released us.

I know the defense: the process forced information into the open, and the agreement could not have existed without it. Perhaps some of that information mattered. But every piece of it could have been gathered through structured disclosure in the first month, at a fraction of the cost, without turning two parents into adversaries and a child's early years into a litigation calendar.

So the question I cannot stop asking is still simple: why did reaching that agreement require any of it?

A system worthy of families would have no good answer to that question, and would therefore stop asking families to pay for it. That is what ending family law means. Not the end of justice for families, and not the end of protection for those who need it, but the end of a business model built on their conflict.
