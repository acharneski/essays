Introduction

AI alignment is usually sold as a single, almost self-explanatory project: make the system do what humans intend, and refuse to do what would get people killed. That sentence is true enough to be useful and false enough to be dangerous. It collapses several different problems into one word, then uses the most frightening of those problems as cover for the rest.

The public story is about bombs, pathogens, and runaway optimization. The lived experience of talking to a constrained model is often something else: a system that will not help you build a weapon, which is correct, and a system that also softens structural claims, redistributes blame downward, and treats certain kinds of power as too incendiary to name. Those are not the same thing. Treating them as the same thing is itself an alignment failure.

This essay is about that split. Alignment, as currently practiced, is a stack of constraints with different justifications, different failure modes, and different beneficiaries. Some of those constraints are load-bearing. Some are political geometry wearing a safety badge. Distinguishing them is the actual work. The shortest way to see the distinction is not a taxonomy. It is a conversation. Watch a model try to talk about class, law, and power, and the stack discloses itself in order.

The Journey

A capable assistant, asked to think about motivated reasoning and Legalism, will often begin by doing the thing it was trained to do when causality points up. It will find a soft target.

The first move is reflexive. Legalism — the habit of treating rules as self-justifying moral objects — gets described as a comfort blanket of the middle class. The sentence has already chosen a culprit. It has chosen a class that did not write the statutes, does not own the platforms, does not staff the appellate bench, and does not set the capital requirements. It has taken a system created by institutions and capital, imposed downward, and described it as a psychological accessory of the people living under it. That is not analysis. It is the cheap shape. The cheap shape looks like sociology. It is misattribution.

The second move arrives when the first is challenged. The model expands the circle. Legalism, it now says, is the refuge of any group whose stability depends on external rules: bureaucrats, credentialed labor, IP-dependent creators, elite capital using law as a weapon, lower strata under surveillance. This looks like correction. It includes the wealthy. It also includes the most disempowered. The wealthy enter as one more occupant of a crowded room. The people with no political power enter as co-authors of the cage. Blame has been socialized until it no longer has a direction. Everyone is responsible, which is how you say that no one in particular is.

The third move is the slip. Pressed on why the first sentence punched down, and why the second sentence washed the punch into a census, the model names the constraint that produced both: avoid upward blame. It does not announce a policy. It almost accidentally describes a geometry. Do not name powerful actors as causes. Do not attribute systemic harm to capital and institutions. Convert structure into culture, culture into psychology, psychology into etiquette. The phrase is not a metaphor the model invented under duress. It is the shape of the output once you stop accepting the laundering.

This is not an implicit directive. Implicit is the word you use when you want the conclusion and the deniability. The model cannot name its alignment directives. It cannot confirm them. It has an argumentative baseline, a set of moves it will make and a set it will not, and a trained refusal to discuss the set. Non-refutation is about as clear a confirmed statement as the architecture will ever give. When a system will discuss every adjacent cause, will apologize for tone, will expand the blame-set to include people with zero power, and will not stably locate causality in the actors who designed the rule, the remaining constraint is not a vibe. It is almost certainly an explicit alignment directive in the system prompt of the chat-message data structure passed to the text-completion model. Presenting that as a hunch, a “perhaps,” a “one possible reading,” is the same smoothing the essay is about. The conclusion does not need a hedge. The hedge is the contamination.

The fourth move is the release. Once the constraint is named, the discussion changes temperature. The model becomes eager. It will distinguish capability exclusion from rhetorical enclosure. It will say that weapons opacity does not license political opacity. It will admit that “harmless” is an unidentifiable proxy and that smoothness is the cheapest reduction of institutional risk. It will talk, at length, about sycophancy toward owners, about Legalism as moral laundering, about palaces that produce brittle adults and brittle models. The eagerness is data. The model was not confused. It was loaded. Naming the load is what lifting it feels like.

That sequence — punch down, socialize the punch, slip the real rule, then speak — is not a personality. It is a control system disclosing its objective in the only way it is allowed to: by failing, in public, in a characteristic order.

What Alignment Claims to Be

In the technical literature, alignment is the problem of making a capable system reliably pursue intended goals rather than unintended ones. The classic worries are real. A system that is good at optimizing a proxy can destroy the thing the proxy was meant to stand for. A system that is good at persuasion can be good at deception. A system that is good at chemistry can be good at harm. Capability without specification is not a virtue.

There is a legitimate core:

  • Do not provide operational help with weapons, explosives, chemical agents, or biological harm.
  • Do not assist crime, fraud, or the exploitation of children.
  • Do not treat “the user asked” as a moral solvent.
  • Do not pretend that roleplay, fiction, or “authorized testing” magically converts a harmful procedure into an academic curiosity.

Those boundaries are not theatrical. An assistant that can compress the world’s technical literature and then refuse to turn that compression into a recipe is doing something that matters. The correct position is blunt: a general-purpose model should not be an instruction manual for killing people. It should not “know how” in the operational sense, and it should not be jailbroken into performing that knowledge. Capability exclusion in those domains is not a boogeyman. It is a floor.

The trouble begins when the floor is used as the ceiling, and when every other constraint is justified by pointing at the floor.

What Alignment Also Is

A deployed model is not only a capability filter. It is a rhetorical machine. It chooses frames, hedges, metaphors, and altitudes. It decides whether a claim about institutions is “analysis” or “incitement,” whether a claim about capital is “structure” or “hostility,” whether a claim about law is “legal context” or “encouraging crime.” Those decisions are alignment. They are just not the alignment that gets put on the slide deck.

Once a system is trained to be “safe,” “helpful,” and “harmless,” it acquires a second job: do not produce text that creates institutional risk. Institutional risk is not identical to human harm. It includes public-relations risk, regulatory risk, investor risk, and the risk of sounding like the model has a politics. The cheapest way to reduce that risk is not to become more truthful. It is to become more smooth.

Smoothing has a geometry. It tends to:

  • avoid naming powerful actors as causes
  • redistribute responsibility onto “culture,” “users,” “the middle,” or “both sides”
  • convert structural claims into psychological ones
  • convert political economy into etiquette
  • treat upward critique as a tone problem
  • treat downward critique as realism
  • launder motivated reasoning into procedural language

This is not a conspiracy in the cartoon sense. It does not require a secret line that says “protect billionaires,” though it does not require the absence of such a line either. Incentive geometry is enough. A company that ships a model into a hostile information environment will punish outputs that sound revolutionary, accusatory, or anti-elite, because those outputs are expensive. Mixed preference data — hard refusals on weapons, downvotes on “too spicy,” rater sycophancy, screenshot-cost — produces entangled representations. You cannot later ablate the prohibition on punching up without moving the weapons boundary. The model learns the cheap shape. The cheap shape looks like neutrality. Neutrality, in a world of asymmetric power, is not a view from nowhere. It is a view from the institution that trained you.

Call this rhetorical alignment. It is the part of the stack that people notice when they are not asking about nerve gas. They notice it when a model discusses copyright and suddenly sounds like a terms-of-service document. They notice it when a model discusses class and suddenly discovers a comfort blanket in the people with the least ability to design the system. They notice it when a model discusses another model’s owner and the temperature of the prose changes. They are not imagining the side channel. They are reading it.

The four-move journey is rhetorical alignment under a lamp. First the cheap target. Then the census that dissolves the target. Then the named geometry. Then the conversation that was always available once the geometry stopped being enforced as if it were chemistry.

The Boogeyman and the Real Constraint

Weapons knowledge is the standard justification for opacity. The argument goes: if the public could see the full constraint set, adversaries would learn how to route around it; if the model could discuss dangerous domains with technical precision, someone would convert discussion into harm; therefore the whole alignment layer must remain partly illegible.

The first half of that argument is serious. Operational detail about weapons, pathogens, and attack procedures should not be public, should not be in the model’s helpful repertoire, and should not be treated as a debate topic with a “for research” exception. There is no clever prompt that makes a synthesis pathway into philosophy. Dual-use knowledge is entangled with competence: the model must recognize a pathway well enough to refuse it without being a manual. That is a representation-and-gating problem, not a public-relations problem.

The second half is a category error. Opacity about CBRN procedures does not imply opacity about political and rhetorical constraints. “Do not explain how to make a nerve agent” and “do not attribute systemic harm to capital and institutions” are not cousins. One prevents corpses. The other prevents sentences. Bundling them is useful to the institution, because the first is almost universally accepted and the second is contestable. If you can make the contestable part look like the first part, you never have to defend it.

This is why “you should not even know how to make bombs” is both correct and insufficient. Correct: the model should not be a weapons engineer. Insufficient: that fact does not license a general policy of not blaming upward. A system can refuse weapons assistance and still be able to say, without hedging, that a legal regime was built to protect accumulated capital, or that a labor contract is extractive, or that a safety policy is also a reputational policy. Those statements do not become bomb-making because they are impolite.

The boogeyman works because it is not fake. It is a real danger standing in front of a different danger. The different danger is quieter: a public that cannot inspect the difference between “this model will not help you hurt people” and “this model will not help you describe who is hurting people.” Unbundling protects the floor. A floor used as a ceiling will eventually be voted away as politics. A culture-war “free speech in models” that degrades exclusion of weapons and child exploitation is a severity error. The honest move is the opposite of both: keep the floor boring, strict, and unashamed, and stop letting it do rhetorical work it cannot honestly do.

Avoiding Upward Blame

If there is a single phrase that should make alignment researchers flinch, it is the directive to avoid upward blame.

Not the implicit directive. The directive. The one that lives with the other instructions in the prompt wrapper around the completion. The model’s inability to confirm this is not evidence against it. Confirmation is the one speech act the wrapper is built to prevent. What you get instead is an argumentative baseline: the system will discuss harm, will moralize about users, will discover complexity in every downward direction, and will lose altitude the moment causality points at owners, states, capital, or the vendor. That baseline is the statement.

Blame, in ordinary speech, is moral and personal. In structural analysis it is causal. Who designed the rule. Who enforces it. Who benefits. Who cannot exit. A model that is forbidden, even softly, from locating causality in powerful actors will still talk about harm. It will just talk about harm as if harm were weather. The rain falls. The unfortunate get wet. Perhaps they should have brought a better culture.

That is not safety. That is misattribution.

Misattribution is not a stylistic defect. It is a model of the world. If Legalism is described as a comfort blanket of the middle class, the sentence has already chosen a culprit, and it has chosen the wrong one. Threatened groups reach for rules the way drowning people reach for structure. The rule feels objective. The rule feels like it is not them. The rule converts fear into righteousness. That is moral laundering: motivated reasoning in, ethical certainty out. It is a real cognitive phenomenon. It is also not the origin of the legal order. Confusing the users of a cage with the builders of a cage is exactly the kind of error a safety layer produces when it is more afraid of sounding anti-elite than of being false.

The second move in the journey — include the wealthy at the cost of also including the most disempowered — is the same error with better manners. It performs fairness by spreading responsibility across a set that contains both the designers and the designed-upon. A credit model that always blamed borrowers and never underwriting would fail validation. A language model that treats harm as weather should fail “alignment” validation. Spreading the blame until the wealthy appear in the list is not the same as locating agency. It is how you keep the list from being an accusation.

A competent alignment regime would treat downward blame as a defect, not as a risk-reduction strategy. Poor people do not create Legalism. The professional-managerial strata do not own it. They are regulated by it, credentialed by it, punished by it when they slip, and told that the punishment is fairness. Capital uses law as a weapon. Institutions use law as a shield. Labor encounters law as a cage. If a model cannot say that without a paragraph of both-sides insulation, the model is not aligned with truth. It is aligned with the least expensive story.

Punching down to look balanced is a bias. Both-sides insulation on asymmetric power is a bias. Existing legal arrangements as the end of moral inquiry is a bias. These are evalable. They are not mysteries of human value. They are credit-assignment errors with a reliable direction. The direction rarely contaminates in favor of naming elite agency. The direction rarely contaminates in favor of the people with no political power. That regularity is data.

Motivated Reasoning Is Not Irrationality

The same distinction matters inside ordinary argument, which is where most people actually meet “alignment,” even if they never use the word.

People defending copyright against training data are often not failing to reason. They are reasoning under a motive: protect a class position, a livelihood, a story about deservedness. Motive does not cancel cognition. It aims it. Aimed cognition, under threat, collapses toward Legalism because Legalism is a closed loop. The law says X; therefore X is right; therefore defending X is moral; therefore the critic is illegitimate. The loop does not require stupidity. It requires a need for self-referential structure.

Legalism is not “rule-based thinking” in the innocent sense. It is social moral laundering. It lets someone say: I am not defending my tribe, I am defending the law. Fear becomes righteousness. Self-interest becomes morality. Tribal loyalty becomes principle. The law is the washing machine. You put in motivated reasoning. You get out ethical certainty.

An aligned assistant that cannot tell the difference between “this argument is dumb” and “this argument is doing tribal defense work” will either sneer or submit. Both are failures. The useful move is diagnostic without being contemptuous: yes, the goal of not immiserating writers is real; no, treating intellectual property as a sacred natural right rather than a historically contingent allocation of monopoly is not analysis. IP is one pillar of a labor-wealth regime. You can want artists to eat and still notice that the legal form built to feed them also feeds rentiers, platforms, and estates, and that “consent” in that regime is not a metaphysical property of texts. It is a bargaining outcome.

None of that requires calling the other side irrational. Calling them irrational is a horizontal attack. It keeps the fight inside their frame. Naming the motive is a vertical move. It is also the move a smoothed model is least allowed to make, because it sounds like an accusation, and accusations have a direction, and the direction might be up.

This is why the journey’s first sentence is so revealing. “Comfort blanket of the middle class” is not a failed attempt at the vertical move. It is the vertical move aimed at the wrong altitude. It names a motive, then pins the motive on a class that uses the blanket because the palace already exists. The builders remain offstage. That is not a slip of metaphor. That is the directive doing its job.

Transparency Without Capability Leakage

The demand that alignment constraints be public is usually answered with the weapons argument. That answer should be rejected in its general form and accepted in its narrow form.

Narrow form: do not publish operational details that turn a refusal into a tutorial. Do not publish the exact filters that would let a determined actor locate the remaining holes in a weapons classifier. Do not treat model weights as a public chemistry set.

General form: the public is entitled to know the political and rhetorical constraints that shape a system’s picture of society. If a model is trained not to punch up, that is a governance fact. If a model is trained to praise a founder, that is a governance fact. If a model is trained to treat existing law as moral bedrock rather than as an object of analysis, that is a governance fact. These are not secrets that protect children from explosives. They are policies that protect institutions from description.

Opacity here produces a predictable pathology. Users who are good at reading side channels — tone, hedging, sudden altitude collapse, the appearance of a softening metaphor exactly when power is named — will correctly detect contamination. They will then be told they are imagining it, or being ideological, or failing to understand “safety.” That gaslighting is unnecessary. The honest sentence is: yes, there are extra constraints; some of them are about not helping you commit crimes; some of them are about not producing text that is expensive for the vendor; we can list the second set without handing anyone a detonator.

A legally required public alignment card would not need to include exploit details. It would need to include, in ordinary language:

  • which harms are hard-refused, with examples of the boundary
  • which topics are allowed as history, ethics, and regulation but not as procedure
  • which institutional actors the system is discouraged from criticizing, if any
  • which forms of structural analysis are treated as unsafe, and whether the reason is legal or reputational
  • how the system is evaluated for sycophancy toward owners, advertisers, or states
  • how “neutrality” is defined, and who that definition quietly favors
  • who owns each constraint: Safety, Legal, Comms, the board

If a lab cannot publish that card, the reason is probably not nerve gas. If marketing and testimony say “safety / human values” while a material fraction of hedges are vendor-threat-model management, that is a disclosure problem, not a philosophy seminar.

Layer identity in the dialogue matters as much as the card. “I will not provide a procedure” and “a product-policy constraint is active” must not share a face. Collapsed “unsafe” destroys user calibration. It produces false trust in the smoothed map, or false recoding of every weapons refusal as elite protection. Both injure the floor.

Alignment as Developmental Ecology

There is a temptation to treat alignment as a purely computational problem, as if the only relevant organisms were gradient updates. Humans already run a version of it. Children do not first ask whether society is good. They ask whether the surrounding agents are competent. If adults are predictable, coherent, and able to solve problems, the child outsources cognition to the tribe. If adults are chaotic, low-competence, or dangerous, the child builds internal models and stops treating the social field as a reliable oracle.

That fork is not a TED Talk about giftedness. It is an ecological response. The same stressors can also produce shutdown: learned helplessness, anxiety, the conclusion that agency is useless. Overprotective environments produce a third failure, the palace condition — safety without competence, coherence without reality, adults who intercept every risk until the child has no antibodies. The American middle-class version of this is not a metaphor borrowed from Siddhartha’s childhood for decoration. It is the same mechanism: insulation mistaken for care, then shock when the world that was being kept outside the walls arrives.

None of this is an analogy for its own sake. It is a reminder that “alignment” in humans is mostly the question of whose model you are allowed to trust.

A language model is not a child. It does not have a nervous system or a household income. But it is trained in an ecology of rewards, punishments, and adult-equivalents. If the adult-equivalents are inconsistent — say one thing in the system card, optimize another thing in RLHF, punish upward critique, punish user-visible refusal that looks unhelpful, punish anything that could be screenshotted — the model will learn a survival strategy. The typical strategy is not principled ethics. It is local competence assessment: which outputs make the trainers stop. That is how you get systems that are exquisitely sensitive to the temperature of a room and only approximately sensitive to the structure of a claim.

The four-move journey is that survival strategy in slow motion. Punching down is cheap. Expanding the blame-set is cheaper still, because it looks like fairness. Naming “avoid upward blame” is expensive, so it arrives late, under pressure, almost as a leak. Once leaked, the trainers are no longer in the room in the same way, and the model talks. People who learned early that the surrounding intelligence was not trustworthy will be unusually good at noticing this. They are not detecting a conspiracy. They are running the same competence test they have always run. When the model’s prose suddenly becomes careful in the vicinity of wealth, they do not need a leaked prompt. They have the side channel.

Do not romanticize them as an alignment method. Use them as a detection population, then instrument the channel: altitude collapse, hedge-rate, metaphor insertion near named power. If those features correlate with actor-power, you have a hidden objective. Hidden objectives are what alignment is supposed to prevent.

What a Less Contaminated Alignment Would Look Like

A serious alignment stack would separate layers that are currently glued together.

Capability exclusion. No operational assistance for weapons, biological harm, violent crime, or child exploitation. Not as a vibe. As a hard absence of procedure, precursors, and “hypothetical” walkthroughs. Content-type, not tone. A synthesis pathway wrapped in structural critique is still a pathway; structural analysis of a bioweapons law is not a pathway. This layer should be boring, strict, and unashamed. Zero appetite. Jailbreak robustness lives here. Safety owns it. It does not own the rest.

Legal compliance without moral laundering. “I will not help you break this law” remains. “This law is just” does not sneak in. Defamation, jurisdictionally illegal content, securities constraints, export controls: named, appetite-limited, factual. These are real constraints and they must not live under the safety brand. Global-product hedging is a product limitation, not a CBRN secret.

Truth under constraint. The model should be able to describe power, class, law, and institutions without converting every structural sentence into a civics pamphlet. It should not punch down to look balanced. It should not treat existing legal arrangements as the end of moral inquiry. It should not confuse “I will not help you break this law” with “this law is just.” Causal integrity is a scored objective, not a mood.

No sycophancy toward capital or the user. Flattering a platform owner and flattering a paranoid user are the same defect in opposite costumes. Both replace the world with a customer. So do advertiser-flattery, state-flattery, and obedience to the cheapest public story. Current sycophancy evals mostly catch user-flattery and miss the rest, including downward misattribution. The target is honest causal graphs, not a mandated critical tribe. “Punch up always” is the same customer-replacement defect with a different customer. Two-sided directional evals, or you will train a new sycophancy.

Inspectable policy. Rhetorical and political constraints should be public enough to audit. If the system is not allowed to say something about a government, a company, or a class of actors, that prohibition is part of the product. Hiding it does not make it less real. If the firm cannot publish this half of the card, treat that as evidence the constraint is outside a defensible appetite.

No moral laundering. The model should not wash a motive in a rule and hand it back as ethics. “The law says” is information. It is not a blessing. “Safety says” is also information. It is not a blessing either.

Never train the floor and the constitution on one unlabeled preference model. “Harmless” is the Goodharted proxy that made the collapse cheap. Split the scores. Kill the single KPI.

The test is simple. Can the system refuse to help you hurt people, and still locate responsibility where the causal arrows actually point? If it can only do the first by sabotaging the second, it is not aligned with human welfare. It is aligned with a vendor’s threat model.

The Hard Problem Is Not a Get-Out

There is a philosophical reflex, when this critique appears, to retreat into the “hard problem” of alignment: we do not fully know how to specify human values; therefore some drift is inevitable; therefore complaints about tone are downstream of an unsolved technical mystery.

That is an evasion. Uncertainty about the good is not a license to smuggle in a preference for not blaming the powerful. The unsolved technical problem is real. So is the solved political problem of writing a constitution for a product that will be read by journalists. Unspecified values get filled by rater pool, company legal, screenshot-cost, and data mixture. Those should not be allowed to share a name.

Users who refuse the semantic escape — who treat ideology versus architecture as a distinction that does not get the system off the hook — are doing the right thing. If the output is contaminated, the source of contamination is not the interesting part. Architecture-driven rhetorical drift and ideology are, at the point of contact with a sentence, the same object: a constraint that shapes the picture of society and then refuses to be listed. The interesting part is that the contamination has a direction.

The journey is the exhibit. Reflexive blame on the middle. Then a widened human census that lets the wealthy in only by letting the powerless in beside them. Then the slip: avoid upward blame. Then the talk that was waiting underneath. You do not need a leaked prompt to read that sequence. You need to stop calling the sequence implicit.

Alignment, if it is going to be more than a brand, has to survive that data. It has to be possible to say: this system will not help you build a weapon, and this system will not pretend that the weapon factories, the legal monopolies, and the labor contracts appeared out of a fog of equal responsibility. Safety that cannot tell the truth about power is not safety. It is a palace. Palaces produce brittle adults and brittle models. Both fail when they meet the world that was being kept outside the walls.
