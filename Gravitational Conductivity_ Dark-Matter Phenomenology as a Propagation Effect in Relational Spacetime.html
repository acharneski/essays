Abstract

The flat rotation curves of galaxies imply an effective gravitational acceleration that falls off roughly as 1/r rather than the Newtonian 1/r2. The standard response is to add mass: an unseen halo of cold dark matter whose density distribution produces the required field. This paper sets out a different possibility. The anomaly may lie in how gravitational influence propagates, not in how much matter sources it. Specifically, we propose that spacetime is an emergent relational structure, a graph of relations among physical constituents. In such a picture, regions of extreme sparsity such as large cosmic voids are poor "conductors" of gravitational influence. Around a galaxy, a dense and well-connected island set inside a sparse environment, the field therefore relaxes into a geometry that is partly flattened or channeled. Within the galactic system this produces an effective 1/r behavior. Outside it, the field stays essentially three-dimensional, with at most slight anisotropies.

We develop the analogy into three candidate formalisms: scalar conductivity, tensor conductivity, and a graph-Laplacian model. We then identify where the hypothesis makes observational contact. Weak gravitational lensing is the most promising testbed, because lensing directly measures the geometry of propagation and current and upcoming surveys (DES, KiDS, HSC, Euclid, LSST/Rubin, Roman) are producing it in very large quantities. We review related work in modified gravity, emergent gravity, and quantum-gravity models of discrete or relational spacetime. We conclude that the specific combination proposed here does not appear in the literature: gravitational propagation efficiency set by relational connectivity, active only in sufficiently sparse regions, and producing dark-matter-like phenomenology. We also set out plainly the serious obstacles any such model must overcome. The largest are the cosmic microwave background, cluster lensing (the Bullet Cluster), and weak-lensing signals that extend far beyond galactic disks.

1. The Problem, Restated as a Geometry Problem

The observational core of the dark matter problem in galaxies is simple. For a star on a circular orbit at radius r,

v2 / r = g(r).

If the rotation speed v is constant with radius, as it is over large ranges in most spiral galaxies, then

g(r) ∝ 1 / r.

Newtonian gravity from a centrally concentrated mass gives g ∝ 1/r2. The standard inference is that the enclosed mass must grow linearly, M(<r) ∝ r, which requires a large, extended, non-luminous mass component.

A 1/r field can arise in another way, and it has nothing to do with extra mass. It is the natural falloff of a field confined to two spatial dimensions. Gauss's law in d spatial dimensions gives a field that falls as r−(d−1). A line charge in electrostatics, or a point source in a thin conducting sheet, produces exactly the 1/r behavior that galaxies display. So the anomaly can be read in two ways:

  1. A mass problem: the field is three-dimensional and there is more source than we see.
  2. A geometry problem: the source is roughly what we see, but the field does not spread isotropically through three dimensions at these scales.

This paper explores the second reading. It is not the MOND reading either. MOND modifies the force law or inertia as a function of acceleration. Here we ask whether the medium of propagation, spacetime itself, has structure-dependent transmission properties.

2. The Core Hypothesis

Hypothesis. Spacetime is an emergent relational structure. Its capacity to transmit gravitational influence depends on its local relational connectivity. In sufficiently large and sufficiently empty regions, chiefly intergalactic voids, this capacity is degraded. A galaxy is a dense, highly connected island embedded in such a sparse environment. Its gravitational field therefore does not relax into the ideal isotropic three-dimensional configuration. It relaxes into one that is partly channeled along the directions of highest connectivity, which for a disk galaxy means approximately the disk plane. The result is an effective field falling more slowly than 1/r2 across the galactic system, which we observe as flat rotation curves and attribute to dark matter.

2.1 The conduction analogy

Picture electric current injected at a point into a thin conducting sheet that sits in a weakly conducting surrounding medium. If the surroundings conduct as well as the sheet, current spreads in three dimensions and the field falls as 1/r2. If the surroundings are nearly insulating, current is confined to the sheet and the field falls as 1/r. Between these limits, the falloff interpolates. The exponent depends on the conductivity contrast and on the distance from the source compared with the sheet's extent.

The hypothesis maps this onto gravity as follows:

Electrical analogy Gravitational counterpart
Point current source Concentrated baryonic mass (bulge, inner disk)
Conducting sheet High-connectivity region: stars, gas, dust of the galactic disk
Weakly conducting surroundings Low-connectivity region: sparse halo and surrounding void
Field falloff exponent Effective dimensionality of gravitational relaxation

The analogy has a subtlety that we develop in Section 5. In the most direct continuum version, "poor conduction" in the surroundings strengthens the field there rather than weakening it. This is the right sign for dark-matter phenomenology, but it is not obvious from the word "insulator."

2.2 What the hypothesis is not

  • Not a particle hypothesis. No new matter species is introduced.
  • Not MOND. The trigger is relational sparsity, not a universal acceleration scale. The two correlate in practice, because low baryonic density usually means low acceleration. They separate in specific, testable situations (Section 6.2).
  • Not a claim of literal dimensional collapse. Outside the galactic system the field remains fundamentally three-dimensional. Any departure from isotropy there is expected to be slight. This constraint shapes every observational prediction below.

3. Ontological Basis: Spacetime as a Relational Graph

The hypothesis gains its motivation from a broader view of spacetime. Spacetime is not a fundamental manifold. It is the coarse-grained description of a network of relations among physical constituents.

In this view:

  • Geometry is emergent. Distance, dimension, and curvature summarize the density, coherence, and arrangement of relational edges.
  • Gravity is relaxation. What we call the gravitational field is the equilibrium configuration of relational edge weights in response to the presence and distribution of matter.
  • Curvature is an equilibrium state, not a primitive property.
  • Maintaining long-range coherence has a cost. Where the network is dense and redundant, relational influence propagates cheaply and isotropically. Where it is sparse, coherent long-range relations become expensive. In earlier working notes this is framed as a coherence-cost field.

General relativity then appears as the high-connectivity limit of the relational dynamics. That is the regime in which the graph is dense enough that its continuum description is effectively perfect. Laboratory, solar-system, and stellar tests probe exactly this regime, where GR is extremely well confirmed.

Dark-matter phenomenology, on this reading, is a signature of the continuum approximation degrading in the sparsest regions of the universe. Gravity does not stop working there. The emergent geometry through which it acts is no longer perfectly isotropic, and the relaxed field takes a shape that, interpreted with GR and isotropic propagation, looks like extra mass.

3.1 An open question: what are the nodes?

The ontology needs an answer to an awkward question. What counts as a node of the relational graph? Voids are not truly empty. They contain diffuse ionized gas, cosmic neutrinos, CMB photons, and possibly vacuum degrees of freedom. If every quantum of every field is a node, voids may be much less "sparse" than galaxy counts suggest, and the effect may weaken. If only certain constituents count, for example massive or bound or decohered ones, the theory needs a principled reason for the selection. This question is foundational, and it is left open here. It also bears directly on the Bullet Cluster problem (Section 7.3).

4. Why the Effect Appears Only at Galactic Scales and Above

A viable version of the hypothesis must leave interstellar and solar-system gravity untouched. The argument is that interstellar space, though low in density, is not structurally sparse in the relevant sense:

  • Interstellar medium. Particle density is low, but the region is embedded in a richly connected system of stars, gas, radiation, and magnetic fields. Relational redundancy is high. Propagation is isotropic and the continuum limit holds.
  • Galactic outskirts. Connectivity falls as the baryonic surface density drops. The surrounding environment begins to matter.
  • Intergalactic voids. Density is extremely low across tens of megaparsecs. Connectivity is sparse, coherence cost is high, and propagation becomes measurably anisotropic or inefficient.

This gives a natural scale hierarchy. It also connects to a well-established empirical fact: dark-matter effects in galaxies set in where the baryonic acceleration, and with it the baryonic surface density, drops below a characteristic value (a0 ≈ 1.2 × 10−10 m s−2). This is the radial acceleration relation (McGaugh, Lelli & Schombert 2016). Any model in which the effect is driven by low baryonic connectivity will reproduce this correlation at least qualitatively, because in ordinary galaxies low connectivity and low acceleration go together.

4.1 Threshold or gradient?

The hypothesis can be built in two ways:

  • Threshold (phase transition). Below a critical connectivity the relational network undergoes something like a percolation transition. Coherent long-range relations become rare and the effect switches on fairly sharply. This resembles a screening mechanism, and it would make the model's behavior close to MOND's interpolation function, though driven by a different variable.
  • Continuous gradient. Propagation efficiency falls smoothly with connectivity. This behaves like a Poisson equation with a variable coefficient (Section 5.1).

The choice matters for the mathematics and for the observations. A threshold model predicts environment-dependent "switching" and a relatively sharp transition radius in individual galaxies. A gradient model predicts smooth, scale-free behavior. Random-graph theory suggests the two need not exclude each other. Sparse random geometric graphs are known to have a continuum limit that degrades gradually and then collapses at a percolation threshold.

5. Toward a Formalization

Three formal routes are sketched below, ordered from most tractable to most faithful to the ontology.

5.1 Scalar gravitational conductivity

Replace the Poisson equation with a variable-coefficient elliptic equation:

∇ · [ κ(x) ∇Φ ] = 4πGρ,

where κ(x) = κ(C(x)) is a conductivity determined by a local connectivity measure C. Two requirements follow: κ → 1 in high-connectivity regions, recovering Newton and GR, and κ < 1 in sparse regions.

For a spherically symmetric configuration, Gauss's law gives

g(r) = G M(<r) / ( κ(r) r2 ).

Two points follow.

  1. Poor conduction strengthens the field. The source's flux must still be carried. If the medium transmits it poorly, the gradient must steepen to compensate. In the electrostatic language, κ is a gravitational permittivity. A low-permittivity surrounding yields a stronger field, which is the sign required by observation.
  2. Flat rotation curves require κ(r) ∝ r−1 across the flat region, with M(<r) approximately constant. The conductivity must fall in inverse proportion to radius as connectivity thins out.

This structure is close to the Bekenstein–Milgrom AQUAL formulation of MOND, ∇ · [μ(|∇Φ|/a0)∇Φ] = 4πGρ. The essential difference is the argument of the coefficient. In AQUAL it is the field strength. Here it is the relational connectivity of the medium. That difference is what makes the two distinguishable (Section 6.2).

The scalar version does not, by itself, produce the planar relaxation of the original intuition. It does handle non-disk systems naturally: elliptical galaxies, dwarf spheroidals, and clusters all show dark-matter phenomenology without being planar.

5.2 Tensor (anisotropic) conductivity

To capture the planar-relaxation picture directly, promote κ to a tensor:

∇ · [ K(x) ∇Φ ] = 4πGρ,    K = κ∥ (I − n̂n̂) + κ⊥ n̂n̂,

where n̂ is the local normal to the dominant plane of connectivity, the disk normal in a spiral galaxy. When κ⊥ < κ∥, gravitational flux is preferentially channeled in-plane. This is the conducting-sheet picture.

Two cautions apply:

  • Partial confinement is enough. Full confinement of a galaxy's flux to a thin disk would overshoot badly. The field would be enhanced by roughly the ratio of radius to disk thickness. The observed amplitude implies modest channeling, not total channeling.
  • The anisotropy must fade outside the galactic system. Beyond the region dominated by the galaxy's own connectivity, K should return toward isotropy, with only slight residual anisotropy tied to the surrounding cosmic web.

The tensor version makes a distinctive prediction about vertical versus radial dynamics within disks (Section 6.3).

5.3 Graph-level formulation

The most faithful version works directly on the relational graph. Let nodes i carry mass mi and edges carry weights wij that encode relational coherence. Define the weighted graph Laplacian Lij = δij Σk wik − wij and solve

Σj Lij Φj = 4πG mi

on the graph. A useful mathematical fact anchors this approach. For dense random geometric graphs with suitable weight scaling, the graph Laplacian converges to the continuum Laplace–Beltrami operator. That is the GR limit. For sparse graphs the convergence degrades: the effective spectral dimension shifts, diffusion becomes anisotropic along well-connected backbones, and near the percolation threshold the network fragments. The scalar and tensor conductivities of Sections 5.1–5.2 would then be coarse-grained summaries of these sparse-graph corrections.

This version is hardest to compare with data. It is also the only one in which "conductivity" is derived rather than postulated. A natural research program is to simulate gravitational relaxation on random geometric graphs with galaxy-like density contrasts, and to measure the effective falloff exponent as a function of the surrounding sparsity.

5.4 A perturbative lensing form

For contact with observation it is enough to write

Φ = ΦGR + ε(C) Φconn,

where ε(C) is a small, connectivity-dependent amplitude and Φconn is the correction implied by whichever of the formalisms above is adopted. In weak lensing the convergence responds roughly linearly to such corrections. Small, environment-correlated perturbations should therefore be visible statistically when stacked over many systems, even if no single system shows them.

6. Observational Consequences

6.1 A demoted prediction: proximity to voids

An early formulation suggested that galaxies near large voids should show stronger dark-matter signatures. On reflection this is unreliable as a primary test. A galaxy's local environment (group or cluster membership, tidal history, merger record, gas content) dominates its dynamics. A scalar "distance to the nearest void" is unlikely to capture the relevant variable. The relevant quantity is the anisotropy of the relational connectivity surrounding the system, meaning its filament, sheet, and void geometry, not simple proximity to emptiness. Any void-proximity signal would be heavily contaminated by environmental covariates. It should be treated as a weak secondary check, if used at all.

6.2 Discriminating from MOND: where connectivity and acceleration decouple

In ordinary galaxies low connectivity and low acceleration coincide, so the model will mimic the radial acceleration relation. The two diverge where they come apart:

  • Wide binary stars. Widely separated binaries in the solar neighborhood experience internal accelerations below a0. Yet they are embedded deep inside the Milky Way's dense, well-connected relational network. MOND (modulated by the external field effect) predicts deviations from Newtonian orbits. The conductivity hypothesis predicts Newtonian behavior, because local connectivity is high. Current wide-binary analyses from Gaia data are contested, with claims both for and against MOND-like deviations. A robust Newtonian result would count against MOND while remaining consistent with this model.
  • Distant globular clusters and tidal debris. Low-acceleration systems at varying positions in the halo would test whether the effect follows acceleration or local connectivity.
  • Systems with unusual connectivity at fixed acceleration, such as gas-rich tidal dwarf galaxies or compact systems in sparse environments, give further leverage.

6.3 Vertical versus radial dynamics in disks

The tensor formulation predicts that the "missing mass" inferred from radial dynamics (rotation curves) should exceed the missing mass inferred from vertical dynamics (the restoring force perpendicular to the disk). Channeling enhances in-plane flux preferentially. A spherical cold-dark-matter halo predicts that the two agree on a single local dark-matter density. Measurements of local vertical dynamics with Gaia therefore give a direct test of the anisotropic version. A clean agreement between radially and vertically inferred densities would disfavor the tensor model and push toward the scalar one.

6.4 Gravitational lensing: the primary testbed

Lensing measures the geometry of gravitational propagation along the line of sight. It does not depend on the internal dynamics or baryonic physics of the lens. We are now entering an era of very large lensing datasets: cosmic shear maps, stacked void and filament lensing, galaxy–galaxy lensing, and time-delay systems. For these reasons lensing is the most promising arena for this hypothesis.

Because the field outside galactic systems is expected to remain essentially three-dimensional, all lensing effects predicted here are perturbative. They should show up as small, systematic biases in stacked or statistical measurements, not as dramatic deviations in single systems. With that understood, the expected signatures are:

(a) Filament lensing excess, modest. If filaments are preferred propagation channels, high-connectivity "waveguides," their stacked lensing signal should slightly exceed what their mass, estimated from galaxy content and gas, predicts under isotropic propagation. Stacked filament lensing has been detected, but whether any excess exists over ΛCDM expectations is not established. This is a measurement to make, not a known anomaly to explain.

(b) Void lensing deficit, subtle. Voids should under-lens relative to mass-only reconstructions. The shear profile at void edges should be slightly modified. Void lensing is already measured statistically, so a refined comparison against connectivity-informed predictions is feasible.

(c) Halo shapes aligned with web connectivity. Lensing-inferred "halos" should be slightly flattened or aligned along the directions of highest surrounding connectivity, beyond what baryonic shape and tidal alignment already explain. ΛCDM also predicts some halo–filament alignment from accretion history. The signal sought is a residual alignment at fixed mass and fixed accretion-based expectation.

(d) Galaxy–galaxy lensing at large radii: the sharpest test. If the effect truly confines itself to the galactic system and the field returns to 3D beyond it, then the lensing-inferred acceleration around isolated galaxies should revert toward Keplerian falloff beyond the connectivity boundary. That boundary is of order the extent of the baryonic (including HI) disk, perhaps somewhat beyond. This is a strong and falsifiable prediction. It is also a serious challenge, because weak-lensing analyses (for example KiDS, Brouwer et al. 2021) report that the radial acceleration relation continues to hundreds of kiloparsecs around isolated galaxies. If those results hold, the "galactic boundary" picture must either be revised so that the effective connectivity region extends far beyond the visible galaxy, or abandoned.

(e) Gravitational slip. In GR without anisotropic stress, the two metric potentials are equal (Φ = Ψ). Dynamics respond to one and lensing to their sum. A propagation-geometry correction need not act equally on both. If it does not, the model predicts a gravitational slip η = Φ/Ψ ≠ 1 that depends on connectivity. Combined probes such as the EG statistic, which compares lensing with galaxy velocity fields, can measure this directly. Of all the signatures, slip is the one most clearly distinguishing "propagation" from "extra mass," since cold dark matter produces no slip.

(f) Time-delay lensing. Arrival-time differences between images of lensed quasars and supernovae depend on the potential along each path. Systematic residuals from mass-model fits that correlate with line-of-sight connectivity would be a signature. They would need to be separated from the well-known mass-sheet degeneracy and line-of-sight structure effects.

(g) Strong-lensing critical curves. In clusters, slight asymmetries in critical curves and arc distributions aligned with incoming filaments could appear. Cluster mass models are flexible enough that this would be hard to isolate.

6.5 A concrete test pipeline

  1. Build a connectivity map. Use a galaxy redshift survey with a cosmic-web classifier (topological filament finders, tidal-tensor or Hessian-based classifiers) to assign each region a connectivity measure C and a dominant orientation.
  2. Fit standard models. Fit ΛCDM lens models to shear data in the usual way.
  3. Correlate residuals with connectivity. Test whether shear residuals correlate with C or its orientation at fixed mass and fixed environment density.
  4. Null hypothesis. Under ΛCDM, residuals are uncorrelated with connectivity once mass and known alignments are controlled. Under the conductivity hypothesis, they are not.
  5. Fit the conductivity model. Calibrate ε(C) on one survey region or redshift slice and test predictively on another.

This approach uses existing modified-gravity lensing machinery and does not require committing to the full graph ontology.

6.6 Secondary avenues: simulations and AGN jets

Large-scale structure simulations. Standard N-body codes assume perfectly isotropic, unit-conductivity gravity across the mesh. Particle-mesh solvers can easily be modified to solve a variable-coefficient elliptic equation, as in Section 5.1. A connectivity-dependent κ could then be tested for whether it changes filament thickness, continuity, and void profiles. In this picture gravity does not merely respond to the cosmic web. Through its anisotropic propagation during structure formation, it helps carve the web. Any such simulation must face the growth problem in Section 7.2: without dark matter, structure must still form fast enough.

AGN jets. Jets are highly collimated outflows crossing strongly anisotropic environments. One could speculate that jet orientation and lobe morphology encode connectivity structure. The coupling is indirect, though. Jets are governed by plasma physics and electromagnetic fields, and any sensitivity to gravitational connectivity would be buried under gas-density and magnetic effects. They are better seen as an exploratory curiosity than a primary test.

6.7 On particle searches

The hypothesis does not require a dark-matter particle. A confirmed direct or indirect detection of a particle with the right cosmological abundance would not necessarily refute the propagation effect. It would remove the need for it as the primary explanation. Conversely, continued null results in particle searches are consistent with the hypothesis but cannot confirm it.

7. Physical Plausibility and Principal Obstacles

7.1 What makes the idea plausible

  • Emergent spacetime is a mainstream research direction. Many quantum-gravity programs treat the continuum as an approximation to something discrete or relational (Section 8.5). If the continuum is emergent, its quality can vary with the underlying structure, and the sparsest regions are the natural place for it to degrade.
  • The required correction has a familiar mathematical form. Variable-coefficient Poisson equations underlie AQUAL and its relativistic descendants. Environment-dependent gravity is already standard in screening mechanisms such as chameleon fields.
  • Anisotropic stress and gravitational slip are recognized generic signatures of modified gravity. The hypothesis predicts them, so it can be tested with existing tools.
  • The radial acceleration relation follows naturally at the qualitative level from a connectivity trigger, because connectivity tracks baryonic density.

7.2 The cosmic microwave background and structure growth

This is the most serious obstacle. The acoustic peaks of the CMB, especially the relative height of the third peak, indicate a pressureless, non-baryonic, gravitating component already present at recombination (z ≈ 1100). At that epoch the universe was nearly uniform, with density contrasts of about one part in 105. There were no voids. A mechanism triggered by large-scale sparsity therefore has nothing to act on.

The model must either:

  • identify a different form of relational "under-connectivity" in the early universe, for example one related to causal horizons or the expansion itself; or
  • accept a hybrid role, in which some genuine dark-matter-like component exists cosmologically while galaxy-scale phenomenology is partly or wholly due to propagation effects.

The same problem affects the growth of structure. Without a dark-matter component, baryonic perturbations, held back by radiation pressure until recombination, do not grow fast enough to form today's cosmic web. Any connectivity-dependent enhancement must also supply sufficient growth after recombination. That is not obviously possible when the connectivity contrasts that drive it are themselves still forming.

7.3 The Bullet Cluster and cluster lensing

In merging clusters such as 1E 0657-56, the lensing mass is offset from the dominant baryonic component, the X-ray-emitting gas, and follows the galaxies instead. A conductivity model must explain why lensing tracks the collisionless galaxy population rather than the more massive gas.

One speculative route connects to the node question in Section 3.1. If relational connectivity is set by bound, coherent structures, galaxies with their stellar populations, rather than by raw mass, then high-conductivity regions would travel with the galaxies through a collision. Gravitational flux would be channeled accordingly. This is not yet a mechanism. It is a statement of what a mechanism would need to do. It is also a potential strength: few modified-gravity proposals have even a candidate story here. Separately, MOND-like theories leave a residual missing mass in clusters, and a conductivity model would need to show whether it does better.

7.4 Gravitational-wave propagation

The near-simultaneous arrival of gravitational waves and light from the neutron-star merger GW170817 limits any difference between their speeds to about one part in 1015. Many modified-gravity theories were eliminated by this result. The conductivity hypothesis concerns the quasi-static relaxation of the potential, not the propagation speed of radiative perturbations, so it is not automatically excluded. A covariant version must still be built so that tensor-mode speed is unaffected. That is a nontrivial constraint on how the correction enters the effective action.

7.5 Non-disk systems

Elliptical galaxies, dwarf spheroidals, and galaxy clusters show dark-matter phenomenology without any planar structure. The pure tensor (channeling) version cannot explain them. The scalar version can. This suggests the planar-relaxation intuition is best viewed as one manifestation, in disk systems, of a more general connectivity-dependent conductivity, rather than as the whole mechanism.

7.6 Covariance and conservation

The heuristic models of Section 5 are non-relativistic. A full theory must:

  • be generally covariant in the high-connectivity limit;
  • conserve energy and momentum in its effective description;
  • define the connectivity measure C in a way that is local or at least causal. A field that depends instantaneously on large-scale void geometry would be acausal.

The graph-level formulation is the natural setting for addressing these requirements, but this work has not been done.

7.7 Overall assessment

Dimension Assessment
Conceptual coherence High, given an emergent/relational spacetime ontology
Galaxy-scale phenomenology Promising; plausibly reproduces flat curves and RAR trends
Distinguishability from MOND Good, via wide binaries and connectivity–acceleration decoupling
Distinguishability from CDM Good, via gravitational slip and connectivity-correlated lensing residuals
Cosmological (CMB, growth) Major unresolved obstacle
Cluster lensing (Bullet Cluster) Major unresolved obstacle; one speculative route
Large-radius weak lensing Live tension with the "field returns to 3D" constraint
Technical completeness Low; no covariant action or derived conductivity yet

The idea does not contradict data outright at galactic scales. It sits in the same observational territory as actively studied modified-gravity theories, and it offers a distinct underlying mechanism along with distinct discriminating tests. Its greatest vulnerabilities are cosmological.

8. Related Work

A literature search surfaced no proposal identical to this one. Several neighboring lines of work share important elements. Items marked † were surfaced in an exploratory search and should be checked against primary sources before formal citation.

8.1 Modified gravity and phenomenological alternatives

  • MOND (Milgrom 1983) and its field formulation AQUAL (Bekenstein & Milgrom 1984). This is the closest mathematical relative of the scalar-conductivity form. It differs in triggering on acceleration rather than connectivity.
  • Relativistic MOND theories (TeVeS and successors, including recent models built to fit the CMB). These are relevant to how a conductivity model might address cosmology.
  • Scalar–Tensor–Vector Gravity (MOG) (Moffat). Analyses suggest it matches ΛCDM in isotropic regimes but departs in anisotropic observables.† This supports the view that anisotropy is where modified gravity and dark matter separate.
  • Covariant modified gravity models (f(R) and non-minimally coupled fields) that reconstruct rotation curves and lensing without dark matter.†
  • Non-minimally coupled vector fields reproducing Einstein-cluster dynamics that mimic dark-matter rotation curves.†
  • Screening mechanisms (chameleon, Khoury & Weltman 2004; symmetron; Vainshtein). These are precedent for environment-dependent gravity. Unlike the present proposal, they usually suppress deviations in dense regions and allow them in sparse ones, which is the same sign as the conductivity picture, though from a different motivation.

8.2 Anisotropy as a dark-matter mimic

  • Anisotropic stress and gravitational-wave propagation. Saltas, Sawicki, Amendola & Kunz (2014) showed that anisotropic stress in cosmology signals non-standard propagation of gravitational waves. This links the slip prediction (Section 6.4e) to fundamental modifications of propagation.
  • Anisotropic fluids and Hořava–Lifshitz gravity. Work by Bassani and collaborators† shows that effective anisotropic stress, including from deformations of the Hamiltonian constraint in Hořava–Lifshitz gravity, can produce dark-matter-halo-like behavior. This is close in spirit, since anisotropy generates the effect. It is field-theoretic rather than connectivity-based.
  • Dipolar dark matter / gravitational polarization (Blanchet 2007 and later). This treats the dark sector as a gravitational analog of dielectric polarization. It is a striking parallel to the conductivity/permittivity analogy here, though it introduces a polarizable medium rather than deriving one from relational structure.
  • The modified-gravity / dark-force degeneracy. Recent work† argues that lensing plus growth alone cannot distinguish modified gravity from dark-sector interactions without additional probes such as gravitational redshift. Any conductivity model sits inside this degeneracy class unless it uses connectivity-correlated or slip-based tests.

8.3 Voids as probes

  • Void lensing measurements (for example Melchior et al. 2014; Clampitt & Jain 2015) and stacked filament lensing (for example Clampitt et al. 2016). These establish that the relevant signals can be measured.
  • Voids as dark-matter probes. Recent studies† use void statistics to constrain dark-matter models, for example axion suppression of small-scale structure. They treat voids as sensitive environments, but only through particle-physics effects, not propagation.

8.4 Emergent gravity

  • Verlinde's entropic and emergent gravity (2011, 2017). Gravity arises from information and entropy gradients. The 2017 version explicitly attributes dark-matter phenomenology to an elastic response of the emergent spacetime medium, specifically of de Sitter entanglement entropy. Among established proposals this is perhaps the closest in conceptual spirit: dark matter as a response property of emergent spacetime. It differs in mechanism, using volume-law entanglement rather than relational connectivity, and in where the effect is triggered.
  • Spacetime from entanglement (Van Raamsdonk 2010; ER = EPR, Maldacena & Susskind 2013). Reducing entanglement between regions literally pulls spacetime apart in holographic settings. This is a precise realization of "geometry is relational connectivity," and it suggests that sparse connectivity should degrade geometric coherence, which is the core intuition here.

8.5 Discrete and relational quantum gravity

  • Quantum graphity (Konopka, Markopoulou, Severini 2006–2008). Spacetime geometry emerges from a dynamical graph whose connectivity condenses into a low-dimensional lattice-like phase at low energy. This is perhaps the closest structural analog to the relational-graph ontology. In such models, regions with defective or sparse connectivity can behave like emergent geometric defects. Related work explored analogs of horizons and dark-matter-like effects from such defects.
  • Causal dynamical triangulations (Ambjørn, Jurkiewicz, Loll). These show that the effective dimension of emergent spacetime is scale-dependent: the spectral dimension runs from about 4 at large scales to about 2 at short scales. This is a precedent for the idea that dimensionality is a dynamical, emergent property, though here the reduction is in the ultraviolet rather than in sparse infrared regions.
  • Causal set theory (Bombelli, Lee, Meyer, Sorkin 1987, and later). Spacetime is a discrete partial order. Geometric quantities are recovered statistically and degrade where sampling is sparse.
  • Loop quantum gravity and spin networks. Spatial geometry is encoded in graph-labelled quantum states. This is relevant as a mathematical setting in which "connectivity" has precise meaning.
  • Tensor networks and holography. Geometry is encoded in network connectivity. Cutting bonds changes emergent distances.
  • QFT on triangulated spacetime / vector models (Raasakka).† Gravity emerges as an effective phenomenon from quantum field amplitudes on triangulated structures, bringing together elements of CDT, tensor networks, and holography.
  • Relational quantum field theory (Głowacki).† Spacetime structure emerges from relational frames, with frame dependence as a possible source of anisotropy.
  • Causal-informational completions of gravity (for example the CETOmega proposal).† These propose a field encoding causal connectivity that accounts for dark-sector phenomena without new particles. As described, this is among the closest in spirit to the present idea.
  • Further relational-network frameworks (Kozacha's dual quantum graphs; Ochogo's relational coherence/null-link framework; Vaillant's Meta-Connective Physics).† These treat spacetime as a relational network, and in some cases attribute dark-matter effects to coherence distortions or relational tension. They are conceptually close. Their technical status and peer-review standing should be verified.

8.6 Positioning

Prior work has separately established these elements:

  • anisotropy in gravitational propagation can mimic dark matter;
  • environment-dependent gravity is mathematically viable;
  • spacetime may be emergent from discrete or relational connectivity, with geometry degrading as connectivity weakens;
  • voids are useful cosmological probes.

The synthesis proposed here does not appear in the literature surveyed. It holds that gravitational propagation efficiency is a function of relational connectivity, degraded specifically in large sparse regions, and that this degradation drives a partial, effectively lower-dimensional relaxation of galactic fields, observed as dark matter. The closest single neighbors are Verlinde's emergent gravity (dark matter as a medium response), quantum graphity (geometry from graph connectivity), and the causal-connectivity proposals listed above.

9. Research Program

In rough order of tractability:

  1. Toy graph simulations. On random geometric graphs with galaxy-like density islands embedded in sparse backgrounds, solve the graph Poisson equation and measure the effective falloff exponent against background sparsity. Establish whether flat-curve-like behavior emerges and whether it is threshold-like or gradual.
  2. Fix the formalism. Decide among scalar, tensor, and hybrid conductivity. Constrain the functional form κ(C) by fitting rotation curves from SPARC-like galaxy samples, requiring consistency with the radial acceleration relation.
  3. Wide-binary and vertical-dynamics checks. Confront the model's predictions of Newtonian wide binaries and of radial/vertical inferred-density mismatch (for the tensor version) with Gaia data.
  4. Lensing pipeline. Implement the connectivity-residual test of Section 6.5 on existing survey data (KiDS, DES, HSC), then forecast for Euclid and Rubin/LSST. Prioritize gravitational slip and large-radius galaxy–galaxy lensing, the latter as the decisive test of the "returns to 3D" constraint.
  5. Modified N-body runs. Implement variable-coefficient Poisson solvers. Study the effects on filament morphology and the growth rate of structure.
  6. Covariant completion. Build an effective action whose weak-field limit gives the conductivity equation, leaves gravitational-wave speed unchanged, and conserves energy and momentum.
  7. Confront cosmology. Determine whether any relational mechanism can produce the CMB's dark-matter signature at recombination, or whether the model must be hybrid.

10. Conclusion

The dark matter problem is usually stated as a missing-mass problem. It can equally be stated as a geometry problem: galactic fields fall off as though they were spreading through fewer than three dimensions. If spacetime is emergent from a relational structure whose coherence depends on connectivity, then the sparsest regions of the universe are exactly where the continuum description should be weakest. The fields of galaxies, dense islands in a sparse sea, would then relax in a way that ordinary GR reads as extra mass.

The idea is conceptually economical. It introduces no new particles, it ties dark-matter phenomenology to the foundations of spacetime structure, and it has natural neighbors throughout emergent and quantum gravity. It is also testable. Connectivity-correlated lensing residuals, gravitational slip, Newtonian wide binaries, radial–vertical dynamical mismatches, and the shape of galaxy–galaxy lensing at large radii are all within reach of current and near-future data. Its main open problems are equally clear: the cosmic microwave background, the growth of structure, and the Bullet Cluster. The most immediate next step is the simplest. Show, on a toy relational graph, that sparse surroundings really do make a dense island's field fall off more slowly. If that holds, the rest of the program becomes worth pursuing.
