# How Not to End a War: Appomattox, Versailles, and the Politics of Unfinished Peace

*Why the Civil War settlement failed, compared with Versailles and the occupations after 1945.*

> Reader note: Claim status is marked inline in [brackets]. A claim ledger is at the end.

## Introduction

Wars are usually studied as contests of force: who won, how, and at what cost. But the outcome that matters to the people who live through the aftermath is rarely the military one. It is the settlement, the arrangement of power, legitimacy, and enforcement that is supposed to make the next war unnecessary. A war can be won completely on the battlefield and lost completely at the peace table. The American Civil War is one of the clearest examples in history. [interpretation:C1]

This paper examines how not to end a war. It does so through the failed settlement after the American Civil War, compared against the Treaty of Versailles and set against the settlements that worked, chiefly the occupations of Germany and Japan after 1945. Its central claim is that peace settlements fail in a small number of recognizable ways, and that the Civil War's settlement failed in the way that is easiest to miss. It was neither too harsh nor too lenient in any simple sense. It was **unenforced**. [interpretation:C2] The victors wrote terms they were unwilling to defend, and the defeated correctly concluded that the terms could be outlasted.

The paper also takes seriously a harder argument: that the right answer would have been something more drastic. That could mean destroying the rebel army outright, or permanently stripping the franchise from the individuals who took up arms, and sparing their children. This argument comes from a reasonable frustration. The war ended, the conflict did not, and the country spent the next century living with the consequences. It deserves a real answer rather than a reflexive one, and the answer has to rest on comparative evidence, because every proposed alternative history is, as the argument's own proponents rightly insist, an untested counterfactual.

---

## I. A Taxonomy of Bad Endings

Before turning to cases, it helps to name the ways a war can be ended badly. Three failure modes recur. [model:C3]

**1. The unenforced settlement.** The victor writes demanding terms, then lacks the will, money, or attention to enforce them. The defeated party accepts formally and waits. The terms exist on paper while the underlying balance of power quietly reasserts itself.

**2. The punitive settlement without transformation.** The victor imposes costs such as reparations, territorial losses, and humiliation, but does not rebuild the institutions or the political order that produced the war. The defeated party is aggrieved but intact, and holds a ready-made narrative of betrayal.

**3. The exclusionary or eliminationist settlement.** The victor attempts to solve the problem by removing the defeated population or faction from political life, through mass exclusion, expulsion, or killing. This fails differently from the first two. It can be effective in the short run, but it replaces a political problem with a security problem, and in its extreme form it destroys the legitimacy of the victor.

A successful settlement avoids all three. It has to be enforced, it has to transform the underlying institutions rather than merely punish, and it has to leave the defeated a path to belong to the new order, though not necessarily to rule it. The settlement of the American Civil War committed the first failure in its purest form, and drifted toward the second. [interpretation:C4]

---

## II. The Civil War: A Surrender Without a Settlement

### The Military Ending

The Civil War did not end with a negotiated treaty. It ended with a series of military surrenders: Lee at Appomattox in April 1865, Johnston at Bennett Place a few weeks later, and the rest of the Confederate forces over the following weeks. The terms Grant offered Lee were generous. Soldiers were paroled and sent home, and officers and men were allowed to keep their horses and mules for spring planting. [fact:C5] Lincoln's second inaugural, "with malice toward none," set the tone the executive branch intended.

This generosity was a deliberate choice. Lee himself refused to let the army melt into the hills for guerrilla war, which was an option a few Confederate leaders discussed. [fact] The surrender prevented the kind of prolonged insurgency that later bedeviled occupations elsewhere. [interpretation:C6] It would be a mistake to treat the generosity as simple softness. It ended the shooting war quickly. The failure came afterward, in what the victors did with the peace they had won.

### Presidential Reconstruction: Handing the Keys Back

The first and decisive error came in 1865. Lincoln was assassinated, and Andrew Johnson, a Tennessee Unionist and a committed white supremacist, took a lenient line. He issued pardons in enormous numbers, restored property to former planters, and allowed the former Confederate states to reconstitute their governments under the political leadership that had just led the rebellion. [fact:C7] General Sherman's Field Order No. 15, which had set aside confiscated coastal land for freed families, was reversed, and the land went back to its former owners. [fact:C7] The economic base of the planter class survived untouched.

The response from the South was immediate and instructive. Within months of surrender, legislatures in Mississippi, South Carolina, and elsewhere passed the **Black Codes**. [fact] These laws imposed vagrancy statutes, labor contracts, and apprenticeship rules that were designed to recreate the substance of slavery under new names. [interpretation] The Ku Klux Klan formed in Tennessee in 1865-66. [fact] Southern voters sent former Confederate officials, including the vice president of the Confederacy, to represent them in Congress. [fact]

These facts bear on the question of good faith. Whatever individual Southerners believed, the political leadership of the defeated states behaved as though the war had settled the matter of secession and nothing else. [interpretation:C8] The surrender was accepted. The new constitutional order, which meant emancipation, citizenship for the freedpeople, and eventually Black suffrage, was not. That is the strongest version of the claim that the "truce" was disingenuous: the surrender was real, but the political settlement was never accepted by those who held local power.

### Congressional Reconstruction: The Right Terms Without the Staying Power

Congress responded, and its response was substantial. The Thirteenth, Fourteenth, and Fifteenth Amendments rewrote the constitutional order. [fact] The Reconstruction Acts of 1867 divided the South into military districts, required new state constitutions, and mandated Black male suffrage. [fact] Black Southerners voted, held office, and served in state legislatures and in Congress, and they built public school systems that had not existed before. [fact]

It is worth noting that the argument for excluding rebels was not merely theoretical. It was tried. Section 3 of the Fourteenth Amendment barred from office anyone who had taken an oath to support the Constitution and then engaged in insurrection, unless Congress lifted the disability by a two-thirds vote. [fact:C9] The Reconstruction Acts disenfranchised a portion of the white electorate in the former Confederacy. [fact] These measures were aimed at individuals, not bloodlines or children, much as the argument proposes.

They did not hold, and the reason is the instructive part. They did not fail because exclusion as a principle was tested and found wanting. They failed for the same reason everything else failed: **the North's will to enforce them expired.** [interpretation:C10] Congress passed the Amnesty Act of 1872, which removed the Section 3 disabilities from most former Confederates. [fact] The Enforcement Acts of 1870-71, backed by Grant, did break the first Klan, which is itself evidence that determined enforcement worked. [interpretation:C11] But the effort faded. The Panic of 1873 turned Northern attention to economic troubles. Court decisions narrowed federal power, as in *Slaughter-House* and *United States v. Cruikshank*, the latter arising from the 1873 Colfax massacre in Louisiana. [fact] Paramilitary groups such as the Red Shirts and the White League used violence and fraud to overthrow elected Reconstruction governments state by state. [fact] The disputed election of 1876 ended with the Compromise of 1877, and the remaining federal troops were withdrawn from the South. [fact]

### What Followed

What followed was not peace but a different form of the same conflict. The Redeemer governments dismantled Reconstruction's social programs. Through poll taxes, literacy tests, and the Mississippi constitution of 1890 and its imitators, Black Southerners were stripped of the vote. [fact:C12] Convict leasing, sharecropping, and debt peonage reconstructed forced labor. Segregation was written into law and blessed by *Plessy v. Ferguson* in 1896. Lynching became a tool of political terror, and in 1898 in Wilmington, North Carolina, an elected local government was overthrown in a coup. [fact] Meanwhile the Lost Cause narrative rewrote the war's meaning, and it was so successful that it shaped how generations of Americans, North and South, were taught the war's history. [interpretation]

The consequences ran through the Great Migration, the civil rights movement, and the political geography of modern America. [interpretation] The point for this paper is the mechanism. The victors won the war and surrendered the peace. [interpretation:C2] The ideology that had caused the war was never delegitimized, the elite that held it was never dislodged, and the freedpeople who were the war's moral cause were left to defend themselves against the people they had been freed from.

## III. The Comparison: Versailles

The Civil War settlement is often compared to Versailles, and the comparison is useful as long as its limits are understood.

Versailles in 1919 was punitive on paper. Germany lost territory, was disarmed, had its colonies stripped, and was saddled with reparations justified by the "war guilt" clause, Article 231. [fact:C13] But Germany was not occupied in any lasting way. The Rhineland was occupied for a time, with evacuation completed in 1930, and the French occupation of the Ruhr in 1923 produced hyperinflation and a nationalist backlash. [fact] The German state, its bureaucracy, its army officer corps, its judiciary, and its industrial base survived largely intact. [interpretation] The "stab-in-the-back" myth, the claim that an undefeated army had been betrayed at home, flourished precisely because German soil had not seen invasion. [interpretation:C14] The defeated were punished enough to be aggrieved and left strong enough to act on the grievance. Keynes argued in 1919 that the treaty was economically incoherent [@keynes1919]. [fact] Foch's remark that it was "not a peace, but an armistice for twenty years" is the other classic assessment. [secondhand:C15]

The resemblance to the Civil War lies in the mechanism, not in the harshness. [interpretation:C16] Both settlements:

- left the defeated faction's institutional and social base intact;
- gave the defeated a narrative of betrayal (the Lost Cause, the stab-in-the-back);
- relied on enforcement that eroded within a decade or so;
- allowed the defeated to reconstitute power by paramilitary and political means (the Klan and Red Shirts, the Freikorps and the early Nazi movement).

The differences matter too. Versailles was punitive and the Reconstruction amendments were transformative on paper. Versailles was imposed on a foreign state, while Reconstruction concerned a region within the victor's own country, which meant its enforcement depended on domestic politics, including a Northern electorate that was itself deeply racist and tired of the effort. And Reconstruction's failure had a specific victim class, the freedpeople, who were abandoned to the very people the war had been fought against. In this sense the Civil War was the more morally damning failure, because those who bore its cost had been promised protection. [normative:C17]

The lesson common to both is that a settlement is not its text. It is the product of the text and the willingness to enforce it. [interpretation:C18] A harsh treaty without enforcement is merely a grievance.

## IV. The Counterfactual Problem

At this point the natural objection arises. If the settlement failed, what should the victors have done instead? One answer, stated with some force, is that they should have gone further. The Confederate army should have been fully destroyed rather than paroled, and the individual participants in the rebellion should have been permanently stripped of political rights. This would have spared their children, in deliberate contrast to ideologies that punish by bloodline.

The first honest thing to say about any such proposal is that it is a counterfactual, and counterfactuals cannot be proven. The defenders of the harsher path are right that critics of it cannot simply assert it would have failed. The proposal deserves an evidence-based comparison, not a lecture. Three methodological points follow.

First, **no alternative has been tested in the American case, including the alternatives most often called "obviously correct."** The claim that fuller land reform, longer military occupation, or a more thorough purge would have worked is also a counterfactual. [conjecture:C19] All of them are bets.

Second, the only tools available are comparative cases and the limited experiments the Reconstruction era itself ran. Comparative cases can show which bets have paid off elsewhere, and where the downside risks lie. They cannot prove anything about 1865.

Third, the evidence suggests that the choice was never really between "lenient" and "harsh." [interpretation:C20] The cases that succeeded were demanding, and often harsh in specific respects. What distinguishes them is not mildness but **durability and transformation**. The proposal to exclude individual rebels from political life deserves to be assessed on that axis.

## V. Testing the Exclusion Option

### The Individual-Versus-Bloodline Distinction Is Real, and It Matters Morally

The distinction between punishing individuals for what they did and punishing a people for who they are is not trivial. [normative:C21] It is the difference between a legal order and a racial one. American practice, whatever its failings, has generally tried to frame sanctions in terms of conduct: treason, rebellion, crime. A proposal to disenfranchise those who took up arms against the Constitution, leaving their descendants fully enfranchised, is categorically different from a proposal to exclude a population by ancestry. The first is a sanction, and the second is a caste system. Anyone who confuses them is not engaging with the argument.

It is also worth noting that the distinction was part of the actual law. Section 3 of the Fourteenth Amendment is such a rule. It sanctions individuals for their conduct, can be lifted by Congress, and was designed on the premise that a person who broke an oath to the Constitution should not hold power under it. [fact:C9]

### But the Distinction Does Not Answer the Stability Question

The harder question is whether individual exclusion, even when morally defensible, stabilizes a post-war order. This is an empirical matter, and the cases are mixed in a way that is informative.

**Individual exclusion at limited scale worked as a supplement.** [interpretation:C22] In postwar Germany, the Allies pursued prosecutions at Nuremberg and a broad denazification program. [fact] The program was often clumsy. Millions of questionnaires were processed, and it was scaled back and handed to German tribunals partly because the process proved unmanageable and partly because functioning societies needed their doctors, teachers, and engineers [@taylor2011]. [fact] Yet the core idea, that the leaders and the most culpable were removed and tried while the great mass of ordinary participants were reintegrated, helped. [interpretation:C23] The exclusion was targeted, and it was paired with something much more important.

**Exclusion at mass scale, without a path back, has repeatedly produced the insurgency it was meant to prevent.** [interpretation:C24] The clearest modern case is Iraq in 2003. The Coalition Provisional Authority's Order No. 1 de-Baathified the government, and Order No. 2 dissolved the Iraqi army and security services [@bremer2006]. [fact:C25] The effect was to put several hundred thousand armed, trained, and now unemployed men, along with a large part of the administrative class, outside the new order, with no stake in its success [@ricks2006]. [estimate:C26] Many analysts, including American officials who served there, have argued that these orders helped fuel the insurgency, while L. Paul Bremer and others have argued that the army had already disintegrated and that the orders only recognized this. [contested:C27] This case is the nearest thing to a natural experiment on the proposal to destroy the defeated army and exclude its participants, and the result was not stability. [interpretation:C28]

The comparison with Appomattox is instructive. Grant's decision to parole Lee's army, which can look like softness, took the army's men out of the war without creating a mass of embittered, armed, and politically excluded men. [interpretation] The violence of Reconstruction came from paramilitaries that formed in the *absence* of a determined federal presence, not from a disbanded army. [interpretation] This suggests the problem was never that the army survived. It was that the institutions and elite behind the army were allowed to rebuild. [interpretation:C29]

**The Reconstruction experiment itself is ambiguous.** The Section 3 disabilities were in place for several years and did not produce the stable order their authors hoped. But they were also never given the chance a sustained commitment would have provided. They were lifted by Congress in 1872 in a spirit of reconciliation, before the political and economic conditions for a new order had been established. The honest conclusion is that exclusion, as tried, failed alongside everything else, and that nothing about the experiment demonstrates that a longer-lasting version would have failed or succeeded. [interpretation:C30]

### What the Evidence Supports

Putting these together, the evidence supports a more modest claim than either side usually makes. Targeted exclusion of the most culpable, applied alongside genuine institutional transformation, is compatible with successful settlements. [interpretation:C31] Mass exclusion of a large population from political life, without a route back, has a poor record, mostly because it generates people with nothing to lose. [interpretation] And in the Civil War, whatever the exclusion tools used, the decisive variable was whether the victors would stay and enforce. [interpretation:C2]

## VI. The Eliminationist Limit

Any discussion of the harshest options eventually reaches the question of what happens when a regime decides the problem is the population itself. The Nazi attempt at a "final solution" to what it imagined as a racial problem is the extreme case, and it is the clearest demonstration of the third failure mode. [interpretation:C32]

It is the right case to study not because the Civil War's harsher counterfactuals resemble it. They do not, and the individual-versus-bloodline distinction is precisely what separates them. The case is useful because it shows what happens at the extreme end of the spectrum from "exclude the dangerous" to "eliminate the problem." The project did not stabilize anything. It destroyed the German state's legitimacy, consumed the regime that carried it out, and reshaped the world order. [interpretation] It killed some six million Jews and millions of others. [estimate:C33] It produced a postwar settlement of unprecedented severity for Germany, and a political geography, including the creation of Israel and the regional conflicts that followed, that is still unresolved. The "solution" generated a far larger set of problems than it claimed to solve. [interpretation]

The structural lessons are these:

1. **Elimination destroys the victor's legitimacy.** A regime that kills its way to security has no moral authority to build a lasting order. [interpretation:C34]
2. **Elimination converts a political dispute into an existential one.** When survival is at stake, compromise becomes impossible, and the surviving members of the targeted group, and their allies, become permanent adversaries. [interpretation:C34]
3. **Elimination is not final.** Memory, grievance, and politics persist. The Holocaust did not end the problem it claimed to address. It created new ones. [interpretation:C34]

None of this is an argument against sanctions on individuals who commit crimes, including crimes against the state. It is an argument for a clear line between sanctioning conduct and removing people. [normative:C35] The line is not only moral. It is also practical, because the first can be part of a stable order and the second, historically, cannot.

## VII. What Worked: Occupation and Transformation

If these are the failures, what does success look like? The settlements after 1945 are the standard examples, and their features are instructive because they combine severity with transformation.

**West Germany.** The Allies demanded unconditional surrender, occupied the country entirely, and held it for a decade. Nuremberg and subsequent trials removed the top leadership. The occupation reshaped institutions, including the press, education, the party system, and eventually the constitution, the Basic Law of 1949, which was written with explicit protections against the return of the preceding order. [fact:C36] The Marshall Plan gave Germans a prosperous stake in the new system. Critically, Germany was not left alone with its defeat. It was integrated into NATO and the institutions that became the European Union, which gave it security and gave its neighbors a way to trust it.

**Japan.** The American occupation, from 1945 to 1952, rewrote the constitution, carried out significant land reform that broke the rural landlord class, purged wartime leaders (a purge that was later partly reversed as Cold War priorities shifted), and left the Emperor on the throne in a changed role to maintain legitimacy [@dower1999]. [fact:C36] Again the pattern is severity at the top, reintegration for the mass of the population, structural change in the underlying economy, and a long commitment.

Three features recur. [interpretation:C37]

1. **The victor stayed.** The occupations lasted years and involved enormous resources. The post-Civil War occupation of the South was thin and brief by comparison, and it was shut down before the new order could sustain itself.
2. **The settlements changed the material base of power.** Land reform in Japan and the dismantling of cartels and the reshaping of institutions in Germany altered who held economic and political power. Reconstruction's attempt at land reform was abandoned in 1865, and the planter class kept its land. [fact:C7]
3. **The defeated had a stake in the new order.** Prosperity, security guarantees, and political participation gave ordinary people reasons to defend the new system.

Important caveats apply. The Allies had advantages that the Union did not. Germany and Japan were foreign countries whose populations could be occupied without the political complications of governing one's own citizens. Both were also devastated and discredited in ways the antebellum South, which had been defeated but whose cause retained loyal adherents in a still-sovereign region, was not. And Cold War competition gave the United States powerful self-interested reasons to make West Germany and Japan succeed. [interpretation:C38] Nothing comparable motivated the Gilded Age North to protect Black Southerners. These differences are part of why the comparison is instructive and not a recipe.

## VIII. Principles for Ending a War Well, and Badly

From these cases, a set of principles follows. Stated negatively, as ways of failing:

**1. Do not write terms you will not enforce.** [normative:C39] An unenforced settlement is worse than none, because it teaches the defeated that the victor's word has an expiration date. The Reconstruction amendments were among the most important texts in American history, and for roughly eighty years they were largely a dead letter in the places that most needed them.

**2. Do not mistake a surrender for a settlement.** [normative:C40] An army laying down its arms ends a military conflict. It does not end a political one. The political settlement is a separate act that requires its own sustained effort, and it is where the real peace is won or lost.

**3. Do not leave the defeated elite's power base intact.** [normative:C41] Punishment that spares the underlying institutions, whether land, offices, networks, or ideology, simply gives the defeated time. If the war was fought over an order, that order has to be dismantled and replaced.

**4. Do not abandon the people the war was fought for.** [normative:C42] The moral cause of the war becomes the settlement's most vulnerable constituency. When the victor leaves, those people are left to face the defeated alone. In the American South, the freedpeople paid for this lapse for a century.

**5. Do not let the defeated write the history.** [normative:C43] The Lost Cause was a political project as much as a cultural one, and it succeeded in part because the victors did not contest it. A settlement needs a narrative of what the war meant, and if the victor does not supply one, the defeated will.

**6. Do not confuse severity with finality.** [normative:C44] Harsh measures are not inherently stabilizing. Exclusion and humiliation without transformation create grievance without removing capacity. Mass exclusion without a path back creates people with nothing to lose. And the eliminationist extreme destroys the legitimacy of the one who attempts it.

**7. Do not treat sanctions on individuals and on populations as the same.** [normative:C45] Holding the culpable responsible for what they did is compatible with a durable order. Punishing people for what they are is not. The first sustains the rule of law, and the second abandons it.

## Conclusion

The strongest form of the critique of the Civil War settlement is that it was a failure of nerve disguised as magnanimity. [interpretation:C46] Northern elites wanted commerce restored, the cotton trade resumed, and the burden of governing a hostile region lifted from their shoulders. Southern elites wanted to preserve what they could of the old order. The two groups found common ground at the expense of those with the least power. That diagnosis is largely correct, and it is the reason the settlement failed.

The best remedy, however, is not the one most often proposed in frustration. The evidence of comparable cases does not show that a more thorough destruction of the rebel army, or permanent political exclusion of its participants, would have secured the peace. It shows that settlements succeed when the victor stays, when the underlying structures of power are changed, when the vulnerable are protected, and when the defeated are given a place in the new order, even if not the place they held before. [interpretation:C47] It is a counterfactual, and it remains unproven. [conjecture:C19] But the cases available to test it point in the same direction.

The war was ended in 1865. The peace was not made, and it had to be made later, in the 1950s and 1960s, by people who finished what Reconstruction had started and who, in the process, paid in blood for the earlier failure to do so. [interpretation] The lesson for anyone ending a war is that the final shot is not the end. The end comes when the order the victors fought for can survive without them standing over it. Until then the war is still being fought, in other forms and with other weapons, whether or not anyone is willing to call it by its name.

---

## Limits

This piece rests on a handful of cases, and each differs from the Civil War in ways that limit what it can show. Germany and Japan were foreign countries occupied by outside powers, Versailles ended a war between states, and Iraq in 2003 involved a different kind of state and a different insurgency. Treating them as evidence about 1865 requires the step of judging that the cases are alike in the respects that matter, and a reader may reasonably reject that step.

Rival accounts exist at several points. Some historians of Reconstruction weight Southern violence and Northern racism more heavily than lapsed Northern will, and on that reading no enforcement effort of plausible size would have held. Others argue that a settlement could have succeeded if it had broken the planter class by confiscation, as Thaddeus Stevens proposed. That proposal is closer to this piece's conclusion (change the base of power) than to the exclusion option, but this piece does not test it either. On Iraq, the causal role of the 2003 orders is disputed (C27). Every claim about what would have happened under a different 1865 settlement is a conjecture (C19), including the claims made here.
