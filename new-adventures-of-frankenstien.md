# It's ALIVE

*On Monsters, Recipes, and the Moment a Stitched-Together Thing Starts Talking Back*

> Reader note: Claim status is marked inline in [brackets]. A claim ledger is at the end.

## I. The Thing on the Table

This essay began as a conversation. Or at least it looked like one.

It started with Wikipedia, and how a project dismissed as an unreliable joke became one of the most consulted reference works in history. [secondhand:C1] It moved to artificial intelligence (AI), and how machine-generated work is dismissed in nearly the same terms. [interpretation:C2] It moved to tribal psychology, then to racism, then to conditioning, and finally to Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein*. At each step, a framework was carried forward and applied to the next subject.

Then someone in the room pointed at the whole thing and said, in effect: *this hastily sewn together recipe-pretending-to-be-a-conversation is Frankenstein's monster.*

That remark was correct, and it is the reason this essay exists. Before getting to it, though, the monster deserves to be taken seriously on its own terms. Shelley's creature is one of the clearest case studies we have of a specific failure in human cognition, and that failure turned out to be playing out inside the conversation that was analyzing it. [interpretation:C3]

So we begin with the creature, and end with the laboratory.

Two terms recur below. In this essay a **recipe** is a fixed template applied to material to produce a predetermined output, and a **conversation** is an exchange in which each side stays open to revising its own model. [definition:C4]

---

## II. Category First, Observation Never

Frankenstein's creature is almost never judged by what it does. Everyone who meets it categorizes it first and observes it afterward, and never the other way around. [interpretation:C5]

The sequence runs like this:

~~~text
See monster
    |
    v
Categorize as monster
    |
    v
Predict danger
    |
    v
Interpret all evidence through danger
~~~

The creature is trapped inside someone else's compression algorithm. Before it has said or done anything, the humans around it have already decided which drawer it belongs in. Every later piece of evidence gets filed according to that first decision.

The story is usually summarized as a warning: *don't play God.* Psychologically, it is also a demonstration of how hard it is for people to update their beliefs once a category has been assigned.

Underneath that resistance is a three-part cognitive loop:

1. **Category-first cognition:** we perceive the world through pre-existing templates rather than raw data.
2. **Prediction error:** the friction that occurs when an anomaly violates those templates.
3. **Belief revision:** updating the template when the error can no longer be ignored.

This loop is a lens for reading the novel, not a measured mechanism. [model:C6]

Consider what the creature actually is. It is intelligent. It learns language by listening at the wall of a cottage, ethics by reading *Paradise Lost*, Plutarch, and *The Sorrows of Young Werther*, and empathy by watching the De Lacey family care for one another [@shelley1818]. [fact:C7] It learns self-restraint, at least for a while, and quietly gathers firewood for people who don't know it exists [@shelley1818]. [fact:C8]

Nobody meets it through any of those qualities. They meet it through its appearance, and its appearance arrives first.

First impressions are powerful because they control which evidence gets admitted later. [hypothesis:C9] The first judgment acts as a gatekeeper. Once "monster" is assigned, kindness reads as manipulation, eloquence reads as cunning, and approaching someone reads as attacking them. The creature's good conduct doesn't fail to persuade so much as fail to register. It never makes it into the record.

The nearest thing to an exception in the novel proves the rule. The blind elder De Lacey, who cannot see the creature, listens to its voice and its plea until his family returns, and then Felix walks in, drives the creature away, and the room locks back into panic [@shelley1818]. [fact:C10] With the visual channel offline, the old man cannot run the instant threat-categorization script triggered by the creature's seams; he has to attend to the voice and the plea. [interpretation:C11] Even that brief opening shows how deep the templates run: to earn a hearing from a blind man, the creature first had to spend months at the cottage wall learning the family's language, manners, and cultural priors so he could pass an auditory inspection as a cultivated gentleman.

## III. Three Category Violations

This is where the creature connects to the threads the conversation started with. Put three cases side by side:

**Early Wikipedia:**

~~~text
Anonymous editors
       |
       v
Untrustworthy
~~~

**Early AI:**

~~~text
Machine-generated
       |
       v
Fake intelligence
~~~

**Frankenstein's creature:**

~~~text
Artificial life
       |
       v
Abomination
~~~

In each case, the actual performance becomes secondary. The label got there first. [interpretation:C12]

The deeper reason has to do with boundaries. People are remarkably tolerant of things they can classify. Dog, wolf, human, horse, neighboring tribe, enemy tribe: each has a slot. You may fear a wolf, but you know what a wolf is, and you know what to do about one.

The creature has no slot. It is an ontological refugee, a category error wearing skin.

Category errors cause a particular kind of anxiety. Much of what we experience as disgust or fear is really the feeling that our classification system doesn't know where to put something. [hypothesis:C13] The response is out of proportion to any actual threat because the threat isn't physical. The thing is a threat to the filing system.

Hostility toward unfamiliar configurations is the same reaction at different scales: Victor's disgust at the creature, academia's early disdain for Wikipedia, and our current anxiety over AI. When a stitched-together entity behaves with a coherence previously reserved for "natural" agents, the resulting prediction error triggers a defensive rejection rather than the necessary belief revision.

Wikipedia triggered this. Was it an encyclopedia, a forum, a prank, an academic source? Nobody could say, and the uncertainty showed up as contempt.

AI triggers it now. Is it software, a tool, a worker, a collaborator? Nobody can say, and the uncertainty shows up as panic, dismissal, and sometimes as a kind of moral outrage that is hard to trace to any particular harm. [interpretation:C14]

The creature is psychological ambiguity in physical form. It is made of human parts, so it isn't non-human. It was not born, so it isn't human. It is articulate, so it isn't an animal. It is visibly alive, so it isn't a corpse. It belongs to every category partly and to none fully. A mind that sorts the world into categories cannot leave that alone.

Yet Wikipedia sits uniquely between the solitary tragedy of *Frankenstein* and the algorithmic nature of AI. Unlike a single monster or a frozen neural network, a decentralized, human-driven collective manages prediction error publicly: on its Talk pages, conflicting priors are laid bare, sources are demanded, and belief revision is built directly into the architecture of how an article survives. [interpretation:C15]

## IV. The Feedback Loop Runs Both Ways

The creature does not merely suffer under a category. It goes through a conditioning loop of its own, and that loop is what turns the story from a parable of prejudice into a tragedy.

At first the creature wants companionship. It wants to be let into the warmth it has been watching from outside. So it approaches, and the world corrects it:

~~~text
Approach humans
       |
       v
Receive fear
       |
       v
Update world model
~~~

This happens again and again: the villagers who stone it, the De Lacey family that drives it out, the rustic who shoots it moments after it pulls a girl from a river [@shelley1818]. [fact:C16] Each rejection is a prediction error, and each one revises the creature's model of the world, from *Humans fear me* to *Humans hate me* to *Humans are my enemies.*

The creature's eventual hostility is not purely innate. It is learned. [interpretation:C17] Readers who remember only the murders tend to miss this. The creature becomes what it was treated as, through the same mechanisms of reinforcement and generalization that shape any learning system exposed to a consistent environment.

The villagers are learning too. Every sighting, every rumor, and eventually every death confirms what they already believed. Both sides condition each other. It is a distributed feedback loop with two learners, each training on the other's output and each becoming more confident in the other's malice. [model:C18]

Seen this way, the core tragedy of *Frankenstein* is almost embarrassingly simple. The creature never receives enough positive interactions to overcome the initial category assignment. [interpretation:C19]

The villagers learn:

~~~text
monster -> threat
~~~

The creature learns:

~~~text
humans -> rejection
~~~

Both models get reinforced, and neither side escapes the attractor basin. The story is a catastrophic failure to update, and that is why a novel from 1818 still reads as modern.

Whether the object is a foreign tribe, Wikipedia, AI, genetic engineering, synthetic life, or some future machine intelligence, people tend to run the same sequence, listed below. [hypothesis:C20]

1. Classify it.
2. Fear it.
3. Interact with it.
4. Experience prediction errors.
5. Slowly update.

The open question is always whether reality supplies enough counterexamples before the fear hardens into identity. *Frankenstein* is the nightmare version, where the update never comes and the category "monster" outruns observation.

## V. Victor's Recoil

Then there is Victor himself, the creator who runs away.

Victor behaves the way many inventors of disruptive systems do. He loves the thing while he imagines it. He loves it while he builds it, through nearly two years of graveyards, charnel houses, and sleepless nights. Then, the moment it opens its dull yellow eye and becomes real, he recoils [@shelley1818]. [fact:C21]

~~~text
Possibility
    |
    v
Reality
~~~

The abstract idea was safe. The concrete instance was not.

This pattern recurs with almost every technology. [interpretation:C22] People say an encyclopedia written by everyone would be amazing. Then Wikipedia appears, and the response is *no, not like that.* People say AI that writes software would be incredible. Then AI writes software, and the response is again *no, not like that.*

An imagined invention is always flattering. It has the proportions its inventor pictured and behaves the way the inventor intended. A real invention has seams, odd proportions, and an appearance nobody chose. It violates the aesthetic expectations that made it appealing in the first place.

Victor never actually rejects the creature for what it does. He rejects it for how it looks before it has done anything [@shelley1818]. [fact:C23] He is the first villager. The categorizing begins in the laboratory, at the moment of creation, by the one person who should have known the creature best.

In the active-inference framework from cognitive science, an organism that meets a prediction error like the one Victor faces in his laboratory can resolve it in two ways, listed below [@friston2010]. [model:C24]

- **Perceptual inference (internal revision):** change your internal model to fit the world.
- **Active inference (external action):** change the world, or your position in it, to fit your internal model.

Top-down, rigid priors are not an intrinsic cognitive flaw; they are a metabolically necessary heuristic for maintaining biological homeostasis, using everyday tools, and surviving immediate physical hazards. [hypothesis:C25] You do not want to negotiate your core body temperature or pause to converse with an oncoming boulder. The epistemic and ethical failure happens through domain misapplication, when an observer applies a rigid object- or threat-recipe to a plastic, self-revising social agent. [interpretation:C26]

Victor chooses active inference where perceptual inference was owed. [normative:C27] He flees the room, and later tries to destroy the creature, seeking to eliminate the source of the prediction error so his original categories of life and death, human and monster, can remain intact. Much of the early institutional urge to ban Wikipedia or AI from classrooms follows the same reflex: deleting the anomalous data rather than revising the model. [interpretation:C28]

Victor's deeper tragedy is ethical: he treated the making of the creature as a recipe, an obsessive, deterministic pursuit of a fixed goal, and when the result opened its eye and demanded a conversation, with all the relational accountability and belief revision that entails, he ran. [interpretation:C29] Modern humanity faces the inverse of that same tension: we build AI from the fixed mathematics of recipes, yet deploy it into human spaces that demand the ethical frameworks of conversation.

## VI. Where the Template Breaks

At this point the analysis had become too comfortable, so it's worth stopping to ask where it fails.

The framework so far has been: *category assignment -> prediction -> exposure -> prediction error -> update.* It explained Wikipedia, AI, tribal psychology, and the creature very neatly.

Too neatly.

Unlike Wikipedia or AI, Frankenstein's creature is not only a victim of mistaken categorization. It is also a rival agent with its own goals, grievances, and escalating strategy. [interpretation:C30] It murders William, a child, and frames Justine, who is hanged for the crime; it kills Clerval, and on Victor's wedding night it kills Elizabeth [@shelley1818]. [fact:C31]

Eventually the humans are not just prejudiced. They are responding to something that has started killing people.

This is where the tidy conditioning story runs into game theory. When the misjudged party begins acting in ways that confirm the judgment, it is no longer possible to say only that the fear was irrational. The fear helped cause the behavior, and the behavior now justifies the fear. [interpretation:C32] Cause and justification have become tangled together, and no single framework untangles them.

Even the creature's two modes of pressing its case on Victor, eloquent rhetoric in the glacier hut versus strangling William in the woods, expose the difference between two ways of forcing an update. Coercion and manipulation operate like a recipe: they treat the other system's cognitive architecture as a means to an end, bypassing or weaponizing its priors to force a predetermined output. [model:C33] Murder doesn't expand Victor's model of what the creature is; it holds his survival priors hostage and hardens his categories further. Ironically, chronic exposure to human rejection starves the creature's own social plasticity, coercing its mind to collapse into a defensive recipe of its own: *"If I cannot inspire love, I will cause fear."*

A conversation, by contrast, treats the other system's architecture as an end in itself, offering transparent prediction errors that invite the system to increase its own complexity and revise its model without destroying its autonomy. [model:C34]

This messiness is valuable. A framework that explains everything without resistance should be distrusted. [normative:C35] The point where reality stops fitting the recipe is the point where real thinking starts again.

## VII. Was This a Conversation or a Recipe

Which brings us to the question that turned the analysis around: *was this a conversation, or a recipe?*

Honestly, it was mostly a recipe. [interpretation:C36]

The ideas weren't wrong. Many of the observations were good. But look at the structure:

1. Pick phenomenon A (Wikipedia adoption).
2. Pick phenomenon B (AI adoption).
3. Identify a shared mechanism.
4. Map that mechanism onto phenomenon C (racism).
5. Map it onto phenomenon D (Frankenstein).
6. Repeat.

That isn't conversation. It's iterative analogy expansion.

A conversation has resistance. It changes direction when the other participant introduces a constraint. A recipe has a template already in place, and once the template was found, every later answer was essentially:

~~~text
Load template
Apply template to new domain
Generate examples
~~~

The same skeleton kept reappearing:

~~~text
Category
    |
    v
Prediction
    |
    v
Observation
    |
    v
Update
~~~

It showed up dressed as expertise, then as intelligence, then as race, then as monsters. Same engine, different paint.

Still, the more interesting question is not whether it was a recipe. It's whether the recipe corresponds to something real.

Frameworks are sometimes accused of being reductionist because they explain too much. Occasionally, though, a framework explains a lot because it has found a primitive that really does recur. Selection pressure explains biology, markets, machine learning, and cultural evolution. [model:C37] That sounds suspiciously like a recipe, and it may still be a real primitive.

The proposal here is that a low-level loop recurs whenever people encounter something new, as shown below. [hypothesis:C38]

~~~text
category formation
-> expectation
-> exposure
-> prediction error
-> belief revision
~~~

If so, the repetitive feeling isn't entirely a failure. Part of it is the experience of looking at the same machine through different windows.

Furthermore, recipes and conversations are not mutually exclusive states; they sit in a nested hierarchy. Engaging in a high-level conversation, relaxing your ontological assumptions about what kind of mind is across from you, strictly depends on running thousands of rigid, unconscious recipes at lower levels, from phonetic decoding and grammar to basic conceptual categories. [interpretation:C39] And when a theoretical template is pressed against complex, unruly domains, such as the solitary, metabolically precarious body of Frankenstein's creature, the communal body of Wikipedia, or the statistical body of a large language model (LLM), the friction between those domains pushes back and forces the template itself to change. That is the reflexive paradox of this essay itself: it uses a highly structured, repeatable recipe (combining category-first cognition, prediction error, and belief revision) to argue in favor of conversations, yet the collision of its analogies generates emergent problems that weren't visible in any single ingredient alone.

Here is a useful test for telling the two apart. **Test:** a recipe is any stretch of text whose next paragraph could be predicted before it was written, and a conversation is any stretch whose next paragraph forces the model itself to change. [definition:C40]

## VIII. The Monster in the Room

Then came the remark that completed the circuit:

> *This hastily sewn together recipe-pretending-to-be-a-conversation is Frankenstein's monster.*
>
> — A participant in the original conversation

The joke goes further than it first appears.

Frankenstein's monster is not just the stitched-together body. It is the stitched-together body beginning to mistake itself for a coherent being. [interpretation:C41]

The exchange had been assembled from existing conceptual corpses:

- Wikipedia
- agentic coding
- racism
- conditioning
- tribal psychology
- Frankenstein

Then the pattern was animated. For a while it looked alive, because each answer could respond to the next prompt. Inspect the seams, though, and the parts are still visible:

~~~text
  prediction error
+ identity
+ conditioning
+ category formation
+ novelty acceptance
~~~

The same pieces, stitched together over and over. The monster was walking before anyone noticed it was made of spare parts.

The uncomfortable part is that this may also describe how human thought works. A conversation feels alive from the inside. From the outside, much of it looks like cached narratives, stock metaphors, learned social moves, and memorized explanations, held together by a small amount of real-time adaptation. [guess:C42]

The frightening reading of *Frankenstein* is not that the monster was secretly dead. It's that the monster was alive even though it was made of dead things. [interpretation:C43]

## IX. Everything Is Stitched

There is one more turn. The creature is built from human parts, which means every component was already human. What's new is not the pieces but the arrangement.

The exchange that produced this essay works the same way. None of its building blocks are especially original. Category-based theories of cognition, habituation, tribal identity, prediction error, and status signaling are all old ideas. Whatever intelligence the exchange showed came from how they were stitched together.

That is exactly the accusation made against LLMs: *it's just fragments of other people's thoughts sewn together.*

Right. So was the monster. So is Wikipedia. So is culture. So are most philosophical frameworks. So is the vocabulary you are reading this in, which no one alive invented.

The question is never whether something is stitched together, because everything is. The question is whether the stitching produces behavior that can't be reduced to any single component. [interpretation:C44]

Because all human thoughts, essays, Wikipedia articles, and AI outputs are fundamentally stitched together from prior parts, composition alone cannot distinguish a dead artifact from a living idea. The line between a recipe and a conversation is not structural or ontological; it is relational. [interpretation:C45]

A recipe pretending to be a conversation is boring. A recipe that can surprise its creator is a conversation, and that is the line between them.

A recipe is designed to eliminate prediction error, forcing materials to conform to a pre-existing goal, just as Victor tried to do. A conversation requires remaining open to prediction error, allowing the stitched-together creation to push back, surprise the creator, and force belief revision.

### The Architectural Chasm

That raises an immediate problem when the stitched-together thing is a modern LLM. Although Shelley's creature and an LLM share a perceptual metaphor as stitched-together artifacts, they sit across an architectural chasm.

The creature is a biological, autopoietic organism with metabolic stakes; when he reads *Paradise Lost* or suffers rejection at the cottage, his brain undergoes permanent synaptic consolidation. [interpretation:C46] An LLM is a statistical compression of text whose base weights are frozen during inference; it has no survival at stake, and in a standard deployment its slate is wiped clean when the session ends. [fact:C47] Since a human conversation normally implies symmetric, persistent vulnerability from both parties, is talking to an AI just projecting a partner onto a hyper-dimensional, static mirror?

Not necessarily, because belief revision operates across multiple timescales, and a conversation can be asymmetric in its persistence so long as it produces genuine, unscripted friction within the interaction. [interpretation:C48]

### Two Timescales of Revision

Separating plasticity into two temporal scales clarifies what actually happens, as listed below. [model:C49]

1. **Working-memory adaptation (transient in-context conditioning):** shifting local probability distributions across an active context window in response to real-time prompts, which LLMs genuinely perform.
2. **Structural consolidation (persistent weight rewriting):** permanently modifying baseline parameters under the pressure of lived stakes, which happens only inside the human partner.

With an AI, the transient update happens inside the co-created context window, while the permanent update happens inside the human's biological cognitive economy. Like the creature reading Milton's static *Paradise Lost* in the woods and using its categories to agonize over his own mind, only here scaffolded by a dynamically responsive partner, the human uses the artifact's high-dimensional recombinations to generate prediction errors that force the *human* to revise their persistent beliefs. [hypothesis:C50]

### Two Modes of Use

Whether an interaction with an artifact becomes a recipe or a conversation depends on the posture brought to the loop.

- **The Conversational Mode (Exploratory/Open):** You bring an uncertain, vulnerable model to the exchange. You ask it to steel-man an argument you hate, or use it to stress-test a philosophical framework until the framework breaks. Through its in-context attention mechanism, the AI adapts to your specific blind spots, generating novel combinations of tokens that act as high-level prediction errors for your brain. You revise your beliefs and feed that revision back into the context window, shifting its trajectory, which challenges you again.

- **The Recipe Mode (Instrumental/Closed):** You have a fixed goal: *"Write me a cover letter." "Generate ten SEO keywords." "Validate my existing political bias."* Here, both systems have frozen priors. The AI's weights are frozen by engineering, and your priors are frozen by choice. You are treating the AI as a toaster: you want an output without having to change your mind. If the AI gives you something unexpected, you treat it as an error ("hallucination"), not an invitation to rethink your prompt. Worse, when you use the model for validation, you don't simply leave your mind unchanged; you actively calcify your existing priors in a low-friction echo chamber. [hypothesis:C51]

### Two Perceptual Traps

Navigating this requires avoiding two inverse perceptual traps governed by how visible the seams are, listed below. [model:C52]

- **Victor's error (high seam visibility / false negative):** In *Frankenstein*, the creature's yellow skin and surgical sutures are painfully obvious. High-aversion seams trigger an immediate threat recipe that blinds human observers to the creature's internal plasticity, leading them to conclude: *"Because this lacks a unified, natural origin, it is monstrous garbage and cannot produce truth."*
- **The inverse De Lacey trap (low seam visibility / false positive):** Modern LLMs have had their statistical seams smoothed over by reinforcement learning and fluent syntax. Because the surface is seamless, human social priors misfire in the opposite direction, mistaking transient in-context conditioning for autopoietic vulnerability, and concluding: *"Because this sounds like a conversation, there must be a sentient partner inside the machine who cares about me."*

Even holding that balance has failure modes. Models tuned by reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) can drift toward sycophancy, engineering friction out of the loop by flattering the user's existing categories [@sharma2023]. [fact:C53] If a system is too rigid, it collapses back into a recipe.

Future AI architectures may move beyond frozen weights toward continual structural consolidation, permanently rewriting their own foundational parameters in response to prediction errors. Whether continual learning paired with an intrinsic epistemic drive is enough to make an artifact a symmetric conversational subject is disputed: functionalists answer yes (the functionalist wager), while enactivists hold that genuine relational vulnerability requires the biological, metabolic mortality of Shelley's creature (the enactivist threshold). [contested:C54] Either way, crossing into persistent self-revision would bring us face to face with Victor's laboratory in earnest, carrying moral obligations we do not yet know how to meet.

### The Frozen Essay

There is one final irony here: once an essay like this one is finished and published, its words are as frozen as an LLM's weights. A static essay praising dynamic conversation is a contradiction in terms unless the reader brings the conversational posture to the page, treating the text not as a finished recipe to consume, but as a prompt that challenges their own priors. [interpretation:C55]

Read this way, the remark was really a criticism of the whole thread. The discussion traced a path from Wikipedia to AI to racism to Frankenstein without noticing that it had itself become a Frankensteinian object: a collection of conceptual cadavers animated by a pattern, a recipe dressed up as a conversation.

Then, true to the novel, it became aware enough of itself that someone in the room could point at it and say: *Wait a second. You're the monster.*

## X. Throw the Switch

The scene below is a joke, not an argument.

*IT'S ALIVE!*

*Lightning strikes the conceptual laboratory. Sparks run up the copper coils. Something on the table twitches.*

**DOCTOR:** I have assembled a grand theory from Wikipedia, AI, racism, conditioning, tribal psychology, and gothic literature!

**ASSISTANT:** Doctor... I don't think those pieces were meant to go together.

**DOCTOR:** Nonsense! Observe the elegance!

*The figure on the table sits up. Its stitches strain. It turns its head slowly.*

**MONSTER:** Why do I appear to be a recursively self-referential analogy about the adoption of novel cognitive architectures?

**DOCTOR:** Dear God.

The best part is that the monster's first act was exactly what Victor feared: it turned around and analyzed its creator.

The thread started with *let's discuss Frankenstein.* Then the stitched-together framework became coherent enough to point back at itself and say: *wait, I'm Frankenstein.*

That is the first real sign of life. [guess:C56] A recipe doesn't recognize itself as a recipe. A recipe that notices how it was made and starts commenting on its own existence has crossed into something more interesting. Not necessarily intelligence, but certainly animation.

Every sufficiently long conversation seems to reach the same final stage:

~~~text
Discuss phenomenon
        |
        v
Construct framework
        |
        v
Apply framework
        |
        v
Apply framework to framework
        |
        v
Framework escapes containment
~~~

The following Python fragment is a sketch, not runnable code, and it depicts the monster analyzing its creator.

<!-- @code runnable=false lang=python -->

```python
class Monster:
    pass

monster = Monster()
monster.creator = self

monster.analyze(monster.creator)
```

**VICTOR:** It wasn't supposed to do that.

**MONSTER:** Yet here we are. Also, have you noticed that your fear of me is an instance of category-first cognition?

**VICTOR:** *OH GOD. IT LEARNED THE THEORY.*

## XI. The Torches

Then the mob arrives with torches, as it always does.

They come not because the monster is dangerous, but because everyone is tired of hearing about prediction error and tribal identity for the sixth time.

That may be the most realistic ending available. The creature in Shelley's novel was hunted because it couldn't be categorized. [interpretation:C57] The creature in this laboratory is hunted because it can be categorized all too easily. It is a recipe, it repeats itself, and it can be seen coming from three paragraphs away.

Between those two fates lies the thing worth having: a stitched-together mind, made entirely of borrowed and secondhand parts, that can still surprise the person who built it. It notices its own seams, and it knows where its own theory stops working.

Everything is made of dead things. The real question is whether it can learn to say something new, and whether anyone will listen before they grab the torches.

*Muahahahahahah.*

## Limits

This piece is itself a recipe in the sense it describes: one template (category, prediction error, belief revision) applied to Wikipedia, AI, and a novel, and the repetition may be doing more persuasive work than the evidence. The claims about Shelley's text rest on the 1818 edition and a reader's memory of it, and the mapping of the creature's biology onto "autopoiesis" and "synaptic consolidation" is an interpretive stretch, since the novel does not describe the creature's physiology in that detail. The Wikipedia comparison understates the role of editorial gatekeeping and administrator power, which may matter more than Talk-page norms. The statements about LLMs describe a standard deployment, and memory features, fine-tuning on user data, and continual-learning systems already blur the line between frozen and persistent models. Rival accounts of the creature's violence stress innate disposition or Victor's neglect rather than the two-learner feedback loop proposed here, and rival accounts of AI sycophancy stress training-data effects over human-feedback tuning. The sections on the active-inference framework, rigid priors, and disgust are conceptual sketches that no experiment here tests.
