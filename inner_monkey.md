# The Arithmetic of the Troop: What Monkeys Know Without Knowing They Know It

Watch a troop of baboons settle in for the evening, and you will see what looks like ordinary animal life. Juveniles wrestle. Mothers groom infants. A large male yawns, showing canines as long as a leopard's, and everyone nearby takes a step back. Nothing about the scene looks like mathematics. Nobody is counting. Nobody is drawing diagrams. Nobody is solving for anything.

But every animal in that clearing is tracking dozens of relationships at once. Each one knows who outranks whom, which family sits above which, who is allied with whom this season, whose mother will come screaming if her child is bothered, and which grooming partner is worth keeping. Each one updates that model constantly. Each one acts on it with real precision.

This essay is about monkeys: what they are, how they live, and what their minds can do. It is also about a quieter argument that monkeys make for us. You can understand a structure deeply without having a single word for it. That fact says a lot about monkeys. It says even more about the primates who study them, and about a kind of genius that most of us carry every day and almost never get credit for. Modern life has a habit of keeping that genius off the books, closeted even from the people who have it. The monkeys can help us read the ledger.

They can also warn us about something less comfortable. A mind that understands a structure without being able to explain it is a mind that can, when it suits, claim not to understand it at all. That darker side of the same gift will come back before we are done.

## What a Monkey Is

"Monkey" is a looser word than it sounds. It names no single natural group, just most of the primates that are neither apes nor the lemurs, lorises, and tarsiers. Monkeys fall into two lineages that split tens of millions of years ago: the **New World monkeys** of Central and South America, such as capuchins, howlers, spider monkeys, and marmosets, some with prehensile tails that work like a fifth hand; and the **Old World monkeys** of Africa and Asia, such as baboons, macaques, mandrills, colobus monkeys, and vervets.

There are well over two hundred species. The pygmy marmoset weighs about as much as a stick of butter. A male mandrill can top thirty kilograms, his face painted red and blue in shades that deepen with his social standing. Japanese macaques sit in volcanic hot springs in the snow, proboscis monkeys wade through Bornean mangroves, and geladas graze the Ethiopian highlands in herds of hundreds.

The settings could hardly be more different. Across nearly all of them, one thing stays constant. Wherever monkeys live together, each of them has to carry intricate mental machinery for navigating the troop.

## The Real Problem Is Other Monkeys

Food, predators, and weather all matter to a monkey. For most species, though, the hardest environment to manage is the group.

Many monkeys live in permanent social groups that range from a handful of individuals to more than a hundred. Living in a group has clear benefits. More eyes spot more predators, and more bodies can defend a fruiting tree against rivals. It also creates constant problems. Food has to be shared, mates are contested, and alliances shift. Every member of the group is at once a potential ally, a competitor, a threat, and a resource.

In the 1980s, primatologists Richard Byrne and Andrew Whiten popularized what became known as the **Machiavellian intelligence hypothesis**. The idea is that primate intelligence evolved mainly to solve social problems rather than ecological ones like finding fruit or cracking nuts. Robin Dunbar later sharpened this into the **social brain hypothesis**. Across primates, the relative size of the neocortex tracks the typical size of the social group. Bigger groups mean more relationships to track, and more relationships seem to demand more brain.

It is not the whole story. Some researchers find that diet predicts brain size about as well as group size does: fruit-eaters, who must remember where and when scattered trees will ripen, tend to have larger brains than leaf-eaters. The two explanations are probably partners rather than rivals. A mind built to map a forest can also be put to work mapping a troop, and the troop is a forest that argues back.

This matters for us more than it might seem. Humans are apes, not monkeys, but we are close cousins of the Old World monkeys. We share a common ancestor with baboons and macaques more recently than either of us shares one with the capuchins of South America. In that sense a macaque is a closer relative to you than it is to a capuchin, and the social machinery in a baboon's head is a working draft of machinery we still use.

Consider what "tracking relationships" actually involves. In a group of twenty animals there are 190 possible pairs. But a monkey cannot just know how it relates to each other individual. It has to know how *they* relate to *each other*, because that determines what will happen if it acts. If I threaten this female, will her sister step in? Does her sister outrank me? Is the sister's main ally currently nearby? The number of things that matter grows much faster than the size of the group.

Monkeys manage it anyway.

## The Graph in the Baboon's Head

Some of the most elegant evidence comes from the work of Dorothy Cheney and Robert Seyfarth, who spent years studying chacma baboons in the Okavango Delta of Botswana and wrote about it in their book *Baboon Metaphysics*.

Female baboons live in a stable hierarchy organized by matrilines. Daughters inherit a rank just below their mothers, so whole families sit above or below other families. Within a family there is a ranking too. The hierarchy has two levels: a ranking of families, and a ranking of individuals inside each family.

Cheney and Seyfarth used playback experiments, broadcasting recorded vocalizations from hidden speakers. Baboons have characteristic calls. A dominant animal threatening a subordinate gives a "threat-grunt," and the subordinate answers with a fearful scream. By splicing recordings together, the researchers could create the sound of an interaction that never happened. They could make it sound as though a low-ranking female had threatened a higher-ranking one and the higher-ranking female had screamed in submission.

The baboons noticed. When the playback implied a rank reversal, listeners looked toward the speaker for longer than when it matched the existing order. The more striking result was that reversals *between* families drew much stronger reactions than reversals *within* a family. A sister overtaking a sister was notable. A member of a lower family overtaking a member of a higher one suggested that the order of whole families might be shifting, and that was news.

Put that in other terms. Each baboon carries something that works like a structured representation of its society. Mathematicians would call it a graph, which simply means a network of dots and lines: each dot is an individual, and each line is a relationship. The dots are sorted into what a mathematician would call equivalence classes, which here are just family buckets, and the buckets are ordered by rank, both within themselves and against one another. Each baboon can detect when an observed event breaks that structure, and it can judge *how serious* the break is depending on which level of the structure it touches. No baboon could explain any of this. Every baboon behaves as though it understands it.

## Monkeys and the Mathematics We Recognize

It would be easy to say that monkeys are good at social reasoning and leave it there. Laboratory studies show that the underlying abilities overlap with what we would openly call mathematical cognition.

Monkeys can make **transitive inferences**. Squirrel monkeys trained on overlapping pairs (A beats B, B beats C, C beats D) can work out that B beats D, a pair they have never been shown together. This is the same logic a monkey would need to place two rivals in a hierarchy without seeing them fight directly.

Monkeys also have a sense of number. In a well-known study, Elizabeth Brannon and Herbert Terrace trained rhesus macaques to touch images containing one, two, three, and four items in ascending order. The monkeys then correctly ordered new pairs of quantities from five to nine, which they had never been trained on. They had learned something like an abstract rule about "more" and "fewer," not just a list of specific pictures. Monkeys can compare quantities, and they can even approximately add sets of dots, with error patterns similar to those of humans estimating without counting. Researchers sometimes call this the approximate number system, a sense of quantity that works before and beneath any counting words.

None of this is formal arithmetic. No monkey has written an equation, and it is worth being careful here. The fact that scientists can describe a behavior with mathematics does not mean the animal is doing mathematics the way a student does homework. A falling rock obeys gravity without calculating anything. But a monkey is not a rock. It builds an internal model, revises it, and uses it to predict what will happen next. The raw abilities are real: ordering, comparing, inferring hidden relations, estimating magnitudes. Formal mathematics, a very recent and very human invention, is built on exactly these foundations. It did not appear from nowhere. It grew out of capacities that were already running.

Keep that caution in mind. It is easy to honor in the laboratory and easy to forget once the subject turns to people.

## Culture Without a Classroom

No capuchin has ever studied lever mechanics. Yet in Brazil, bearded capuchins use stones as hammers and anvils to crack hard palm nuts, and they handle the physics with a precision any engineer would recognize. They choose hammer stones of suitable weight, carry them to anvil sites, place each nut so it will not roll, and adjust the force of each strike. That is implicit physics: mass, angle, and impact, solved by feel. It is also optimization. A stone too light wastes effort, and a stone too heavy wastes energy carrying it. The capuchins find the balance.

Young capuchins take years to master the technique, and they spend a lot of time watching skilled adults, usually the best nut-crackers in the group rather than just any adult. Archaeologists working at Serra da Capivara have excavated capuchin stone tools spanning roughly three thousand years. It is a monkey archaeological record, with tool styles that changed over time. Knowledge of this kind does not just live in one animal. It is passed on, and it persists across generations. Each troop is, in effect, a library that has never been written down. Its only copies are kept in living bodies, and it lasts exactly as long as the chain of watchers and learners stays unbroken.

The famous case of passing it on comes from Koshima Island in Japan. In 1953 a young female Japanese macaque named Imo began washing sweet potatoes in water before eating them. Researchers had been leaving the potatoes on the beach. The habit spread, first to her playmates and relatives and then through much of the troop. Older males were among the last to adopt it, which is a pattern familiar from human social life. Later, Imo is reported to have started separating wheat from sand by throwing handfuls into the water, where the sand sank and the grain floated. That is a density separation, a principle of buoyancy put to work by an animal that has never heard the word.

This story, too, deserves a caution. The psychologist Bennet Galef pointed out that potato-washing spread slowly, over most of a decade, not in the quick wave that popular retellings suggest. The macaques also shared a beach, a shoreline, and a supply of sandy potatoes that humans handed out. Some animals may have arrived at washing partly on their own, nudged by the same setting, rather than by copying Imo. Observation surely played a role. It was probably not the only one.

Either way, the knowledge is passed on without explanation. No capuchin lectures its offspring on the relationship between stone mass and impact force. The knowledge moves through watching, trying, and failing, and it settles in the body as skill.

## Fairness, Grievance, and the Cucumber

Monkeys also seem to notice when they are getting the worse end of a deal.

In a widely discussed 2003 experiment, Sarah Brosnan and Frans de Waal trained brown capuchins to trade a small token for a food reward. When two monkeys sat side by side and both received cucumber slices, they traded happily. When one monkey got a grape instead, which capuchins much prefer, the partner still getting cucumber often refused to keep trading. Some threw the cucumber back at the experimenter.

The interpretation is still debated, and so are some of the replications. Part of the reaction may simply be frustrated expectation: the monkey sees a grape and wants one, whoever is getting it. Even with that caution, something worth noticing remains. In cooperative settings like this one, capuchins appear to monitor the terms of an exchange, and those terms seem to include what is happening to the monkey next door. To whatever degree that holds, the animal is running a model of exchange, and it reacts when the model is broken.

Notice also what the capuchin does *not* do. It does not accept praise in place of a grape. That will matter later.

## Deception: The Sophistication of Seeming Simple

The Machiavellian hypothesis got its name for a reason. Wherever there is a social model, there is a chance to exploit it.

Byrne and Whiten collected field reports of what they called **tactical deception**. These were behaviors that seemed designed to mislead other group members to the deceiver's benefit. One well-known account describes a juvenile chacma baboon, Paul, watching an adult female, Mel, dig up a nutritious underground plant. Paul looked around, saw no one else nearby, and screamed as if attacked. His mother came running, saw Mel next to her apparently distressed son, and chased Mel away. Paul ate the plant.

Anecdotes are fragile evidence, and the researchers knew it. More systematic studies have since documented similar behavior. Brandon Wheeler, studying tufted capuchins in Argentina, found that subordinate monkeys sometimes gave alarm calls when no predator was present. They did so especially when food was concentrated and contested. As the others scattered or became distracted, the caller grabbed food it otherwise could not have reached.

We do not need to know whether Paul "intended to deceive" in a richly self-aware sense. We do not need to know whether the capuchin has any explicit belief about what its group-mates believe. The behavior is calibrated anyway. It happens when it pays, it is aimed at the individuals whose reactions matter, and it is used at the moments when the social structure can be bent in the deceiver's favor. Paul screamed when his mother was within earshot and the other adults were not. That is not randomness. Whatever is happening, it is not stupidity.

The deeper point is this. Deception works only because the deceiver's apparent state is easy to read: a frightened child, an alarmed sentinel. The trick lies in presenting a simpler, more innocent version of oneself to an audience that will respond to it predictably. Looking harmless can be a strategy. Hold on to that image of a small, helpless-seeming animal who ends up with the food. We will meet grown-up versions of Paul, and some of them run institutions.

## Knowing-How Without Knowing-That

The philosopher Gilbert Ryle drew a distinction, in 1949, that fits the monkeys almost too well. There is *knowing-that*, which is explicit, statable knowledge of facts, and there is *knowing-how*, which is practical competence that may never be put into words. A child can catch a ball long before she can state Newton's laws. Her inability to formalize the dynamics is not an inability to understand them. In the sense that matters for catching, she understands them very well.

Ryle's distinction needs one refinement to fit the troop. Ryle meant knowing-how as a set of skilled dispositions, a matter of what the body is ready to do, and he was suspicious of talk about hidden inner models. Some of what monkeys know is exactly that kind of skill: the capuchin's strike, the child's catch. But the baboon's hierarchy is something else. It is a map of who stands where, with facts in it that could in principle be stated, even though the baboon never states them. The chemist and philosopher Michael Polanyi had a phrase for this second kind: *tacit knowledge*. "We can know more than we can tell." Monkeys carry both kinds, the skill in the hands and the unspoken map in the head, and so do we.

Monkeys are the clearest demonstration of how far both can go. A baboon has no vocabulary for networks, family buckets, rankings, or what statisticians call Bayesian updating, which just means revising your estimates of what is likely as new evidence comes in. Yet it behaves as if it does all of these things on the fly. It maintains a structured model of its society and reacts with graded surprise to violations at different levels of that structure. A capuchin has no theory of fairness, yet it registers unequal pay and refuses to cooperate. A juvenile baboon has no theory of mind it could describe, yet it stages a performance tuned exactly to its audience.

Ask a monkey to explain any of this and you will get nothing. Change one variable, such as which family the threatening female belongs to, whose mother is nearby, or what the neighbor is being paid, and the monkey adjusts at once. That responsiveness is the evidence. The model is real even though no one could recite it.

## The Mirror

Here the monkeys turn the question back on us.

Humans are primates, and our social lives run on the same kind of machinery, scaled up enormously. In a single day an ordinary person tracks who can be safely interrupted and who cannot. They track which jokes raise their standing in one room and wreck it in another, which rules will be enforced strictly and which only selectively, whose word will be believed, and which explanations will be accepted as innocent. They do this fluently, in real time, across dozens or hundreds of relationships. Described in formal terms, what they are doing looks like inference over a partially observed social network: filling in the lines they cannot see, sorting people into categories, weighing the cost of guessing wrong, and revising constantly.

Many of these same people will tell you, sincerely, that they are "bad at math."

What they usually mean is that they are uncomfortable with a particular notation, the symbols on the whiteboard and the procedures of the classroom. They do not mean they are incapable of building and using structured models of the world. Like the baboon, they run the machinery without a word for it. And like the baboon, their competence becomes obvious the moment their own interests are at stake.

That gap, between what people can do and what they believe they can do, is where the rest of this essay lives.

## Off the Books

Every society decides which kinds of intelligence count. Ours made a particular choice, and for understandable reasons. Modern schooling, modern work, and modern credentials reward the kind of knowledge that can be written down, tested, graded, and transferred to a file. Knowing-that travels well. It fits on an exam, a résumé, a certificate. Knowing-how, and tacit knowledge generally, does not. It lives in hands, timing, and attention, and it disappears the moment you try to put it in a box with a checkmark.

So the genius that does not fit gets kept off the books. It is not denied, exactly. It is simply never entered in the ledger. A person can spend twelve years in school being told, in a hundred small ways, that intelligence means symbol manipulation done quickly under supervision. If that person is slow with symbols, they draw the obvious conclusion. They decide they are not smart. Then they walk out of the classroom and go on doing, every hour of the day, things that no computer could reliably do until very recently and that many still cannot.

This is what it means for a mind to be closeted in modern life. The capacity is there. It is used daily. It is real capital, earning returns for the people and institutions around it. But it is uncredited, kept out of view even from its owner, because the accounts it would appear in have no column for it. The person who has it learns to describe themselves in the vocabulary of the classroom, and that vocabulary only has words for what they lack.

## A Field Guide to Hidden Genius

It helps to go looking for it, the way a primatologist goes looking for baboons. Once you know what to watch for, it is everywhere.

Watch a server in a busy restaurant. She holds the state of eight tables in her head: who has ordered, who is waiting, who is irritated, who is celebrating, which kitchen ticket will come up next, and which route across the floor lets her refill two waters and drop a check in one trip. She is solving a scheduling problem under constant interruption, with incomplete information, while reading faces. If you put the same problem on paper as a word problem, she might tell you she hates word problems.

Watch a cook who never measures. A pinch, a splash, a glance at the color of the onions, a change in the sound of the pan. She is controlling a chemical process by feedback, adjusting heat and timing as the signals change. Engineers have a whole field devoted to this kind of control. She learned it from her grandmother, who also never measured.

Watch a driver merging onto a crowded highway. In a few seconds he estimates the speeds of half a dozen cars, predicts their paths, guesses which drivers will yield and which will not, and picks a gap. He is solving for trajectories and reading intentions at the same time, the way Paul read his mother's position before he screamed.

Watch a nurse on a night shift who looks at a patient and says, "Something is off," before any monitor agrees with her. She is not guessing. She is detecting a pattern across skin tone, breathing, restlessness, and a dozen small cues she could not fully list. Often she is right. Hospitals have learned to take that sentence seriously, even when nobody can write down the formula behind it.

Watch the person in every extended family who keeps the peace at holidays. She knows which uncle cannot sit near which cousin, which old grievance is still live and which has gone quiet, who needs to be asked about their job and who must not be. She is managing a two-level hierarchy, families and the individuals within them, exactly like a baboon female in the Okavango. She would laugh if you called it graph theory. Graph theory is just the formal name for the structure she is navigating.

Watch a teenager read a group chat. A one-word reply, a delayed reaction, a missing emoji: each carries meaning, and the meaning depends on who sent it, to whom, and in front of whom. Teenagers decode this layered signaling instantly and get it right most of the time. Many of them are also failing algebra.

Watch a mechanic listen to an engine, a carpenter sight down a board, a farmer look at the sky, a parent hear one cry from another room and know whether to run. Each is a model, built from experience, compressed into intuition, and fast enough to act on before it could be explained.

None of these people would call what they do mathematics, and they would be partly right. It is not the mathematics of the textbook, and it would be its own kind of condescension to pretend otherwise. But it is built from the same raw materials the monkeys showed us: ordering, comparing, estimating, inferring what cannot be seen, and revising as evidence arrives. The textbook is a late, written-down extension of that older work. It is not the same thing, and it is not foreign to it either.

## Why the Genius Stays Hidden

If the genius is so common, why does it stay off the books? Part of the answer is the classroom. But there are other forces too, and some of them are uncomfortable.

The first is money. Work that can be described can be priced, credited, and promoted. Work that cannot be described tends to be called "soft," "natural," or "just experience," and paid accordingly. The server's scheduling and the family peacekeeper's diplomacy are real cognitive labor, but because they leave no written record, they are easy to treat as if they cost nothing. A great deal of the world runs on knowing-how that nobody has to pay for, because nobody has to admit it exists.

Naming it will not fix that on its own, and it is worth being honest about why. Wages are not set by how complex a job is inside someone's head. They are set by how many people could do it, how expensive it is to replace them, and how much bargaining power they have. The social machinery the monkeys revealed is widespread across our species, which is exactly why the market treats it as cheap. A capuchin who is told her cucumber is very impressive still wants the grape.

The second is identity. "I'm bad at math" is not just a belief. It is often a social position, sometimes even a comfortable one. It explains past failures and excuses future ones. It can signal modesty, or belonging to a group that does not do that sort of thing. People defend it, even against evidence of their own competence, because giving it up would mean revisiting a story they have told about themselves for years.

The third returns us to Paul. Sometimes looking simple is safer. In many settings, visible intelligence draws attention, expectations, and threat. A subordinate who appears to understand too much can be seen as a rival. So people learn, often without deciding to, to keep their competence below the surface: to defer, to play dumb, to let the person above them believe they had the idea first. This is the monkey's oldest trick, and humans are very good at it. The hiding is not always imposed from outside. Sometimes it is a shelter people build for themselves.

## The Shadow of the Same Genius

The machinery that makes us capable also makes us capable of harm, and the monkeys show this too.

A baboon can place a stranger in the hierarchy at a glance, because it compresses individuals into categories. That compression is efficient. In humans it can harden into prejudice: a whole person reduced to a single category, held with the false certainty that comes from having less to think about. The error is not a lack of reasoning. It is reasoning that is too eager. Call it overcompression.

But the analogy has a limit, and it matters. A baboon's family buckets track something real, the actual kinship that determines who will defend whom. Many of the categories humans compress each other into do not track anything of the sort. They are built from history, rumor, and myth, and then treated as if they were nature. And unlike baboons, we are not stuck with tacit knowledge alone. We have language. We can step back, name the shortcut we just took, and ask whether it was fair. That is exactly where our responsibility lies. The primate heuristics come with the body. The duty to audit them, and to override them when they are wrong, comes with the gift of knowing-that.

There is a second shadow, the one Paul foreshadowed. Humans also bend the social machinery the way Paul bent his mother's protectiveness. Sometimes "I didn't understand," or "that's just how things are," or "nobody could have known" works like a false alarm call. It is a display of simplicity that happens to clear the way to the food.

The person need not be consciously lying. The structure can be felt and used without ever being stated, which is exactly what makes it hard to name and hard to hold anyone responsible for. In an institution, the not-knowing can even be spread out, so that no single person ever has to hold the whole picture, and the organization knows more than any member is required to admit. But "not consciously lying" is not the same as "not responsible." A human, unlike a baboon, can be asked to look at the pattern, and can be expected to.

A simple test, borrowed from watching primates, helps here. Pay attention to when the supposed incomprehension disappears. If a leader, or the people running an organization, seem unable to follow cause and effect when the costs fall on those lower in the hierarchy, yet reason sharply about consequences the moment their own status, money, or safety is threatened, then "confusion" stops being a convincing explanation. The baboon who cannot explain the hierarchy still never forgets who outranks whom.

That test can be made more than a private suspicion. Regulators, journalists, and anyone else charged with holding power to account usually look for documented intent, a memo or an email, a piece of knowing-that. But much of what institutions know is never written in that form. It shows up instead in how they behave. So compare the claims. Set an organization's professed confusion about harm to its workers or the public beside the sophistication of its forecasting when executive pay or capital is on the line. Where the self-protective modeling is sharp, a claim of ignorance about the damage done to others deserves to be treated as what Byrne and Whiten would recognize: tactical deception.

The same inner genius that lets a nurse sense a failing patient lets a powerful person sense exactly how much ignorance they can afford to display. Recognizing hidden genius cuts both ways. It means giving people credit for what they can do, and it means refusing to accept that they cannot see what they plainly navigate around.

## Putting It on the Books

What would it look like to take this hidden genius seriously?

It would start with naming. When someone says they are bad at math, it is worth asking what they do all day, and then pointing out, gently, what that work actually involves. Not to flatter them, but to correct the record. The server is scheduling. The family peacekeeper is mapping a hierarchy. The driver is solving for trajectories. Hearing that can change how a person approaches the symbols that once defeated them. A classroom could begin this way on purpose, with something like an audit of what students already know without saying: how their friendships are arranged, how a shift gets covered, how far a throw will carry. Only then would symbols arrive, first tied to the concrete case and then gradually set free of it.

It would continue with honesty about what the symbols add. Formal notation is not a mere transcript of what people already speak. It is scaffolding, a structure that lets us climb past where intuition can reach. Primate intuition is good at hierarchies and trajectories. It is bad at compound interest, at rare-disease probabilities, and at anything too large or too slow to feel. Those are exactly the places where intuition fails and the notation earns its keep. So the bridge from the kitchen table to the equation is real, but it has to be built with patience and support, not just pointed at. Many people who froze at equations come alive when the equation describes their own kitchen, their own shop floor, their own family table. They stay alive when someone helps them carry it across. And there is a civic reason to make the crossing. Statistics and formal reasoning are some of the best tools we have for auditing the troop: for checking the patterns that powerful people would prefer to leave unspoken.

It would include paying for what we use, and here naming is only the first step. Tacit skill earns money when it becomes leverage. Workers and their advocates can make the case that the nurse's "something is off" and the floor manager's quiet peacekeeping are risk management that cannot be replaced, and that every time an experienced person walks out the door, an unwritten library burns with them. Employers who want to keep that library can reward it in ways that do not require turning every intuition into a metric: pay that grows with tenure, bonuses nominated by peers, who recognize a master practitioner as readily as young capuchins pick out the best nut-cracker to watch, and credit in schools and training programs for competence people can demonstrate. The goal is to put this knowledge on the books without forcing it into a spreadsheet that strangles it. Measure it badly, and you end up managing people by dashboard and destroying the very judgment you meant to reward.

And it would include the harder part: using our explicit minds on our implicit ones. The baboon cannot do this. We can. We can take the model we run without words, put words to it, and ask whether it is accurate and whether it is just. That is the one capacity we have that the troop does not. Knowing-how gets us through the day. Knowing-that lets us decide what kind of troop we want to be.

## Conclusion: Respect for the Unspoken

Monkeys deserve study for their own sake. They are a sprawling, varied group of animals: marmosets drinking tree gum, howlers whose calls carry for kilometers across the forest canopy, capuchins with stone tools, macaques in hot springs, baboons with their complex politics. Each troop carries knowledge that exists nowhere else: which stones crack which nuts, which plants are worth digging for, which family sits above which, passed from watching parent to watching child over centuries. When forests fall to logging and monkeys to hunting and the wildlife trade, we are not only losing species. We are burning down libraries that were never written down, collections of social and practical knowledge that took thousands of years to assemble and cannot be recovered from a book.

Monkeys also teach a broader lesson. Intelligence is not the same as the ability to explain oneself. A great deal of real understanding, perhaps most of it, lives in the body and in practice: in catching, in navigating, in sensing where status rises and falls and which boundaries are costly to cross. Explicit theory, when it comes at all, usually comes afterward. It begins as a description of a structure that people already know how to operate, and then, at its best, it carries them somewhere they could not have reached by feel.

That is true of the troop, and it is true of the people around us: the server, the cook, the nurse, the teenager, the relative who keeps the holiday table from exploding. Their genius is real, and much of modern life keeps it closeted, uncredited and off the books. So the next time someone insists they are bad at math, it is worth showing them what they already do. And the next time someone insists they did not understand, or that nobody could have known, it is worth remembering the baboons of the Okavango. They know their world very well, even though they have no words to describe it. We have the words. That leaves us with fewer excuses.
