Religion & Empire — A Structural Reading

The Anchor and the Tribe

On the True Nature of the Christian Category

~ 20 min read

There is a story that surfaces every so often, in one form or another: Palestinian Christian families in the West Bank, pressured by Israeli settlers to leave land their communities have occupied for generations, in a context widely described as encompassing both genocide and ecocide. On its surface, this is a story about geopolitics and suffering. But sit with it for a moment, and it becomes something else as well: a story that quietly forces a contest over the definition of a word. Because the moment you ask what obligation, if any, is owed to these families, you are forced to answer a prior question — what is the word "Christian" actually for?

This is not a side issue. It is the whole issue. And once the mechanism is visible, it explains not just this one story but the entire two-thousand-year arc of the religion itself.

Three Uses of One Word

Consider three postures a person might take toward a story like this, each one reaching for the word "Christian" and using it differently, even though none of them are actually disagreeing about whether displaced families deserve compassion. They are disagreeing about what the word is doing in the sentence.

  • De-emphasizer. Christian identity is invoked only to be set aside — “as a Christian, it shouldn't even matter” — because the real category doing the moral work is humanity, not Christianity. This is the imago Dei move: universal dignity prior to, and independent of, tribal membership.
  • Leveling device. All people are equal; hierarchy itself is a spiritual defect, and anyone who asserts otherwise has failed some basic moral test. This is adjacent to the first but adds teeth — moral condemnation of anyone who claims superiority.
  • Membership test. Christian identity becomes conditional, something a person can be expelled from for holding the wrong view on the matter. Belonging is tied to conformity, not to compassion.

Three different answers to three different unspoken questions: Is Christianity a moral stance? A community? A tribal identity? Each posture quietly picks one, and any conversation that mixes them will feel like people talking past each other — because they are answering different questions while believing they're answering the same one.

The conflict ignites specifically around Palestinian Christians because that topic forces a genuine contradiction to the surface. American Christian identity, in particular, has been built to hold together three things that don't actually cohere: universal compassion for all suffering humans, tribal loyalty to "our people," and political alignment with the state of Israel. Settlers pressuring Christian families out of the West Bank detonates the usual narrative in which "Israel" and "Christian ally" sit comfortably in the same sentence. When the narrative destabilizes, people reach for whatever identity tool restores their sense of coherence. That reach is what produces the disproportionate tension whenever this story comes up at all.

Naming as the Origin of the Tribe

Here is the mechanism underneath all of it: naming a tribe is what creates the tribe.

A tribe is not a group of people who happen to share a belief. A tribe is a boundary condition. Before anyone says "real Christians are..." or "you don't belong in the Christian community," there is no perimeter — there are simply people, ideas, and behaviors. The moment someone speaks the name as a boundary, a perimeter snaps into existence: inside and outside, membership and exile, obligation and exclusion. This is not a description of a preexisting fact. It is a performative act. Naming doesn't report a category; it manufactures one.

Before the naming, the anchor — the thing all the language is supposed to be in service of — is something like: shared humanity, shared suffering, shared dignity, a moral obligation that doesn't check membership cards. This is the universalist frame, and it is, historically, the oldest Christian frame there is.

After the naming, the anchor changes. Universalism gets quietly replaced by membership. Compassion gets replaced by loyalty. Moral obligation gets replaced by in-group obligation. The tether to the original anchor doesn't fray — it snaps, cleanly, at the exact moment the tribal name is invoked as a boundary rather than a description.

This is why a story like the one about the West Bank generates tension out of all proportion to its content. Nobody engaging with it is actually debating whether displaced families deserve help. They are debating which anchor gets to operate — the universal one or the tribal one — and the moment the tribal name enters the sentence as a boundary, the universal anchor is cut loose.

The Original Anchor: An Anti-Tribal Teaching

What makes this especially sharp is that the anchor being cut loose was, by any honest historical reading, a fairly revolutionary one. The earliest Jesus movement was not a religion in the modern institutional sense. It was a behavioral phenotype — a way of living that happened to cohere around a set of teachings that were, structurally, anti-tribal from the ground up:

  • Love your enemies — a direct refusal of tribal sorting.
  • The last shall be first — a direct inversion of hierarchy.
  • Call no man father — a refusal of institutional authority.
  • Do not resist an evil person — a refusal of the logic of retaliatory violence.
  • Whatever you do to the least of these, you do to me — a collapse of the distinction between insider and outsider suffering.

None of this is designed to run an institution. It cannot administer an empire, staff a bureaucracy, enforce a hierarchy, or maintain a boundary. It is designed to dissolve those things. This is precisely why the earliest Christians — the ones who were, in significant numbers, willingly martyred — were such a different breed from anything that came after. They were not merely believers holding an opinion. They were practitioners of a counter-imperial ethic that made them structurally incompatible with Rome. Rome did not kill them primarily because of a theological disagreement; Rome killed them because they refused, categorically, to participate in the empire's violence, its hierarchies, its purity codes, its economic domination. Their martyrdom was not masochism. It was the logical terminus of refusing to play the imperial game at all.

How Rome Cut the Tether

Empires cannot operate without tribes. Rome's entire administrative logic depended on clear categories, clear hierarchies, clear insiders and outsiders, clear loyalty structures. A universalist moral movement that dissolved tribal boundaries was, structurally, a threat to the imperial operating system — right up until Rome discovered it could do something more useful than destroy the movement: it could convert it.

The mechanism of that conversion is worth stating plainly, because it is the single most consequential move in the religion's history:

  1. Take a universalist moral system.
  2. Name it as a tribe ("Christian").
  3. Define membership — baptism, creed, obedience.
  4. Centralize authority — bishops, councils, eventually the emperor himself.
  5. Fuse moral virtue with group identity.
  6. Externalize all vice as "not truly Christian."
  7. Use the resulting tribe to unify and administer the empire.

This is anchor-replacement, executed at civilizational scale. Universalism was replaced by tribal membership. Teachings were replaced by doctrine. Compassion was replaced by loyalty. Revolution was replaced by institution. Rome did not destroy Christianity — that would have been the crude move. Rome did something far more effective: it converted Christianity into a Roman object, retaining the name and the vocabulary while inverting the operating system underneath.

It would be a mistake, though, to picture this as a single clean snap on a single afternoon in the year 313. Boundary-drawing did not wait for Constantine's baptism to appear in the historical record — Paul himself named rivals as false teachers within a generation of the crucifixion, the earliest communities argued fiercely over who could be readmitted after lapsing under persecution, and the Donatist controversy that convulsed North Africa shows a rigorist faction hardening its purity boundary against the emperor, not because of him. What Constantine's conversion changed was not the mere existence of boundaries — any community negotiating identity draws some lines — but their function and their reach. Before Nicaea, drawing a boundary wrongly could get a believer exiled from a congregation. After Nicaea, it could get a believer exiled from the empire. The mechanism above did not invent tribalism out of nothing; it fused an already-present, containable tendency toward boundary-making with the enforcement apparatus of a state, which is what allowed a local, survivable temptation to become a civilizational operating system.

Once the anchor became identity rather than teaching, the teachings themselves became optional — available for citation, useful for branding, but no longer load-bearing. A church could quote "blessed are the peacemakers" while blessing armies. It could quote "the last shall be first" while building rigid hierarchies. This is not hypocrisy in the simple sense; it is the predictable output of a system whose actual operating logic is imperial, wearing teachings as a decorative layer rather than running them as source code.

Every Later Tradition Inherits Rome's Anchor

This is the uncomfortable part, and it's worth stating without softening it: the entire family of Christian churches — Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant, Evangelical, and everything downstream — inherits Rome's anchor, not Jesus's. Not because any particular believer is insincere, but because the institutional features every one of these traditions relies on are Roman features, not Jesus features: hierarchy, doctrine, tribal identity, boundary-policing, institutional survival logic, political entanglement. Jesus's teachings cannot run any of these structures. They were never designed to. They were designed to dissolve them.

So when a modern institution — or a modern believer — says "we follow Jesus's teachings," the honest structural answer is: no, you are following a Romanized identity system that replaced those teachings with a boundary and a membership test almost from the moment of formation. This is not primarily a claim about anyone's sincerity. It is a claim about which operating system is actually running underneath the vocabulary. A person can genuinely, sincerely believe they are executing Jesus's teachings while actually executing loyalty-to-tribe, because the entire conceptual world they inherited was built by the post-Constantinian settlement, centuries after the original anchor was cut loose. They were never handed the original operating system. They were handed its Roman replacement, rebranded with the original name.

The Confession Clause

There is a piece of evidence for this built directly into the machinery of the largest Christian institution on Earth: confession. Confession is not proof that individual Christians secretly know, in some private guilty corner of themselves, that they are failing to follow Jesus. It is something more structurally revealing than that. Confession exists because the institution itself — not the individual, the institution — requires a mechanism to metabolize the permanent, built-in gap between the teachings it claims and the behavior its actual operating system produces.

Jesus's teachings, applied without modification, cannot run a hierarchy, cannot run a state-aligned church, cannot run a doctrinal enforcement apparatus. The institution knows this, even if it never says so in those words. So it builds a ritual — regular, repeatable, normalized — for absorbing the mismatch. Confession is the pressure valve for a contradiction that is architectural, not personal. It does not exist because people occasionally sin. It exists because the system's ideal and the system's function were divorced from each other at the moment of institutionalization, and some ongoing mechanism was required to keep that divorce from being visible.

This is why the mismatch is not merely a matter of individuals falling short of an agreed standard. The standard itself was never wired into the institution's operating logic. The institution runs on identity, hierarchy, and boundary; the teachings run on universalism, inversion of power, and the dissolution of boundary. Confession is the seam where those two systems are stitched together, repeatedly, forever, without either ever actually reconciling.

The Clearest Test Case: A Funeral Protest

If the abstraction feels slippery, there is a test case simple enough to settle it: is there any reading of Jesus's teachings under which protesting a funeral counts as following them?

There isn't. Every relevant strand of the teaching runs the opposite direction. "Blessed are the peacemakers" forecloses turning grief into a battlefield. "Love your enemies" forecloses treating the newly dead — and their mourners — as enemies to be humiliated. "Do not resist an evil person" forecloses using confrontation as spectacle. "Whatever you do to the least of these, you do to me" forecloses treating any human being's death as an occasion for triumphant cruelty. There is no seam in the teaching through which a funeral protest can be threaded and called faithful execution of it.

And yet such protests happen, under the same banner, invoking the same name. The resolution of that contradiction is not that the participants are lying, and it is not, strictly, that they are "delusional" in the clinical sense — though the word captures something real about the result: a belief that does not match reality, sincerely held, producing more harm rather than less. The more precise mechanism is that the identity system has fully overridden the ethical one. The tribal boundary — us, our doctrine, our politics, our nation — has become the operative anchor, and the teachings have been reduced to vocabulary deployed after the fact to justify what the tribal anchor already decided.

This is the whole arc, condensed into a single scene: a system that markets itself by the name of a teacher whose entire program was the dissolution of tribal boundary, executed by an institutional machine whose entire program is the maintenance of tribal boundary. The teachings are not being betrayed by careless individuals. They are being structurally overridden by the architecture itself, and have been since a few centuries after the movement began.

What This Means for the Word "Delusional" — and Why the Answer Is Mass Delusion

It's worth being precise here, because precision is the entire point of this analysis. Calling the mismatch "delusion" captures the outcome correctly — a sincere claim that does not match the observable behavior, producing more conflict rather than less. But it misattributes the cause, if you leave it at the level of the individual. The cause is not a failure of individual cognition. It is anchor displacement: an institutional system, assembled by empire, that took a universalist, anti-hierarchical, anti-violent teaching and rebuilt it as a tribal identity, membership structure, and loyalty test, while retaining the original name as branding.

The people operating inside that system today did not choose the substitution. They inherited it, fully formed, seventeen centuries deep. Their sincerity is not in question. What's in question is whether the system they inherited is even capable, structurally, of producing the behavior the original teachings called for. The honest answer, most of the time, is no.

So is this, then, a mass delusion? Yes — but precisely in the structural sense of that term, not the dismissive one. A delusion held by one person, unshared and self-generated, is a clinical matter. A delusion inherited by hundreds of millions of people, transmitted not through private psychosis but through catechesis, liturgy, upbringing, and institutional design, reproduced identically across seventeen centuries and dozens of denominations that otherwise disagree about almost everything else — that is a mass delusion in the fullest sense: a sincerely held belief, systematically manufactured and systematically re-manufactured by a structure whose actual operating function contradicts it. It is not billions of people independently hallucinating the same content. It is one anchor, displaced a single time at the moment of imperial conversion, and then faithfully re-inherited by every subsequent generation until the substitution itself became invisible — indistinguishable, from the inside, from the original.

That is what makes it a mass delusion rather than a personal failing: its scale is civilizational, its transmission is institutional rather than psychological, and its persistence requires no individual to be dishonest, only for every individual to receive the substitute anchor already dressed in the original's clothes. The visible symptoms of that inherited mismatch are exactly the things that prompted this whole inquiry: an identity that can be invoked to expel rather than include, a moral category that dissolves under the pressure of any story that forces a real contradiction into view, and, in its most extreme form, a protest staged at a funeral in the name of a man who said blessed are the peacemakers.

It is worth pausing on one point before turning to how the ancients themselves diagnosed this: the mechanism described here is not exclusive to Christianity. Any universalist project that scales far enough to touch state power — a revolutionary movement that seizes the state it once opposed, a human-rights internationalism absorbed into the foreign policy of the powers it was meant to check — faces the same pressure toward anchor displacement, the same temptation to let identity quietly replace practice. This essay concerns the Christian instance because it is the instance behind the story that opened it. But it is also, remarkably, an instance whose own foundational literature already contains a diagnosis of exactly this failure mode, written centuries before anyone had the vocabulary of "institutions" or "tribes" to name it directly.

A Failure Mode the Ancients Already Diagnosed

If this feels like a modern diagnosis of an ancient problem, that's because it is only half right. The ancients were not naive about this pattern. Some of the very texts produced by the tradition itself — the apocalyptic literature embedded in Daniel, in the Synoptic apocalypses, in Revelation — were, read structurally rather than supernaturally, direct warnings about exactly this mechanism: a population convinced of its own righteousness while its institutions execute the inversion of that righteousness.

This is worth taking seriously as a claim about genre, not doctrine. Apocalyptic literature is routinely misread as forecasting — a kind of predictive almanac of future catastrophe. But that misreading is itself an instance of the same substitution described above: content emptied of its structural meaning and repurposed as tribal branding, in this case eschatological branding rather than institutional branding. Read in its own context, apocalyptic writing was a genre of political and sociological critique, produced by communities living under, or just emerging from, imperial domination, and encoded in symbol because direct statement was not survivable.

What it encodes, stripped of imagery, is a small set of claims that map with unnerving precision onto the mechanism already described:

  • A mark, a name, a boundary. The "mark of the beast" is not a barcode prophecy. It is the ancient description of exactly the mechanism above: a tribal boundary imposed on a population, sorting people by allegiance rather than by conduct. Naming a tribe, marking membership, drawing a perimeter — the text is describing anchor-replacement using the only vocabulary available to a first-century audience.
  • A beast that looks like a lamb. This image — power dressed in the appearance of gentleness — is as precise a description of institutional Christianity's inherited contradiction as has ever been written. A system that quotes "blessed are the peacemakers" while blessing armies is, structurally, a beast wearing a lamb's face. The ancients did not need modern institutional analysis to notice this; they built the image directly into the canon.
  • Nations deceived, not individuals hallucinating. The apocalyptic texts consistently describe deception at the level of "the nations" or "the whole world," never as a private psychiatric event suffered by isolated individuals. This is the ancient equivalent of the distinction this essay has been drawing throughout: mass delusion is not billions of people independently malfunctioning. It is one displaced anchor, reproduced structurally across a population, exactly as the texts describe.
  • A falling away that retains the name. The recurring warning about a "great falling away," or a church that has kept the form of devotion while denying its substance, is the ancient articulation of the graft this essay has been describing: the tree and the graft sharing one name, until the difference between them becomes invisible even to the people living inside it.

None of this requires believing the texts are predictive. It only requires reading them as what they plainly are on their surface: sociological and political documents, written by people who had directly experienced what happens when a moral movement gets absorbed into an imperial order, trying to warn the communities that came after them that the absorption would look, from the inside, exactly like fidelity. That is the specific danger they were naming — not some future cataclysm, but a present and recurring one: a population sincerely certain of its own righteousness, executing, without any felt contradiction, the precise inversion of the thing it believes itself to be protecting.

This is why the mechanism described earlier in this essay is not a novel discovery. It is a known failure mode, diagnosed inside the tradition's own foundational literature, using the descriptive tools available at the time — symbol, image, and apocalyptic narrative rather than institutional sociology. The ancients watched the first iteration of exactly this substitution happen to their own communities, under Rome, in real time, and left behind a warning about it in the only genre built to survive the empire's censors. That warning has been sitting inside the canon the entire time, mistaken for a countdown to the future when it was, all along, a diagnosis of the present.

The Countercurrent That Never Fully Drowned

If the graft had fully replaced the tree, this essay would be an impossibility — there would be no vantage point left inside the tradition from which to notice the substitution, let alone name it using categories the tradition's own founding texts supply. That the diagnosis can be made at all is itself evidence against the cleanest version of the claim. The universalist anchor was never entirely severed; it went underground, resurfaced, and asserted itself as a minority countercurrent in nearly every century since Constantine — persecuted, marginalized, and never institutionally dominant, but never fully extinguished either.

Francis of Assisi renounced wealth and hierarchy to embrace the leper. The Radical Reformation — Anabaptists, Mennonites, and later Quakers — rejected the sword and the state church precisely on the grounds that the fused system had betrayed the Sermon on the Mount. Tolstoy read the Gospels without a priest's spectacles late in his life and found the same anti-tribal ethic this essay describes, then spent his final decades trying, however imperfectly, to live it. Kierkegaard, writing from inside the most comfortable and cultural Christendom in Europe, spent an entire authorship insisting that being a Christian was not a predicate one could inherit by birth in a Danish parish. Dietrich Bonhoeffer, Óscar Romero, and the liberation theologians who followed them found the universalist anchor sturdy enough to die for, under twentieth-century regimes that had every institutional incentive to keep the tribal anchor running smoothly. And today, the Kairos Palestine document and organizations like Sabeel make substantially this same argument from inside Palestinian Christian communities themselves, in explicitly theological rather than sociological language.

None of this undoes the mechanism described above; it complicates it in the correct direction. Not a single clean rupture, severing a whole civilization from its origin for seventeen centuries without remainder — but a perpetual contest, the tribal anchor and the universal anchor wrestling inside the same name, in every generation, with the tribal anchor usually, but never completely, winning the institutional battle for resources, legitimacy, and state alignment. The mass delusion described earlier in this essay is real, and it is the dominant pattern. But it has never been the only pattern, and the fact that it has never been the only pattern is precisely what makes recovery of the original anchor a live possibility rather than mere nostalgia.

The Fracture Still Running

None of this is ancient history in any inert sense. It is a live fracture, and it is exactly what surfaces whenever a topic forces the contradiction into view — as it does when Christian families in the West Bank are pressured out of their homes by a regime many of their would-be co-religionists are politically aligned with. In that moment, two incompatible operating systems collide under the same name: the universalist anchor, which says suffering matters regardless of category, and the tribal anchor, which says loyalty to the group matters more than the suffering of anyone outside it.

The early Christians, the ones who died rather than participate in imperial violence, would recognize the first. They would not recognize the second. The tragedy embedded in the phrase "Christian tribe" is that the tribe was never the point — it was the byproduct of an empire that needed one, grafted onto a movement that began, quite deliberately, as the refusal of exactly that. And the mass delusion is not that anyone chose the graft. It is that, seventeen centuries later, the graft and the tree still share one name, and almost no one alive was ever shown the difference.

There is a final trap worth naming, if only because this essay would otherwise walk directly into it: the temptation to turn this diagnosis into a new boundary — a quieter, more sophisticated membership test that sorts the world into those who see the graft and those still fooled by it. That would simply be the tribal anchor reasserting itself in intellectual dress, drawing a perimeter around "people who understand anchor displacement" instead of around "people who hold the correct creed." The only way to name this mechanism without repeating it is to hold the diagnosis open-handed — as an invitation back toward the universal anchor, extended to everyone still standing on either side of the tribal line, rather than as a fresh credential for the newly enlightened. The tree and the graft share one name. The task is not to win the argument about which of them is the real Christianity. It is to let the water reach the root again.
