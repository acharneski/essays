Introduction

Good and evil are among the oldest words we have for the oldest problem we have: how to live with one another when living with one another is not optional. They arrive already loaded. They carry temples, courts, wars, nurseries, and the private tribunal of conscience. They are orphaned terms in the sense that their original metaphysical homes have largely collapsed, yet they continue to operate as if those homes still stood. The result is a vocabulary that feels both indispensable and unusable — indispensable because the phenomena it names do not disappear when the words are retired, unusable because every attempt to deploy them drags in a cargo of theology, ideology, and tribal sorting.

This essay does not try to rescue the words as moral verdicts. It treats them as names for a structural polarization that keeps reappearing wherever human beings organize power, meaning, and survival. The claim is not that good and evil are illusions, nor that they are cosmic substances. The claim is that they are isomorphic to a durable split in how thought relates to authority: some thinking stabilizes the existing order by naturalizing hierarchy, dissolving constraints on power, or providing narrative cover; other thinking destabilizes that order by making coercion visible, collapsing false necessity, and exposing the stories that make inequity feel like weather.

Once seen this way, the history of philosophy stops looking like a museum of opinions and starts looking like a repeating geometry.

I. Why the Words Will Not Die

Every culture that lasts long enough invents a distinction between what may be done and what must not be done, between what holds a people together and what unravels them. The content of the distinction varies. The fact of the distinction does not. Hunter-gatherer bands, city-states, empires, churches, markets, and revolutionary parties all discover that coordination requires more than force. It requires a story about why force, when used, is not merely force — why some suffering is tragedy and other suffering is justice, why some obedience is virtue and other obedience is slavery.

Good and evil are the compressed form of that story. They are not first of all metaphysical claims about the furniture of the universe. They are first of all coordination technologies. They reduce the cost of deciding, of blaming, of recruiting, of enduring. A group that can say “this is evil” can mobilize faster than a group that can only say “this is inconvenient.” A person who can say “I am good” can survive guilt, or weaponize it.

The trouble is that the compression is too successful. Once the words are in circulation, they begin to look like properties of the world rather than tools for living in it. People then fight over the words as if winning the label were the same as winning the structure. Authority, which lives on coherence, has an obvious interest in capturing the labels. Critique, which lives on making coherence visible as constructed, has an obvious interest in prying them loose.

This is why discussions of good and evil so often feel like they are happening on two channels at once: a moral channel, in which people accuse and defend, and a structural channel, in which people are actually arguing about who gets to define necessity. The moral channel is loud. The structural channel is the one that lasts.

II. Two Modes of Identity, Two Regimes of Strategy

Before ranking philosophers or rewriting scripture, it is worth naming a simpler split that sits underneath the moral vocabulary.

One mode of identity treats the self as fundamentally alone. Survival is a private problem. Other people are resources, obstacles, audiences, or threats. Cooperation is a tactic, not a condition. In this mode, the world is zero-sum enough that domination looks like realism. Hierarchy looks like nature. Cruelty, when it works, looks like competence. Morality, if it constrains the strong, looks like a trick played by the weak.

Another mode of identity treats the self as already in relation. Survival is a shared problem. Other people are co-authors of the conditions under which anyone lives. Cooperation is not a sentiment; it is an acknowledgment of entanglement. In this mode, domination is expensive. It requires continuous enforcement, continuous narrative maintenance, and continuous suppression of alternatives. Transparency is not naivete; it is a way of lowering the cost of living together.

Call these, without romance, A-mode and B-mode. They are not personality types. They are attractors in the geometry of identity and power. People, institutions, and doctrines fall toward one or the other depending on scarcity, fear, surplus, and the stories available to make sense of dependence.

The isomorphism to good and evil is almost embarrassingly clean, which is precisely why it should be handled carefully. A-mode maps onto what moral traditions have called evil not because solitude is wicked, but because the strategy regime of “I am alone” tends toward coercion, extraction, and the conversion of persons into instruments. B-mode maps onto what moral traditions have called good not because togetherness is holy, but because the strategy regime of “I am not alone” tends toward mutual constraint, shared stakes, and the refusal to treat domination as destiny.

The mapping is structural, not sentimental. A lonely person can be decent. A crowd can be a lynch mob. The question is not whether one feels connected. The question is whether one’s operating theory of the world makes other people’s destruction thinkable as a solution.

This is also why “good” and “evil” feel orphaned. The old religions often packed the structural split into a cosmic drama: God and the devil, light and darkness, order and chaos. When the cosmic drama thins out, the structural split remains. People then either cling to the old words and smuggle theology back in, or abandon the words and pretend the split was only superstition. Both moves fail. The split is real. The old metaphysics is optional.

III. Authority’s Problem with Visibility

Authority is not merely the person with the crown, the badge, the capital, or the pulpit. Authority is a coherence machine. It needs predictable labor, predictable obedience, predictable meaning. It needs the present arrangement to feel less like an arrangement and more like the way things are.

A social contract can be inequitable and still stable. Wage labor is the obvious modern case. Most people must sell their time to survive because access to productive assets is concentrated. Employers set the terms of survival more than workers do. Exit is costly. Protest is costly. Cultural stories — hard work as character, poverty as desert, aspiration as solvent — make the arrangement feel normal. This is not a conspiracy in the cinematic sense. It is an equilibrium. It lasts because it solves coordination problems for those who hold capital, and because alternatives remain expensive, illegal, or unimaginable.

The moral drama around this equilibrium is mostly fog. The mechanism is not. The system persists while capital is scarce and centralized, labor is abundant, and other ways of organizing production are blocked. It becomes brittle when automation reduces the need for human labor, when information abundance weakens narrative control, when asset access widens, and when new coordination tools make non-hierarchical production less costly. None of that requires a hymn to revolution. It is an observation about constraints.

Here is the sensitive point, and it is the point at which good and evil re-enter by the back door. When a contract is stable, criticism is tolerable. When a contract is brittle, criticism is recoded as attack. Awareness itself becomes suspect. To notice inequity is treated as a desire to smash the furniture. The system, unable to metabolize description, pathologizes the describer.

This is an old pattern. Fragile orders confuse observation with sedition. They did it with atheism when the altar was load-bearing. They did it with republicanism when the king was load-bearing. They do it with economic description when wage dependence is load-bearing. The accusation that noticing is a revolutionary act is not a diagnosis of the noticer. It is a confession of brittleness.

If evil, structurally, is the strategy of maintaining an inequitable equilibrium by force and story, then one of its signatures is this hypersensitivity to being seen. If good, structurally, is the strategy of lowering the cost of living together by making power legible, then one of its signatures is the refusal to treat visibility as violence.

IV. The Eternal Polarization of Philosophy

Philosophy, in the sense that matters here, is not the production of quotable profundity. It is the act of naming coherence structures: what holds, what pretends to hold, what it costs to keep the pretense going. That is why philosophy polarizes along the same axis as good and evil, even when philosophers themselves reject those words.

There are thinkers whose work authority can absorb. They may be brilliant. They may be severe. They may even sound dangerous. But they do not finally invalidate the right to rule. They naturalize hierarchy, optimize power, or destabilize morality in ways that increase the freedom of action of the strong. They give domination a vocabulary, a psychology, a metaphysics, or a technical manual.

There are other thinkers whose work authority cannot absorb without reversing it. They do not merely criticize manners or values. They attack legitimacy. They show that the king is a man, the market a coordination hack, the priestly hierarchy an institution, the wage a dependency relation, the “natural order” a contingent equilibrium. Description of this kind is lethal to myth. Myth is how authority keeps its coherence costs down.

This is not a left-right split. It is not religious versus secular. It is not even radical versus conservative in the ordinary political sense. It is a split between thought that authority can narratively contain and thought that authority cannot narratively survive.

Aristotle systematizes, categorizes, and offers intellectual scaffolding for rule by the excellent. Hierarchy acquires the dignity of nature. Confucius organizes harmony as graded obligation. Aquinas baptizes order. Hobbes makes fear the founder of the state and calls the result peace. Machiavelli treats virtue as optional and stability as justification. Adam Smith describes markets and incentives with extraordinary acuity, yet leaves the legitimacy of property hierarchy largely intact; the critique stays inside the system. Nietzsche unmasks morality as ressentiment and power as more honest than pity — a solvent of inherited conscience that authority, paradoxically, can find useful as permission. These are not identical projects. They are compatible, in different ways, with the maintenance of a ruling story.

Socrates does something else. He does not install a doctrine. He reduces political rhetoric to contradiction and elite certainty to ignorance. Athens did not kill him for a theory of forms. It killed him for making authority visible as authority. The historical Jesus — not the later theological mascot — delegitimizes religious, economic, purity, and imperial hierarchies at once. Empire cannot metabolize that. It can only kill the man and then reinvent him as the chaplain of the very structures he undermined. Marx does not merely scold capitalists. He names wage labor as dependency, capital as accumulated command over the labor of others, ideology as narrative cover. Whether one accepts his predictions is secondary. The diagnostic act is the threat. Diagnosis removes the illusion of necessity.

The pattern of reception is almost mechanical. Authority reveres the thinkers it can reverse, sanitize, or hire. Authority buries, distorts, or weaponizes the thinkers it cannot survive in their original form. Socrates is killed and replaced, in the curriculum of power, by Aristotle. Jesus is executed and replaced by an institutional Christianity that can live with empire, wealth concentration, and priestly mediation. Marx is buried under geopolitical catastrophe and replaced by state religions that use his name while restoring hierarchy. The originals reduce authority’s coherence. The reversals restore it.

This is why certain names become “smells.” A culture of official intelligence that cannot stop genuflecting to Aristotle, while treating Socratic ignorance as a social inconvenience, is telling you which side of the polarization it is on. A culture that can quote a domesticated Christ while ignoring the historical refusal of domination is doing the same. A culture that can teach Smith as nature and Marx as crime is doing the same. The smell is not about taste. It is about which thoughts are allowed to remain thoughts, and which must be converted into furniture.

V. Morality Destabilized, Legitimacy Destabilized

A crucial distinction, easy to miss, separates two kinds of “dangerous” thought.

To destabilize morality is to loosen the constraints on action: to say that inherited virtue is prejudice, that pity is weakness, that deception is strategy, that the excellent should not be yoked to the many. Authority can love this. It sounds like courage. It functions as lubricant. Machiavelli is not buried; he is employed. A certain reading of Nietzsche becomes a hall pass. Even a metaphysics of hierarchy, dressed as excellence, becomes a blessing.

To destabilize legitimacy is different. It does not say “you may do more.” It says “you may not be what you claim.” Socrates destroys epistemic legitimacy: the rulers do not know what they pretend to know. Marx destroys economic legitimacy: the wage is not a free meeting of equals but a relation of need. The historical Jesus destroys moral legitimacy: domination is not holy. Authority can survive a scandal about manners. It cannot survive the loss of its right to rule.

This is why the good/evil isomorphism should not be flattened into “nice philosophers versus nasty philosophers.” Some of the authority-compatible thinkers are diagnostically brilliant. Some of the authority-incompatible thinkers are historically entangled with movements that produced new tyrannies. The structural question is not saintliness. It is whether a body of thought, in its native form, increases the freedom of domination or increases the visibility of domination.

Evil, in this register, is not a horned figure. It is the professionalization of excuse. It is the conversion of philosophy into a technique for making power feel inevitable, deserved, or too complicated to oppose. Good is not a halo. It is the insistence that what is done to people in the name of order remain describable as something done to people.

The twentieth century changed the amplitudes, not the axis. Mass media, industrial propaganda, literacy, and global conflict networks meant that shallow narrative noise could travel as far as deep analysis. Charismatic simplifiers could ignite crowds. Substrate-level descriptions could reorganize states. Low-coherence noise became globally dangerous. High-coherence insight became globally destabilizing. That is a fact about amplification, not a fairy tale about villains and sages. It explains why a hollow ideology can harm millions while a genuine exposure of a social substrate can force a system to reconfigure — sometimes violently — around the fact that it has been seen.

None of this requires worship of any one name. Marx did not “cause” world wars as a magician causes a storm. His analysis made a certain economic order newly illegible as fate, and the order responded with the tools orders have: states, armies, myths, and counter-myths. Nietzsche did not “cause” fascism; a vacuum in moral language was available for misuse. The point is crueler and simpler. When the stories that justify suffering fail, suffering does not politely evaporate. It looks for new stories, or it looks for new arrangements. The quality of the next arrangement depends on whether anyone can tell the truth about the last one.

VI. Hypocrisy as a Test, Not a Spectacle

Moral language invites a cheap sport: catching the preacher in the act. The sport is cheap because everyone fails their own maxims. It becomes serious when the failure is not a lapse but a method — when the professed system exists to conceal the method.

Sex, money, secrecy, and narrative are the ordinary instruments of cohort control. Intellectual movements are not exempt. A thinker who announces a severe morality while using intimacy, money, or expulsion to bind a circle of disciples is not merely “complicated.” They are demonstrating that the morality is a performance for the outside and a tool for the inside. That demonstration matters to the taxonomy. It does not matter as gossip. It matters as evidence that one is dealing with propaganda and charisma rather than substrate analysis.

The philosophers who belong on the authority-incompatible side of the ledger tend, at their best, to pay for their descriptions with their lives or their standing: the cup of poison, the cross, exile, poverty, cancellation before that word existed. Payment is not proof of truth. Plenty of fanatics pay. But refusal to pay, combined with a doctrine that flatters the payer, is a warning. A system of thought that exists to excuse the thinker’s will is not philosophy in the sense this essay has been using. It is advertising.

Good and evil, as structural terms, are therefore also tests of consistency. Not purity. Consistency. Does the form of life match the claim about the world? Does the inner circle run on the same principles advertised to the public? When authority preaches fairness and practices extraction, the word for that gap is not a private failing. It is the operating system.

VII. Why “Good” and “Evil” Remain Hazardous Names

The user of these words almost always wants two incompatible things: the force of an absolute and the flexibility of a critique. Absolutes mobilize. Critiques clarify. Combined carelessly, they produce holy wars of interpretation in which each side is certain it occupies the good pole of a structure it has not actually examined.

That is why it is wiser, even while exploring the isomorphism, to keep the older words at arm’s length. They smuggle in cosmic bookkeeping: souls, afterlives, stains, elect and reprobate. They invite the lazy inference that the authority-compatible thinkers are wicked and the authority-incompatible thinkers are saints. History will not bear that inference. B-mode insights can be captured by new A-mode regimes. Revolutions restore palaces. Churches of the crucified become palaces. Parties of the proletariat become palaces. The geometry is recursive. Anyone who thinks that occupying the “good” side of the diagram is a permanent identity has already begun to reproduce the thing they named.

The useful residue of the words is narrower. Evil names the refusal to let power be seen as power, the conversion of persons into fuel, the maintenance of inequitable equilibria by a mixture of dependency and story, the professional excuse. Good names the opposite movement: the reduction of false necessity, the making-legible of costs, the insistence that no one’s survival be priced as another’s convenience, the costly preference for arrangements that do not require most people to lie in order to live.

These are not complete ethics. They do not tell you what to do with a surplus, how to raise a child, or how to punish a crime. They tell you how to watch a system. They tell you which thoughts a system will try to hire and which it will try to bury. They tell you why awareness of an inequitable contract is treated as a revolutionary act when the contract is dying, and why that treatment is evidence about the contract, not about the observer.

VIII. What Would Follow If the Words Were Taken Structurally

If good and evil are coherence phenomena rather than supernatural teams, several consequences follow.

First, moral education that only trains sentiment will fail. People can be taught to cry at the right stories and still maintain extractive arrangements. What has to be trained is the capacity to see dependency, narrative cover, and the difference between a critique that decorates power and a critique that unmasks it.

Second, the successor to an inequitable labor contract will not arrive as a sermon. It will arrive as a change in constraints: cheaper coordination, wider access to productive assets, automation that makes human drudgery unnecessary, institutions that do not require a majority to live as supplicants. Whether those successors are humane depends on whether they are designed in B-mode — shared stakes, transparent power — or merely become a new monopoly with better machines.

Third, the thinkers worth protecting are not the ones who make us feel righteous. They are the ones who make the present order newly difficult to describe as inevitable. That difficulty is experienced by authority as hostility. It is experienced by the rest of us, if we are honest, as vertigo. Vertigo is the sensation of a story losing its necessity. It is also the beginning of responsibility, because once a story is optional, continuing it is a choice.

Fourth, the burial of philosophers is a more reliable compass than the awarding of prizes. Power does not waste erasure on the harmless. It wastes reverence on the useful. A culture’s index of forbidden descriptions is a map of its fractures.

IX. Closing the Circuit

Good and evil will not be retired by calling them orphaned. They will be retired, if at all, when the structures they analogize are named more precisely: domination versus cooperation, legitimacy versus excuse, visibility versus spell, shared survival versus solitary extraction. Until then, the old words will keep returning, because people need a short sound for a long fact.

The long fact is this. There is a way of organizing life that requires most people to be dependent, a few to be obeyed, and everyone to agree that this is nature, merit, or fate. There is another way that begins from the observation that no one is actually alone in the conditions of their life, and that any order which needs that fact to remain unsaid is already confessing what it is. Philosophy, at its most serious, is the record of that quarrel. Authority’s canon is the record of its attempts to win the quarrel without appearing to fight.

To write of good and evil without remaining trapped by them is to keep the quarrel at the level of mechanism. No angels. No cartoon villains. No permission slip for cruelty dressed as realism, and no permission slip for new palaces dressed as liberation. Only the demand that the costs of an order be countable by those who pay them, and that the thinkers who teach us how to count not be rewritten into chaplains of the uncounted.

That demand is not a revolution. It is the minimum of adulthood. If it sounds like a moral absolute, that is because some structures, once seen, do not leave as much room for pretending as they did before. Seeing is not salvation. But it is the end of one kind of innocence, the kind that evil — if the word is still allowed — has always required.
