Abstract

Political history is often narrated as a pendulum: order versus chaos, liberalism versus nationalism, reason versus passion. This document proposes that the pendulum model is a projection artifact — a two-dimensional shadow cast by a three-body system. We introduce a formal triad of political forces — Universalism (U), Tribalism (T), and Parasitism (P) — and show that treating them as three coupled fields, rather than two poles, resolves a set of long-standing puzzles: why universalist movements collapse rapidly after their founding, why tribal identity persists no matter how thoroughly it is argued against, why atrocities committed at sufficient scale exit the moral vocabulary entirely, and why every "end of history" moment has so far been temporary. The result is not a moral theory but a dynamical one — a set of coupled equations describing how coherence, cohesion, and extraction compete for the same finite pool of civilizational energy. What follows should be read as a synthesis and formalization of existing, separately well-supported findings across political science, anthropology, and institutional economics, offered as a common vocabulary for those findings rather than as a finished, independently validated theory — a distinction we return to explicitly in Section 10.

1. Introduction: The Failure of Two-Pole Models

The standard historical model treats political change as oscillation between two attractors:

  • Reason vs. Feeling (Enlightenment vs. Romanticism)
  • Universalism vs. Nationalism
  • Order vs. Chaos
  • Openness vs. Closure

This model has predictive value — it correctly anticipates that periods of abstract, rule-based coordination will eventually provoke an identity-based backlash, and vice versa. But it fails to explain three classes of observation:

  1. Early corruption. Universalist movements (Axial-age religious ethics, Enlightenment liberalism, 20th-century internationalism) do not degrade gradually under tribal pressure. They are captured almost immediately by something that is neither universalist nor tribal — a bureaucratic, extractive, narrative-controlling layer that outlives both the founding ideology and its tribal antagonists.
  2. Asymmetric moral vocabulary. The same act, at different scales, receives radically different moral labeling — not gradually, but as though crossing a threshold. A two-pole model of "more or less tribal" cannot explain a discontinuous collapse of vocabulary.
  3. Non-convergence. If history were a genuine oscillation between two poles, amplitude should decay over time as institutions learn (a damped pendulum). Instead, the amplitude appears roughly constant or even growing across eras, suggesting the system is not damped at all — it is being re-energized by something outside the U/T axis.

A third force is required. We call it Parasitism (P), and we show that once it is added, the system becomes a proper three-body problem: chaotic, non-convergent, and structurally incapable of settling into a fixed point — which is itself the point.

1.1 Relationship to Prior Work

None of the three observations above is, individually, new. Robert Michels' "iron law of oligarchy" (1911) already documented the tendency of egalitarian and revolutionary organizations to generate entrenched, self-perpetuating leadership castes almost immediately upon founding. Mancur Olson's logic of collective action (1965) explained why small, concentrated interests reliably capture the benefits that large, diffuse coalitions are meant to enjoy. Karl Polanyi's "double movement" (1944) described the recurring societal backlash against unregulated market liberalization — a structural precursor to the tribal-resurgence dynamic in Section 2.2. Robert Klitgaard's corruption identity, corruption = monopoly + discretion − accountability (1988), is a direct institutional-scale ancestor of the η/θ coefficients governing Parasitism below. Francis Fukuyama's account of "repatrimonialization" (2014) traces almost exactly the U→P slide this model formalizes. Peter Turchin's cliodynamics (2003, 2016) already treats political stability as a quantifiable dynamical system subject to structural-demographic cycles.

What this document adds, if anything, is not a new phenomenon but a proposed common state space in which these previously separate literatures' variables can be expressed as coupled terms in a single system. The justification for the exercise is unification and precision of vocabulary, not discovery of a hidden force nobody had noticed. Readers already familiar with this literature should treat U, T, and P as relabelings — with an explicit dynamical coupling attached — of phenomena those authors documented independently and, in most cases, more rigorously.

2. The Three Fundamental Forces

2.1 Universalism (U): The Coherence Field

Universalism is the tendency of a political system toward symmetric, abstract, rule-based coordination. Its defining properties:

  • Moral symmetry (rules apply identically regardless of identity)
  • Abstraction (individuals are fungible under the rule)
  • Scalability (in principle, coordination cost does not grow with group size)
  • High information requirements (coherence must be actively maintained, communicated, and enforced)

Universalism is energy-expensive. It requires surplus — of trust, of institutional bandwidth, of shared language — to exist at all. Historically it appears at the founding moments of the Axial-age religious-ethical traditions, the Enlightenment, and 20th-century international institutions. In each case, the founding articulation is far more symmetric and abstract than anything the surrounding society can sustain.

We model Universalism as a field variable u(t) ∈ [0, 1], representing the fraction of total political "charge" currently organized around symmetric, rule-based coordination.

2.2 Tribalism (T): The Background Gravitational Field

Tribalism is not a pathology; it is the ground state of political cognition. Its defining properties:

  • Identity-based coordination (in-group / out-group)
  • Narrative rather than propositional processing (myth, grievance, destiny)
  • Low information requirements (identity is cheap to compute; abstraction is not)
  • Evolutionary priority (kin/coalition detection predates language)

Critically, Tribalism does not need to be produced. It is always present, at some nonzero background level, in every human system, the way mass is always present in a gravitational field. It cannot be argued away, legislated away, or educated away — it can only be temporarily outweighed by a sufficiently energetic universalist structure, and it reasserts itself the instant that structure's energy supply runs low. This is consistent with, though independently arrived at from, Henri Tajfel's social identity theory and Tooby & Cosmides' account of coalitional psychology as an ancient, fast, low-cost cognitive default rather than a learned pathology — and it matches the empirical record of prejudice-reduction interventions, which reliably suppress the expression of tribal cognition without eliminating the underlying disposition.

We model Tribalism as a field variable t(t) ∈ [0, 1], and treat it as possessing a constant, low-magnitude restoring force — a gravitational term that is always pulling the system's trajectory back toward identity-based coordination regardless of the system's current state.

2.3 Parasitism (P): The Extraction Field

Parasitism is qualitatively different from both of the above. It is not a moral stance, an identity, or a coordination strategy. It is a strategy for extracting value from the coordination costs paid by U and T. Its defining properties:

  • No intrinsic ideology (it adopts whatever vocabulary is locally dominant)
  • Growth proportional to the surface area of open systems (universalist systems, being open and trust-based, are especially exposed)
  • Narrative capture rather than narrative production (it does not create myths or moral codes; it hijacks existing ones)
  • Institutional persistence (it outlives the ideological structure that hosted it)

Unlike Tribalism, Parasitism does not correspond to a single evolved cognitive module, and it should not be modeled as though it does. It is better understood as an emergent strategy assembled opportunistically from several distinct, individually well-studied adaptations — status-seeking and dominance-hierarchy navigation, kin-directed nepotism, and cheater strategies that exploit gaps in cheater-detection (Tooby & Cosmides, 1992) — deployed wherever the coordination costs of U or the boundary-maintenance costs of T create exploitable surplus. It has no unified psychology because it does not need one: it is a niche, not an organ.

This matters for the model's integrity, because it means P must never be allowed to become a residual bin for "political behavior the observer dislikes." Section 9.1 supplies an explicit, symmetric operational test for the category; until that test is applied, any claim that a given actor or faction is "exhibiting P" should be read as a hypothesis, not a diagnosis.

It is also useful to disaggregate P institutionally rather than treat it as monolithic. Borrowing directly from the governance-corruption literature, we distinguish at least four modes: grand capture (control of the highest levers of resource allocation), petty extraction (diffuse, low-level rent-seeking), clientelist capture (exchange of loyalty for selective benefit, which blurs into T at its edges), and narrative capture (control of the interpretive and informational layer itself — the mode most relevant to the moral-bandwidth collapse discussed in Section 6). Klitgaard's (1988) formula, corruption = monopoly + discretion − accountability, is a direct institutional-scale antecedent of the η/θ ratio introduced below, and any future calibration of this model should attempt to map Klitgaard's three terms onto η and θ directly rather than treat them as independently free parameters.

Parasitism is modeled as a field variable p(t) ∈ [0, 1], whose growth rate depends not on its own ideological content (it has none) but on the product of exposure it can extract from the other two fields.

3. Formal State Space

Let the instantaneous state of a political system be the point

x(t) = (u(t), t(t), p(t)) ∈ Δ2

on the 2-simplex, where u + t + p = 1 and all coordinates are non-negative. This constraint reflects a finite pool of political "attention" or "coordination capacity" at any given time — a society can allocate its coordination energy toward abstraction, identity, or extraction, but not an unbounded amount of all three simultaneously.

We additionally define a scalar systemic energy term E(t) ≥ 0, representing the surplus resources (economic, informational, institutional) available to the system at time t. E is not part of the simplex — it is an external driving variable, analogous to temperature in a thermodynamic system, and it modulates the carrying capacity of u.

A methodological caveat is owed at the outset. The equations that follow should be read as a proposed common notation for expressing the already-documented qualitative relationships surveyed in Section 1.1, not as a calibrated empirical model. None of the eleven Greek-letter coefficients introduced below have yet been estimated against real-world proxies — governance indices, corpus-based sentiment measures, institutional-trust surveys — and the state variables themselves (u, t, p, E) are theoretical constructs, not directly measured quantities. The formalism's value at this stage lies in making the qualitative claims precise enough to be falsified once such proxies are chosen, not in the numerical content of the equations themselves. We flag this explicitly to avoid the false precision that mathematical notation can lend to what remains, for now, a structured and hopefully useful metaphor.

4. The Governing Equations

We propose a replicator-style dynamical system, structurally related to Lotka–Volterra competition models but with an explicit extraction term:

u̇ = u( αE − βt − γp )
ṫ = t( δ − εuE + ζp )
ṗ = p( ηut − θ )

where all Greek-letter coefficients are positive constants. Read informally:

  • u̇: Universalism grows in proportion to available energy E, but is suppressed both by the ambient pull of tribalism (−βt, the gravitational drag) and by direct extraction (−γp).
  • ṫ: Tribalism has a constant background growth term δ (gravity is always "on"), is suppressed only when high-energy universalism is active (−εuE), and is amplified by parasitic activity (+ζp) — because parasites profit from stoking identity conflict once they have captured a universalist host, converting extracted trust into tribal grievance.
  • ṗ: Parasitism's growth is proportional to the product u·t — it requires both an open, trust-based structure to infiltrate (u) and an identity-based fault line to exploit (t) — and decays at constant rate θ in the absence of hosts to feed on. This product term is the mathematical signature of the observation that parasites do not generate their own ideology; they require the friction between the other two fields to metabolize.

A further technical caveat is necessary. The three equations above are written independently for expositional clarity, but as stated they do not automatically preserve the simplex constraint u+t+p=1 introduced in Section 3 — a proper closed replicator system on Δ2 requires either normalization (dividing by u̇+ṫ+ṗ) or an explicit shared depletion term subtracting the population-average growth rate from each equation. We leave this normalization implicit here rather than resolve it, consistent with the illustrative status of the formalism flagged above. A fully rigorous treatment would rewrite this system as a proper replicator equation with an explicit average-fitness subtraction term before any quantitative claim is drawn from it.

4.1 Why This Is Not a Two-Body Oscillator

If p ≡ 0, the system reduces to a damped competition between u and t modulated by E — a simple oscillator that should converge to a fixed ratio as institutions "learn." This is the naive two-pole model, and it is empirically wrong (see §1).

The u·t coupling term in ṗ is what converts this from a two-body oscillator into a three-body problem. Three-body gravitational systems are famously non-integrable — they do not settle into stable orbits except on measure-zero initial conditions. Competitive systems of this general dissipative, nonlinear type (in the spirit of May–Leonard cyclic competition or Rössler-type attractors) are similarly known to admit non-convergent, oscillatory, or chaotic trajectories rather than fixed points, for broad regions of parameter space. We invoke this literature to support the qualitative claim that non-convergence is a plausible and well-precedented outcome for a system of this structural type — not as a proof that our specific, uncalibrated coefficients produce chaos. For generic initial conditions and generic coefficients within that broad class, the (u, t, p) trajectory does not converge to a fixed point; it cycles, and the cycle's phase and amplitude are history-dependent, not law-like. This matches the observed record far better than any damped-pendulum model: political history does not appear to be converging on a stable regime; it appears to be touring a manifold of temporary states.

4.2 Fixed Points and Their Instability

Setting u̇ = ṫ = ṗ = 0 yields, among trivial solutions, one interior fixed point:

u* = θ / (ηt*), t* = (αE − γp*) / β, p* = (δ − εu*E) / (−ζ)

Note that these are only implicit conditions, not a closed-form solution: u*, t*, and p* each appear on the right-hand side of another's expression, so what is presented is a fixed-point condition rather than a derived value. Solving it explicitly requires substitution we do not carry out here.

A linear stability analysis (Jacobian eigenvalues at x*) suggests this interior fixed point is generically a saddle or unstable spiral for parameter regions in which parasite growth (η) is not swamped by parasite decay (θ) — i.e., the system has no stable resting state in which universalism, tribalism, and parasitism coexist in permanent balance, at least within that region. We have not carried out a full stability classification across the entire eleven-dimensional coefficient space, so this should be read as a structural possibility the model makes available rather than a theorem established for all parameter values; outside the region described, the system may instead approach one of the boundary equilibria discussed in Section 8.1. Any historical era that looks like a stable equilibrium is, in this model, at best a slow transit near a saddle point — which is one candidate explanation for why such eras (the "end of history" moments) always eventually end.

4.3 The Guardian Problem: Recursive Capture

The base model above treats θ, the parasite decay rate, as a fixed constant representing institutional resilience — courts, free presses, watchdogs, term limits, audits. This implicitly assumes anti-corruption machinery is permanently immune to the very dynamic it exists to suppress, which is not a safe assumption: oversight bodies, from the Praetorian Guard to modern regulatory agencies, are themselves prime targets for parasitic capture. We therefore extend the model with a fourth equation governing θ itself:

θ̇ = −κpθ + μ(θ0 − θ)

Here sustained parasitic pressure corrodes enforcement capacity at rate κ (the guardians are captured too), while a repair process — institutional reform, elite turnover, generational renewal, constitutional renewal — restores θ toward a baseline θ₀ at rate μ. This closes a gap the base model leaves open: without it, the system has no way to represent the "who guards the guardians" failure mode, in which an apparently low-p, high-θ regime is not stable at all but is instead in a slow, silent decline toward a second, faster collapse once θ itself crosses zero. This second route to collapse is distinct from — and can occur independently of — the founding-moment vulnerability discussed in Section 7, and any institution designed using this model's recommendations (Section 9) must be checked against it explicitly, not merely against the base three-variable system.

6. Moral Bandwidth and the Scale-Collapse Function

A second-order phenomenon requires its own formalism: the observation that moral vocabulary — the capacity of a system to name an act as atrocity — degrades non-linearly with the scale of the act, and that this degradation is mediated by the same (u, t, p) state. This formalizes, and gives dynamical structure to, a pattern already documented under other names: Paul Slovic's research on "psychic numbing" and the collapse of compassion at scale, and the well-replicated identifiable-victim effect in behavioral economics, both describe the same empirical phenomenon the function below is built to capture. What this model proposes to add to that literature is not the underlying finding — which is independently well-established — but the claim that the rate of collapse is itself state-dependent, modulated by the same (u,t,p) coordinates governing the rest of the system, rather than a fixed cognitive constant.

Define moral bandwidth M as the system's capacity to apply symmetric, universalist moral categories to an event of magnitude s (e.g., s = log(deaths)):

M(s; u, t, p) = u·exp(−s/sc) − λt·t·s − λp·p

where sc is a scale-collapse constant representing the largest event magnitude that unaided universalist cognition can process symmetrically (individual crimes, discrete tragedies), λt captures the rate at which tribal narrative absorption converts moral magnitude into identity content (myth, grievance, sacrifice) rather than shared culpability, and λp is a flat suppression term representing direct institutional/narrative suppression by parasitic capture.

6.1 The Moral Event Horizon

Define the moral event horizon sh as the value of s at which M(s) → 0: the scale beyond which the system can no longer produce symmetric moral vocabulary for the event at all. Solving M(sh) = 0:

sh ≈ sc·ln( u / (λt·t·sh + λp·p) )

This is a transcendental equation (self-referential in sh), which is itself structurally meaningful: the event horizon is not fixed, it is state-dependent and falls as t and p rise. In a high-u, low-t, low-p regime, sh is large — the system can process even large-scale tragedy as tragedy. In a high-t or high-p regime, sh shrinks toward the scale of a single identifiable victim — beyond which the event stops being "a tragedy" and becomes, functionally, "history": absorbed into myth (t-mediated) or simply unspeakable within compromised institutions (p-mediated).

This gives a candidate mechanism behind the folk observation that "a single death is a tragedy, a million is a statistic": it is not a claim about the relative moral weight of the deaths, but a direct consequence of M(s) crossing zero as s increases in the presence of nonzero t and p. The model predicts (testably, in principle, via textual/sentiment analysis of historical archives) that events near sh should show a measurable transition from individuated moral language to either mythic/narrative language (t-dominant capture) or administrative/euphemistic language (p-dominant capture), rather than a smooth gradient.

7. Exceptional Regions: Energy Injection and Phase Transitions

Because E(t) modulates both the growth rate of u and the suppression rate of t (via the −εuE term), sudden large increases in systemic energy — industrialization, the printing press, postwar reconstruction, computing, the internet — produce exceptional regions of the phase space: transient zones where the normally-dominant saddle dynamics are overridden and u can rise rapidly.

These regions are the closest real history comes to the two-pole "Enlightenment defeats tribalism" narrative — but the model makes a specific, falsifiable prediction that the naive narrative misses: the ηut term guarantees that any exceptional region with nonzero residual t will also produce a spike in ṗ, because rising u against a nonzero t floor maximizes the product u·t that feeds parasitic growth. In other words:

Every exceptional universalist region contains, built into its own success condition, the maximum possible fuel for parasitic capture.

This is why high-energy universalist moments (revolutionary founding periods, postwar international orders, rapid technological utopian movements) are historically the most, not least, vulnerable to early and severe parasitic capture — precisely when they appear most secure. The model formalizes the intuition that naive utopias are not merely unlucky; they are structurally self-undermining unless an explicit counter-term is engineered against p (see §9).

8. Historical Trajectories Through State Space

Rather than narrate history as a sequence of eras, the model treats it as a single trajectory x(t) winding through the simplex, occasionally passing near (but never resting at) the unstable interior fixed point, and periodically entering exceptional regions driven by exogenous energy shocks E(t).

Several qualitative features of this trajectory are worth naming explicitly:

  • Non-convergence is structural, not accidental. Because the interior fixed point is unstable for realistic coefficients, no amount of institutional learning within a single (u,t,p)-parameterization converges the system. Learning can change the coefficients (better institutions can raise θ, the parasite decay rate, or lower η, the parasite growth-from-friction rate) — but cannot, by itself, produce a stable rest state without also changing the structure of the equations.
  • Tribalism's floor never reaches zero. Because ṫ contains the constant term δ, tribalism never fully vanishes even under maximal u and E. This matches the observation that no universalist movement in history has ever fully eliminated identity-based cognition — only temporarily suppressed its expression.
  • Parasitic capture is the fastest-moving variable. Because p's growth term is multiplicative in u and t rather than additive, p can spike on a much shorter timescale than either u or t can rebuild — which is consistent with the historical observation that institutional corruption/capture (Stalin-era bureaucratic distortion of the Soviet project, the swift co-optation of many revolutionary movements generally) tends to visibly outpace the "natural" timescale of either ideological consolidation or identity re-formation.

8.1 Comparative Statics: Why Not Everywhere, and Why Not Always

The trajectory framing above risks implying that every polity is condemned to tour the same U→P→T cycle at the same tempo. That is not the model's claim, and the historical record contains an important class of cases it must be able to accommodate rather than explain away: long-duration, comparatively stable mixed orders — the Nordic social democracies, Switzerland's confederal cantons, Botswana's post-independence governance record — that have sustained high u and moderate t for many decades without the catastrophic p-spikes the table in Section 5 associates with founding moments elsewhere.

The model's account of these cases is not that they have somehow escaped the triad, but that they occupy a region of parameter space where θ (parasite decay, including its recursive form in Section 4.3) has been engineered durably high and η (parasite growth-from-friction) durably low — through mechanisms such as consensus-based coalition government, high press freedom, small-N accountability networks, and guardian institutions unusually resistant to the capture dynamic of Section 4.3. These cases are therefore not falsifications of the model but its most important calibration targets: any future parameterization of η and θ should be able to reproduce both the rapid Bolshevik-style p-spike and the multi-generational Nordic plateau from the same functional form, differing only in coefficient values, not in structure. A model that cannot in principle generate both outcomes would be wrong; a model that has not yet been shown to generate both, as is currently the case here, is merely incomplete. Supplying that comparative calibration is treated in Section 10 as the immediate next research task rather than a settled result.

9. Design Principles: Toward Parasite-Resistant Universalism

If the model is approximately correct, the practical implication is sharp and somewhat counter to conventional political rhetoric on all sides:

The primary design target for anyone attempting to build a durable universalist system should not be the suppression of tribalism (t), but the suppression of the parasite growth coefficient η and the elevation of the parasite decay coefficient θ — including its recursive, guardian-capture form (§4.3).

Concretely, this reframes several perennial debates as engineering problems rather than moral ones:

  • Institutional transparency functions as a direct reduction of η: it reduces the "friction surface" available for extraction between open (u) and identity-based (t) subsystems.
  • Redundant, decentralized enforcement raises θ and, importantly, lowers κ in Section 4.3's guardian equation: a parasite that captures one node does not automatically capture the whole narrative/resource-allocation apparatus, and no single guardian institution can be captured to bring down the entire enforcement layer.
  • Tolerance for tribal expression within bounded domains — rather than attempts at total suppression — may counter-intuitively lower long-run p, because attempts to drive t → 0 increase δ's effective pressure (a compressed spring), which historically manifests as violent correction (the WWI pattern: naive universalist suppression of national identity followed by explosive tribal reassertion) and also creates larger u·t friction surfaces during the suppression phase itself.
  • Treating tribalism as background gravity rather than as an enemy to defeat reallocates political energy away from an unwinnable fight (against δ, a constant) and toward the winnable fight (against η and θ, both engineerable constants).

This is the mathematical restatement of the essay's central discovery: naive utopian projects fail not because tribalism is too strong, but because they mistake tribalism for the threat, leaving the parasite term completely undefended — and an undefended multiplicative growth term against a constant background field will always eventually dominate.

9.1 An Operational Test for P, and Why It Must Be Symmetric

Because "parasite" is among the most rhetorically loaded words available in political discourse, this model is only responsible if it supplies a test for the category that cannot simply collapse into "whichever faction I oppose." We propose the following conditions, all of which must hold before an actor or institution is classified as exhibiting high p rather than high t or low u:

  1. Ideological content-independence. The actor's vocabulary and stated values could be swapped for those of a rival faction with no material change in its extraction behavior. A structure that behaves identically whether it is nominally communist, nationalist, religious, or liberal is exhibiting p; a structure whose behavior tracks its stated ideology, even when that ideology is one the observer dislikes, is exhibiting u or t, not p.
  2. Institutional outlasting. The actor or apparatus persists across changes of ideological leadership, regime, or founding cohort, continuing to extract value under new banners.
  3. Directional asymmetry of information flow. The actor systematically increases its own informational or resource position while decreasing the transparency or accountability available to those it extracts from.
  4. Falsifiable counter-test. The classification must in principle be revocable: if the actor submits to external audit, term limits, or redistributive constraint and the extraction behavior stops, it was accountable governance, not parasitism, and the classification must be withdrawn.

Any application of this model — including by its own authors — that labels a rival political tribe "parasitic" without checking these four conditions is not using the model; it is using the model's vocabulary to do what tribal cognition (§2.2) already does for free. This is the single most important safeguard the model requires, and the one most likely to be skipped by exactly the actors most eager to cite the model in the first place.

9.2 A Note on Normative Status: Description Is Not Destiny

A clarification is owed regarding the status of Section 2.2's claim that tribalism cannot be eliminated. That claim is empirical, not prescriptive: it is a statement about the durability of a cognitive default, not a statement about what is owed to it, and it does not license the inference that resisting tribal escalation locally is futile or not worth attempting. Persistent forces are not thereby forces one has no obligation to resist — gravity is persistent and we still build airplanes.

Nor does the model's diagnostic framing license unconstrained technocratic intervention against p in the name of "engineering." The same concentration of monitoring and enforcement capacity that raises θ is also the raw material from which the next parasitic capture is built, if that capacity is not itself bound by the accountability constraints in Section 9.1 and the guardian-capture dynamic of Section 4.3. Any institution built on this model's recommendations should therefore be designed with explicit, hard, rights-based limits — due process, sunset clauses, distributed rather than centralized audit authority — that apply even when, especially when, the institution's stated purpose is fighting p. A design that raises θ by concentrating unchecked power in a single anti-corruption body has not solved the problem this model describes; it has relocated it, and Section 4.3's κ term will eventually find it.

10. Scope, Calibration, and Epistemic Status

It is worth stating plainly, in one place, what kind of claim this document is making, since the notation used throughout risks implying a stronger claim than is warranted. The Universalist–Tribal–Parasite model is best understood as a synthetic diagnostic heuristic: a proposed common vocabulary and state space for integrating findings that already exist, in more rigorous and empirically grounded form, across political science, evolutionary anthropology, and institutional economics (§1.1). It is not, at present, a validated or falsifiable predictive theory, for three specific and correctable reasons:

  1. No coefficient has been estimated against data. The Greek-letter constants introduced in Section 4 (and Section 4.3) are currently free parameters chosen for narrative plausibility, not fit to governance indices (e.g., the World Bank's Worldwide Governance Indicators, Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index), corpus-based sentiment measures, or historical event datasets of the kind used in cliodynamics.
  2. The comparative cases have been identified but not fitted. Section 8.1 names the right class of negative cases — long-duration stable mixed orders — but does not yet demonstrate that the same functional form reproduces both those cases and the founding-moment collapses of Section 5 from a single, principled parameterization.
  3. The state variables are constructs, not measurements. u, t, and p are not currently operationalized in a way that an independent party could code from primary sources and reproduce.

None of these are objections to the model's continued development; they are the model's own research agenda, listed here explicitly so that the confidence lent by the equations in Sections 3–4 does not imply they have already been resolved. A model that specifies exactly what would need to be true to falsify it, and has not yet done the work to check, is in a meaningfully better epistemic position than one offering no such specification at all — but it is not yet in the position its equations visually suggest, and readers, especially readers inclined to invoke Section 9's design principles in real institutional debates, should treat it accordingly.

11. Conclusion: The Cycle Continues

The Universalist–Tribal–Parasite model does not predict an end to the cycle it describes — quite the opposite. Because the interior fixed point is unstable and the tribal background term never vanishes, the model predicts, structurally, that:

  1. Every high-energy exceptional region will produce a universalist resurgence,
  2. every such resurgence will generate, as an unavoidable side-effect of its own success, the maximal friction surface for parasitic capture,
  3. every episode of parasitic capture will eventually stoke a tribal correction, and
  4. every tribal correction will eventually exhaust itself and await the next energy shock — unless, per Section 4.3, its own guardians are captured first, in which case collapse can arrive by a second, faster route.

This is not pessimism; it is a specification, offered in the more modest register described in Section 10: a synthesis of existing findings into a common vocabulary, not a proven law of history. A system that cannot be permanently stabilized can still be steered — its amplitude reduced, its parasitic capture delayed and weakened, its tribal corrections made less violent — by engineering directly against the η and θ terms rather than against δ, and by binding that engineering itself to the symmetric test of Section 9.1 and the rights-based limits of Section 9.2. The lesson of the long historical current, from the Axial age through the Enlightenment, through the world wars, through the Cold War, and into the present, is not that reason must defeat tribe, or that tribe must be tolerated, but that every era which forgot to defend against the parasite — and every defense against the parasite that forgot to guard itself — lost the fight it thought it was having.
