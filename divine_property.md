# The Palace and the Library

## How a Partial Order of Claims Becomes a Hierarchy of Persons

### I. A Universe Without Provenance

In 1970 John Conway invented the Game of Life. It is a grid of cells, each either alive or dead, updated in discrete generations by a few rules that look only at each cell's eight neighbors. A live cell with too few or too many live neighbors dies. A dead cell with exactly three live neighbors comes alive. Out of those rules come structures that move, collide, and reproduce. The most famous, the glider, is a cluster of five cells that crawls diagonally across the board forever.

In 1984 Bill Gosper described Hashlife, a way to simulate the Game of Life at astonishing speed. Its central trick is *canonicalization*: every pattern is reduced to a single stored representative, so that two identical patterns are literally the same object in memory. The board is divided into a tree of square blocks, and every distinct block is stored only once. When the same arrangement of cells appears twice, whether in two corners of the board or a million generations apart, the algorithm does not compute it twice. It looks the pattern up, finds that it has already seen it, and reuses the result.

Hashlife does not ask where a pattern came from. It does not know who placed the glider, whether the glider was copied from somewhere else, or whether two gliders arose independently in distant regions that never touched. A pattern is identified by what it is. There is no inventor field, no owner field, and no license field. Provenance is not part of the physics.

Now imagine, as a thought experiment, that the universe is run by something like a quantum Hashlife and that God is watching it. From that vantage point God sees configurations and transitions: which states lead to which other states. He does not see property as a primitive object, and he does not necessarily see rights. He sees matter and information evolving according to rules that recognize only structure.

I want to be clear about what this device is and what it is not. It is not a claim about real physics. Quantum mechanics as we understand it is unitary: in principle the global state retains the trace of its history. The no-cloning theorem forbids duplicating an unknown quantum state, so a literal quantum Hashlife could not reuse patterns as freely as Gosper's classical one does. Nor is it a claim about the God of Scripture. That God is the opposite of provenance-blind. He remembers lineages, swears covenants to particular ancestors, and treats a family's ancestral land as an inheritance that cannot simply be sold off. Persons and suffering do not appear as primitive objects in Hashlife either, and nobody should conclude that they are therefore unreal.

The provenance-blind observer is a methodological subtraction. Strip away every piece of normative metadata humans attach to the world, and see what is left. The exercise cannot tell us what is valuable. It can help us tell which layers of human order the physics supplies and which layers we add ourselves.

Inside this universe a small region of configurations, the ones we call humans, starts doing something peculiar. They begin arguing about who is allowed to instantiate which patterns.

This essay is about what such an observer would actually see when he watched that argument. My claim is that he would not see what the participants believe they are doing. He would watch them fight over a *partial ordering* and arrive instead at a *power hierarchy*. By the end, the exercise will yield a distinction between two kinds of human artifact. The first is the palace, which concentrates the power to choose among futures. The second is the library, which preserves the ability to reconstruct them.

### II. The Order Spacetime Already Has

Hashlife is a toy universe. Our actual universe has a feature that matters even more for this argument.

In relativity, two events stand in one of three relations. Event *a* can causally precede event *b*, meaning a signal from *a* could reach *b*. Event *b* can precede *a*. Or neither precedes the other, because they are spacelike separated: too far apart in space for any signal to connect them in the time between them. That third case is not a gap in our knowledge. Different observers will disagree about which of the two happened "first," and physics declares no winner. There is no fact of the matter about their order.

Mathematicians call a structure like this a *partial order*. Some pairs of elements are ordered, and others are *incomparable*, written *a* ∥ *b* ("neither precedes the other"). Causal set theory, developed by Bombelli, Lee, Meyer, and Sorkin in the late 1980s, takes this seriously enough to treat causal order as the primitive structure of spacetime. On that view, geometry is something recovered from the order of events. Philosophers working on the theory have even read the ordering relation as a constraint on how the universe is permitted to grow.

Two consequences matter here. First, nature tolerates incomparability without strain. Spacelike-separated events coexist without the universe deciding which one is globally "above" the other. Second, omniscience is not the same as total ordering. An observer who sees both events does not thereby gain the right to manufacture a relation between them. Seeing everything means seeing which relations actually exist, including the ones that do not.

I am not trying to derive ethics from physics. My point is narrower: partial order is not merely a clever metaphor imported from computer science. It is already a feature of the causal architecture in which human law operates. That raises the question this essay keeps returning to. Why does nature tolerate incomparability so easily, while human institutions keep trying to eliminate it?

### III. The Ordering Humans Want

Take a simple case. Alice invents a mechanism and files a patent. Years later Bob, who has never heard of Alice, never read her patent, and shares no causal history with her work, invents the same mechanism. No information had to flow from Alice to Bob. The two devices are the same because the problem admits that solution, and both minds found it.

"The same" needs care. Alice's device and Bob's device are not microscopically identical. They differ in their atoms, their tolerances, and their scratches. They canonicalize to the same node only once we choose a level of abstraction, a projection under which

> X_A ≡ X_B

where ≡ means "equivalent with respect to the patented mechanism." Physics does not supply that projection. The law does. A patent claim does not say that two pieces of matter are identical. It classifies physically different implementations as legally equivalent for the purpose of the claim.

God supplies configurations. Humans supply equivalence classes. Then the law orders the classes.

The law says Bob may not build his device. Write the relation like this:

> A ≺ B

read as "A's claim takes priority over B's ability to instantiate this pattern."

On its own terms this is not a hierarchy of persons. It is relation-specific. Alice precedes Bob with respect to mechanism X. Bob might precede Carol with respect to song Y. Alice and Carol might be *incomparable*, with no relation between them at all. A system of such relations is properly a partial order, or more precisely a large collection of them, each indexed by a particular pattern or claim:

> { A ≺ₓ B, C ≺ᵧ A, B ∥_z C, … }

Here the subscript names the pattern at issue, and B ∥_z C says that with respect to z, neither B nor C has priority over the other.

This structure is plural. Nothing in it implies a globally supreme element. Nobody sits at the top, because there is no top. There are only local precedences, each tied to a specific thing. In this respect the ordering humans say they want resembles the causal order of spacetime: sparse, local, and full of incomparable pairs.

If human law were only this, it would be a strange but defensible project: an attempt to describe which claims should precede which other claims, pattern by pattern, as a matter of fairness or incentive.

The difficulty is locating where the ordering comes from. The universe may well contain causal traces showing that Alice built her device before Bob built his. A perfect observer could read

> A constructed X before B

directly off the transition graph. What he would not find anywhere in the physics is the next line:

> ∴ A ≺ₓ B

The "therefore" is human law. Even perfect provenance does not generate a right. The ordering has to be maintained by some other dynamical system running on top of the physics.

### IV. Why Orders Need Enforcers

That other system is the apparatus we know well: courts, contracts, registries, money, police, institutional legitimacy. These exist because a claimed ordering that nobody can enforce has no effect on what happens. Bob builds his device, and the universe doesn't object.

So the desired ordering of claims requires a mechanism to enforce claims. The mechanism is built from humans and their artifacts, and humans do not control it equally. Unequal control of a mechanism that can constrain other people's actions is what we call power. The progression runs:

> desired order of claims → mechanism for enforcing claims → unequal control of the mechanism → power hierarchy

Each step makes sense locally. Disagreements about the ordering are inevitable. If Bob disputes Alice's priority, someone has to decide, so we need an arbiter. Disagreements about the arbiter are also inevitable, so we need a higher-order mechanism: appeals, legislatures, constitutions. Each layer resolves disputes about the layer below by being able to make its decisions physically consequential.

Somewhere along this climb the operative relation changes character. It stops being "A's claim precedes B's claim with respect to X." It becomes something like:

> A ▹ B

meaning that A has enough institutional capacity to narrow the set of state transitions available to B.

These relations behave differently, and the difference may be the thesis of this whole essay: **power propagates where claims do not.**

A claim is indexed. A ≺ₓ B says nothing at all about A and B with respect to Y. Enforcement resources are not indexed in that way. Money earned through one privilege hires lawyers for another dispute. Political access acquired in one domain affects outcomes in a second. Institutional prestige travels, and so do connections. Influence over the arbiter in patent disputes is useful in tax disputes, zoning disputes, and criminal disputes.

Suppose that making each local relation effective requires drawing on some reusable pool of resources:

> P = { money, courts, lawyers, officials, prestige, coercion }

Then the enforcement layer acts as a *coupling mechanism* between partial orders that ought to be independent. Whoever accumulates P can push many orderings in the same direction at once. The sparse relations begin to correlate, and the correlated relations begin to approximate a single ranking of persons:

> rank(A) > rank(B)

The problem is not merely that enforcement requires power. It is that enforcement power is less domain-local than the claims it enforces. Public-choice economists describe one face of this as regulatory capture. Biblical writers, as we will see, described it as the corruption of the gate.

The patent turns out to be almost a decoy. It says, "Here is the ordering." The institution behind it says, "Here is who gets to make that ordering physically consequential." What actually governs outcomes is the second statement.

The humans believed they were deciding which claims come before which claims. What they actually built was a machine that decides which humans can constrain which other humans.

### V. The Executable Constitution

The observer outside the automaton would not need to read the jurisprudence to learn the real law. He could simply watch the transition graph and ask one empirical question:

> Which configurations of humans reliably cause which other configurations of humans to become impossible?

The answer to that question is the *executable constitution*. It may resemble the written one closely or hardly at all. Where they differ, the executable one decides what happens.

This is a theological version of an insight the legal realists reached by other means. "The prophecies of what the courts will do in fact, and nothing more pretentious," wrote Oliver Wendell Holmes in 1897, "are what I mean by the law." A generation later Robert Hale showed that markets which call themselves voluntary rest on legally distributed powers of coercion that are rarely named as such. A network engineer would recognize the same gap as the difference between the intended routing policy and what the telemetry shows packets actually doing. On every account the operative law is not exhausted by formal propositions. It consists of what courts, officials, police, corporations, and citizens actually do. The statute describes one graph, and behavior executes another. The gap between them is not a rounding error. It is where power lives.

This external view leaves something out, and H. L. A. Hart named it: the *internal point of view*. Most people obey most laws most of the time without anyone threatening them, because they regard the rules as genuinely binding. A purely behaviorist map of who can stop whom misses that. But the omission sharpens the model; it does not refute it. Internalized obligation is itself part of the executable constitution. Belief is machinery. The control plane works as well as it does because millions of human nodes run the rules voluntarily, and that is exactly why capturing the source of those beliefs is so valuable.

This yields an inversion. Human law presents itself as a hierarchy of *abstractions*: constitution above statute, statute above regulation, regulation above contract. To an observer outside the automaton, it would show up as a hierarchy of *causal power*. The abstractions are real in the sense that people act on them, but their ordering is only as real as the machinery that implements it.

### VI. Control-Plane Packets

Look again at what the patent is, physically. It is ink on paper, or bits in a registry. Bob never encountered it. It made no causal contribution to his invention. If we write T_B for Bob's act of building his device and P for the patent document, there is no sense in which P contains T_B or was required to produce it.

Even so:

> P → institution → ¬T_B

where ¬ means "not": the transition T_B no longer occurs. A small symbolic configuration, routed through a social mechanism, removes a physical possibility from someone who never interacted with it.

The patent is not unusual in this respect.

- A deed does not contain a house.
- A dollar does not contain bread.
- A patent does not contain an invention.
- A statute does not contain a prison.

Each of these is a tiny informational object that participates in an enormous causal structure. In networking terms they are *control-plane packets*. They carry no payload of their own, but they reconfigure how the payload moves. Perhaps the most consequential human invention was not any particular machine. It was the discovery that sufficiently coordinated symbolic information can change other humans' physically available futures. Legal scholars such as James Boyle and Lawrence Lessig have long argued that intellectual property and software code act as architectures of control. The control-plane framing pushes their point down to the level of state transitions.

These packets are not all the same kind of thing, though, and the differences matter. A deed governs *rivalrous* matter. Two families cannot farm the same field in the same season, and two people cannot sleep in the same bed on the same night without some arrangement between them. Exclusivity over land is partly forced by physics, and the deed is a coordination device for a scarcity that exists whether or not anyone writes it down. A patent governs a *non-rivalrous* pattern. Bob's device takes nothing from Alice's. Both can run at once, and the world simply contains more instances of a good idea. Hashlife canonicalization applies to patterns. It does not apply to plots of land. The control-plane critique therefore bites hardest where the payload is non-rivalrous, because there the packet creates a scarcity the physics never imposed.

Once you see law this way, the important question about any legal system becomes: who controls the control plane, and what keeps that control from being converted into everything else?

The biblical writers spent a lot of time on exactly that question.

### VII. Impartiality as a Refusal to Cache Rank

The Bible says nothing about patents in any modern sense, and reading it as if it did would be anachronistic. The structural problem, however, appears throughout: humans try to build a just ordering of claims, and the machinery required to enforce it collapses into a hierarchy of persons.

Start with impartiality. Leviticus 19:15 is direct: "You shall do no injustice in court. You shall not be partial to the poor or defer to the great, but in righteousness shall you judge your neighbor." Deuteronomy 10:17 describes God as one "who is not partial and takes no bribe." The New Testament repeats the principle in Acts 10:34 and Romans 2:11: God shows no partiality.

In the vocabulary of this essay, divine judgment is intensely relation-specific. It has the form

> judge(A, B, X)

where the result depends on A, B, and the specific matter X in dispute. What it refuses to be is

> rank(A) > rank(B), therefore A wins.

The second calculation is cheaper. It doesn't require examining X. A judge who already knows who matters can skip the evidence. Humans therefore have a strong incentive to *cache* rank: to compute social standing once and reuse it across every dispute rather than evaluating each relation on its merits.

That caching may serve as a working definition of aristocracy. The commandment against partiality is a commandment not to cache. Evaluate the relation each time, and don't let the answer to "who is greater?" substitute for the answer to "who is right about this?"

The objection here is serious. Institutional economists from Ronald Coase to Oliver Williamson, and political realists going back to Hobbes, would point out that caching is what makes a society computationally tractable. A stable deed spares every buyer from re-litigating the history of a field. A medical license spares every patient from personally examining a surgeon. *Stare decisis* spares every court from re-deriving the law of contract from first principles. Without cheap, reusable judgments, transaction costs would swamp the division of labor.

All of that is true, and it does not touch the commandment, because those are caches of a different kind. Precedent is a cache keyed on X: it stores how disputes of this type should be resolved. A deed is a cache keyed on a particular relation: it stores who holds this field. A credential stores a finding about competence in one domain. What the commandment prohibits is a cache keyed on A: a stored judgment about a *person* that gets reused across every relation that person enters. Stare decisis is what judge(A, B, X) looks like when it is done efficiently. Aristocracy is what happens when the cache key slips from X to A.

The ban on partiality toward the poor matters as much as the ban on deference to the great. The commandment is not a redistribution rule in disguise. It is a constraint on the *form* of judgment. Rank in either direction is prohibited as an input.

### VIII. Asking for a King

The transition from ordering function to power hierarchy is dramatized almost exactly in 1 Samuel 8.

Israel asks Samuel for a king "to judge us like all the nations." Their request is not obviously sinister. They want coordination, adjudication, and defense, which are the reasonable functions of a central authority. Samuel's answer is notable because it isn't primarily a moral condemnation of monarchy. It is an operational forecast. He lists what the king will do: take sons for his chariots, daughters for his kitchens, the best fields and vineyards for his servants, a tenth of the grain and flocks. "And you shall be his slaves."

In systems language, Israel asked for a centralized conflict-resolution service, and Samuel warned them that they were instantiating an actor that would acquire privileged write access to everyone else's state.

The point is that the problem doesn't start with the relation King > Subject. It starts with functions: coordinate, adjudicate, defend. Implementing those functions requires capabilities, such as soldiers, revenue, and administrators. Capabilities accumulate around the node that implements them, and accumulated capability is power. The abstract ordering problem produces a concrete power topology.

The memory of this stays in the text. In 1 Samuel 12 the people, terrified by thunder in the wheat harvest, confess that "we have added to all our sins this evil, to ask for ourselves a king." They asked for an ordering function and accidentally created root.

Honesty requires adding that this is not the Bible's only voice on kings, and an argument that cited only the anti-monarchical passages would be curating its evidence. The same canon contains the covenant with David in 2 Samuel 7, which promises his house a throne established forever. It also establishes a hereditary priesthood descended from Aaron, which looks a great deal like cached rank. The scriptures accept the palace as well as warn against it.

The most revealing text, though, is the one that tries to do both. Deuteronomy 17 anticipates that Israel will want a king and permits one, under conditions. He must not multiply horses, wives, or silver and gold. He must write out his own copy of the law and read it all his days, "that his heart may not be lifted up above his brothers." That is a domain firewall written into a constitution: the king's authority in his own sphere must not turn into general superiority over his brothers. The canon's position is not "no palace." It is a palace bound by a text the palace did not write and is required to read.

### IX. Babel and the Scaling of Agency

Babel, in Genesis 11, is usually summarized as a story about pride. Structurally it is about something more specific.

The text describes a humanity with one language and the same words, settling in one place, adopting a common project, building monumental infrastructure, and explicitly seeking a durable collective identity: "let us make a name for ourselves, lest we be dispersed." It is an extraordinary account of coordination capacity.

Coordination does not itself require domination. It does make domination *possible*, because it allows agency to be aggregated. There is a second progression alongside the first:

> shared representation → coordination → concentrated capability

Under the Hashlife reading, the observer is not alarmed by tall masonry. He sees something more interesting forming in the causal graph: previously semi-independent agents fusing into a single higher-order actor. The divine assessment in Genesis 11:6, "nothing that they propose to do will now be impossible for them," reads less like architectural criticism and more like an observation about scaling collective agency. The Babel narrative interrupts that aggregation by scattering the language, which means breaking the shared representation that made it possible.

Whatever one makes of that response, the structural contrast is clear. A single integrated actor with unbounded reach is a different kind of thing from many actors who must negotiate with each other. I should be careful with the mathematics here. Strictly speaking, a total order, in which every pair is ranked, is also a partial order. The point is that a *heterarchical* order preserves incomparability: precedence in one relation need not settle another. Babel represents the opposite tendency. Previously distinct agents are absorbed into a common project with steadily more general collective capacity, until everyone is comparable because everyone has a place in the same tower.

### X. The Corruption of the Control Plane

The biblical correspondence is strongest in the prophets. Their complaint is not mainly that individuals behave badly. It is that the *mechanisms of judgment* have been captured.

Amos condemns those "who afflict the righteous, who take a bribe, and turn aside the needy in the gate," the gate being where cases were heard. Isaiah 10:1–2 pronounces woe on "those who decree iniquitous decrees, and the writers who keep writing oppression, to turn aside the needy from justice." Proverbs 11:1 calls a false balance an abomination. Over and over, the target is the instrument of adjudication and measurement: the court, the scribe, the scale.

That is the failure mode traced above, stated in ancient terms. A formal relation between claims becomes the ability to determine the relation. That becomes the ability to influence whoever determines the relation. And that becomes power. Once this happens, the nominal ordering and the executable ordering diverge. The statute says one graph exists while the court executes another, and the prophets are the people who keep pointing out the difference. In the language of Section V, they are the telemetry.

The pairing in Deuteronomy 10:17, "not partial *and* takes no bribe," is precise. Partiality is the internal corruption of judgment, and bribery is the external channel through which power purchases it. Together they cover both ways the control plane fails.

### XI. Service and Rank Propagation

The New Testament inversions of first and last, servant and master, are often treated as moral encouragement. They are better read as a structural attack on *rank propagation*.

In Mark 10:42–45, Jesus observes that the rulers of the Gentiles "lord it over" their subjects and their great ones "exercise authority over them," and then says: "it shall not be so among you. But whoever would be great among you must be your servant." Authority in this account does not license domination. It obligates service in the specific domain where the authority applies.

James 2:1–4 makes the mechanism concrete. A man with gold rings and fine clothing enters the assembly and is offered a good seat, while a poor man is told to stand or sit on the floor. James's verdict: "have you not then made distinctions among yourselves and become judges with evil thoughts?" The rich man's advantage in one domain, wealth, has been allowed to determine his standing in an unrelated domain, the assembly. James calls this wrongful *judgment*, which shows he understands it as a corruption of evaluation and not merely bad manners.

Both passages target the same transition:

> local advantage → global rank

This is precisely how a partial order turns into a near-total social hierarchy. Authority in one relation must not recursively become superiority in every relation. Michael Walzer's *Spheres of Justice* gave the modern philosophical name to this wrong. Walzer called it *dominance*: the condition in which a good that properly belongs to one sphere, such as money, office, or birth, converts into command over goods in every other sphere. His alternative, "complex equality," allows inequality within each sphere while forbidding conversion between spheres. James's assembly is a first-century case study in exactly that conversion. Read structurally, the servant ethic is a firewall against rank leaking across domains.

### XII. The Node That Cannot Be Built

The biblical writers are careful to separate two claims:

1. God possesses supreme power.
2. Therefore God's judgment is whatever supreme power happens to prefer.

They affirm the first and emphatically reject the second. God is repeatedly described as impartial and unbribable. That is a strange thing to insist on about an omnipotent being, since nothing could compel such a being to be fair. The insistence makes sense only as a deliberate statement of an ideal.

A human ruler is dangerous because enforcement power leaks into judgment:

> power → privilege

The theological ideal reverses this:

> unlimited power + zero partiality

Biblical divine authority is not supposed to mean that the biggest ape wins. It describes a hypothetical adjudicator whose capacity to enforce a relation *cannot* corrupt its evaluation of that relation. It also connects back to the observer of Section II. Perfect judgment does not require ranking everyone. It may require perfect respect for which relations actually exist and which do not.

Humans cannot build that node. Every human implementation of judgment has interests, dependencies, and a position in the very graph it is meant to adjudicate. This is what public-choice theorists mean when they say institutions are staffed by agents with incentives of their own, so that implementation cannot be assumed to follow specification. It is what anarchist theorists mean when they ask how task-specific authority hardens into durable domination. And it is what distributed-systems engineers mean when they say that any privileged node is a single point of failure.

The engineering version of the story is the cleanest:

> heterarchy → coordination layer → privileged nodes → power hierarchy

The desired object is a distributed constraint system. Resolving disagreements within it requires authority. Authority creates privileged nodes. Privileged nodes accumulate dependencies, and those dependencies make the nodes expensive to remove. Somewhere along the way everyone forgets that the hierarchy was implementation machinery for the relations and begins treating it as the thing the relations exist to justify.

Hobbes would insist on the other half of the story, and he would be right. The alternative to an imperfect palace is not a serene library. It is the condition in which life is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short," and in that condition libraries burn first. Peace, security, and a working rule of law are the material life-support that scholarship, copying, and teaching depend on. A palace that judges impartially is itself one of the rarest patterns humanity has ever discovered. It is hard to reconstruct, easy to lose, and worth preserving in the library alongside everything else. It is an *enabling constraint*, a restriction that makes larger freedoms possible.

So the practical question is not whether to have palaces. It is how to build them so that they do not propagate rank.

### XIII. Building Around the Missing Node

If the impartial node cannot be built, it has to be approximated by design. Moral exhortation is not enough, because the pressure to cache rank never relaxes. Every tired judge, overloaded clerk, and harried official is tempted by the cheaper computation. A system that relies on willpower to resist that temptation will lose. The protocol itself must starve the arbiter of the inputs needed to compute rank(A) > rank(B).

Some of this machinery already exists, in fragments. Blind review, anonymized grading, and the image of Justice wearing a blindfold are all attempts to turn judge(A, B, X) into

> judge(anon₁, anon₂, X)

Party-blinded dockets could extend the principle wherever a dispute does not genuinely turn on who the parties are. Rotating arbiters, or selecting them by lot as Athens did, keeps any single node from accumulating the dependencies that make it worth capturing. Domain firewalls address coupling directly. Public financing of judicial elections cuts the channel by which money in one sphere buys influence over judgment in another. So does refusing to suspend driver's licenses or voting rights over unrelated civil debts. Each of these is Deuteronomy 17 in procedural form.

The executable constitution can also be observed and audited. Laws can be evaluated by measuring what they actually do to the transition graph: who ends up constrained, who is exempted in practice, and whose cases never reach the gate at all. The intentions written in the statute are not the measure. Paired with sunset clauses, such audits force a periodic choice. Either a constraint still earns its keep, or it expires.

The biblical tradition anticipated the deepest version of this. Deuteronomy 15 commands a release of debts every seventh year. Leviticus 25 describes a Jubilee every fiftieth year, when ancestral land returns to the families who held it, on the stated ground that "the land shall not be sold in perpetuity, for the land is mine." Historians debate how often the Jubilee was ever actually observed. As design, though, it is remarkable. It is scheduled garbage collection for accumulated privilege: a periodic flush of the caches through which temporary advantage hardens into permanent rank. It flushes them without burning down the order that the caches serve.

None of these mechanisms escapes the problem it addresses. Every auditor, lottery, and Jubilee administrator is another node that can be captured. The aim is not to build the impossible node. It is to build many modest ones whose failures are visible, bounded, and correctable.

### XIV. The Circle and the Idol

The forgetting described in Section XII has a recognizable shape: a circle.

The king demonstrates the legitimacy of kingship by being king. The title establishes ownership because the institution recognizes the title. The institution's authority comes from the legal order that the institution itself puts into operation. Inside the system each step seems grounded in the one before it. From outside the automaton the loop is obvious. Hart's internal point of view is what keeps the loop stable. Once enough nodes have internalized the rules, the circle no longer needs to be defended. It simply runs.

Humans generate representations describing desired restrictions on state transitions. They build machinery that can enforce those restrictions. Then they point to the machinery and say: *the machinery proves the ordering is legitimate.*

That is the recursive mistake, and it brings the biblical prohibition on idolatry unexpectedly close. The prophetic satire of idols, the craftsman who cuts a tree, burns half of it to bake bread, and bows down to the other half saying "Deliver me, for you are my god" (Isaiah 44), is a satire of forgetting authorship. The deepest recurring human error may not be worshipping statues as such. It may be building a representation, giving it causal power, and then forgetting that we built it.

Laws, titles, and patents are not idols in themselves. They are tools, and often good ones. They become idolatrous at the moment their power is taken as evidence of their legitimacy, when the fact that the control plane *can* enforce an ordering is taken to settle whether it *should*.

### XV. Burning the Palace, Burning the Library

All of this sharpens a distinction that history tends to blur: the difference between burning the palace and burning the library.

From inside history the two acts can look alike. Both attack institutions. Both destroy culturally important objects. Both get called revolutionary or barbaric depending on who tells the story. Under the model developed here, they point in nearly opposite directions.

The palace says: *these people occupy a privileged position in the transition graph.* Burning it attacks an instance of the power hierarchy.

The library says: *these patterns allow future people to reconstruct states that need not pass through us.* Burning it destroys the information from which alternative orderings might be rebuilt.

The asymmetry between them is severe, but it has to be stated precisely. A palace is mostly instantiated state. The library preserves generative structure. Instantiated state is replaceable when the information that generated it survives. You can build another palace, appoint another king, and reconstitute the bureaucracy, because the recipe for doing so survives in people's heads, habits, and documents. Generative information is different. Once it is lost, the states it could have produced may become permanently unreachable, because nobody still holds the recipe for getting back there.

The distinction is not between stone and paper. A broken artifact, a unique astronomical measurement, or the last specimen of a species may embody information that exists nowhere else. Destroying it is library-burning, whatever material it is made of. What matters is whether the generating information survives somewhere else.

This also resolves an apparent contradiction in the argument. Bob reinvented Alice's mechanism without ever seeing it. If knowledge is that convergent, how can the library be fragile? The answer is that knowledge comes in two kinds. Some of it is *convergent*: it is forced by the structure of a problem, and anyone who works on the problem long enough will find it. Newton and Leibniz both found the calculus. Alice and Bob both found the mechanism. Burn every copy, and the problem itself will eventually teach it again. Other knowledge is *path-dependent*: the grammar of a dying language, the poems of Sappho, the record of what a particular people did and suffered, the observations made on a night that will never recur. The problem does not regenerate these, because there is no problem, only history. The lost plays of Aeschylus are not waiting to be rediscovered by someone clever enough. Library-burning does its permanent damage in the path-dependent part of the collection.

So:

> destroying power ≠ destroying possibility

and, more strongly:

> destroying path-dependent knowledge destroys branches of the future

This reverses an ordinary intuition about value. We treat the palace as enormously valuable because someone owns it and it cannot be duplicated, while we treat copied knowledge as cheap because nobody can meaningfully hold a copy exclusively. From a computational perspective the valuation often runs the other way. The palace is largely cache, and the library is largely generative structure.

There is a further asymmetry in what each institution does with alternatives. A library can hold monstrous ideas, obsolete ideas, and mutually contradictory ideas without resolving among them. That is its structural virtue: *a library preserves incomparability.* It can hold A ∥ B and hand both to five hundred years of strangers. A palace cannot do that. A palace has to act, and acting means choosing, so sooner or later it produces A ▹ B. The palace concentrates the ability to select among futures. The library distributes the ability to reconstruct them.

### XVI. Jehoiakim's Knife

The Bible contains its own library-burning, and it is instructive. In Jeremiah 36, the prophet dictates his oracles to the scribe Baruch, who writes them on a scroll. The scroll is read to King Jehoiakim as he sits by a brazier in his winter house. As each few columns are read, the king cuts them off with a scribe's knife and throws them into the fire, until the whole scroll is gone.

This is not an attack on a palace. The king *is* the palace. It is the palace attacking the library, the power hierarchy trying to delete a pattern that describes a future in which the hierarchy is judged.

The ending of the story is the important part. Jeremiah takes another scroll, Baruch writes again everything that was on the first, "and many similar words were added to them" (Jeremiah 36:32). The burning fails because the pattern was reconstructible. The scroll was a copy, and the generative source, the prophet and the scribe, survived. In Hashlife terms the king destroyed one instance of a node whose canonical form still existed elsewhere, and the regenerated version was larger.

A caution belongs here. In its own terms, Jeremiah 36 is not a parable about the free circulation of information. The scroll is an oracle of divine judgment, rewritten at divine command, and its message is that Babylon is coming. To read it as an Enlightenment fable about open access would be to replace the text's theology with mine. My claim is narrower and structural. The king tried to delete a pattern, and he failed because its generative source survived his knife.

The story also shows that idolatry and book-burning are nearly inverse pathologies. The idolater mistakes the instantiated representation for the thing that gives it significance. The book-burner understands, horrifyingly correctly, that a representation is not merely an object. Destroying the informational structure is a way of attacking what future minds will be able to reconstruct. The first error overvalues the artifact. The second values it accurately and sets out to destroy it.

That points to a sober conclusion. The power of library-burning is proportional to the scarcity of copies and the fragility of the minds that could regenerate them. Tyranny that understands this does not stop at burning books. It goes after scribes, schools, languages, and the habits of mind that let people reconstruct what was lost.

So the two acts express two different ambitions:

- **Burning the palace:** "You shall no longer rule us."
- **Burning the library:** "Those who come after us shall not even be allowed to know this alternative."

The first attacks authority over the present. The second claims authority over the reachable future.

This gives a deeper conception of tyranny. Tyranny is not only occupying the top of a hierarchy. It is pruning the tree of possibilities so that alternatives to the hierarchy become steadily harder to reconstruct. A tyrant who rules the present can be overthrown. A tyrant who has edited the future may never be recognized as a tyrant, because nobody downstream retains the patterns needed to imagine anything else.

### XVII. The Patent, Revisited

This brings the argument back to the patent, which now looks more complicated than it did at the start.

Patent law presents itself as an instrument for building the library. In exchange for disclosure, so that the pattern is written down and eventually made public, the inventor receives a temporary exclusive right. The intended effect is to add to the stock of reconstructible patterns.

It accomplishes this by granting a small palace inside the space of possible implementations. For a period, one node in the graph gets to make a particular transition unavailable to everyone else, including people like Bob, who reached the pattern independently and borrowed nothing.

Bob's case is fatal to one familiar justification for patents. The Lockean story says an inventor owns what she has mixed her labor with, and that copying her work takes the fruit of that labor. Bob mixed his own labor and took nothing. No information flowed from Alice to him, and the causal graph contains no theft to prevent. Whatever justifies stopping Bob, it cannot be that he wronged Alice. The only defense left is consequentialist: that a regime which stops even independent inventors yields more invention and disclosure overall than one that does not. That is an empirical bet, and modern patent practice often loses it. Thickets of overlapping claims block whole fields. Disclosures are drafted to satisfy examiners while teaching practitioners as little as possible. Litigation costs are so asymmetric that the threat of a suit works whether or not the claim is valid.

So whether patents are justified cannot be settled by asking whose property an invention is. The universe records no such fact. The question has to be put in terms the observer of the transition graph would recognize:

> Does this constraint create more future possibility than it suppresses?

Sometimes the answer will plausibly be yes. A temporary palace may call into existence a library that would otherwise never have been written. Sometimes the answer will be no. The palace outlives its purpose, its holder learns to influence the arbiter, the local precedence A ≺ₓ B becomes general capacity A ▹ B, and the instrument meant to enlarge the reachable state space starts shrinking it.

The model also suggests what reform would look like. An independent-invention defense, together with stronger rights for prior users, would sever the A ≺ₓ B block exactly where no causal copying occurred. Such protection matters most in convergent fields like software, where the problem space drives many minds to the same solutions. Strict enforcement of the requirement that a disclosure actually enable a skilled reader to build the thing would make the temporary palace pay its rent into the public library. Fee-shifting would make it harder to turn the cost of litigation into a weapon, which is one of the main routes by which a patent holder's local precedence becomes general power. None of this abolishes the palace. All of it keeps the palace small and temporary and makes it pay into the library.

The same test applies well beyond patents, to every control-plane packet a civilization issues: deeds, licenses, credentials, classifications, charters. Each is a claimed ordering, and each relies on enforcement machinery that can turn into hierarchy. The right question to ask of each is not "is it legitimate?", since legitimacy is exactly what the circular machinery is good at producing, but "what does it do to the space of possible worlds?" The answer will differ for packets that govern rivalrous matter and packets that govern non-rivalrous patterns.

### XVIII. Which Futures?

That question needs one more refinement, because in its raw form it is dangerously wrong.

If the measure of good law were simply the number of reachable futures, then the best possible library would include working recipes for engineered pandemics, efficient handbooks of chattel slavery, and the shortest path to a nuclear weapon. All of them expand the space of reconstructible patterns. Murder destroys a person's reachable futures, but the law against murder also removes a set of possible worlds. Abolition deliberately and rightly made certain social configurations unreachable. Civilization consists partly of pruning. Maximizing the raw cardinality of the reachable set, max |R|, cannot be the observer's objective function.

What the argument actually cares about is something harder to measure. It is the preservation of futures compatible with flourishing and with agency: the optionality that agents retain *without domination*. The Hebrew scriptures have a word for the condition this points toward, *shalom*. It does not mean the absence of constraint. It means a wholeness in which each person can sit "under his vine and under his fig tree, and no one shall make them afraid" (Micah 4:4). The fig tree is a constraint. It belongs to someone, it grows in one place, and it fixes the shape of a life. But no palace can take it.

This changes why the library matters. It does not matter because more states are always better. It matters because it lets later minds evaluate and reconstruct alternatives for themselves, instead of having one node decide in advance which possibilities everyone downstream may even consider. Preserving the history of slavery is not the same as preserving the capacity to enslave. A civilization can keep the record of every horror precisely so that it can recognize the next one. The pruning that civilization requires has to be done openly, by bounded and impartial processes that are themselves subject to judgment. The palace cannot be the one quietly deciding, in its own interest, which branches are too dangerous to keep.

That is where the two halves of the essay meet. Human law becomes most dangerous at the moment the palace gains the power to decide what may remain in the library.

### XIX. A Different Last Judgment

Return to the observer outside the automaton.

Under quantum Hashlife, God would not need to ask which institutions claimed legitimacy. Claims of legitimacy are patterns too, and he could watch them propagate like anything else. He would not need to consult registries of ownership, because the physics keeps no such registry. He would not even need to decide which written constitution was authoritative, because he could read the executable one directly from the transition graph. And like the observer of relativistic spacetime, he would not need to rank everyone. Seeing every relation is not the same as imposing an order on every pair. Omniscience can leave incomparable things incomparable.

What he could see is simpler and harder to fake. He could see which humans enlarge the space of reconstructible, agency-preserving patterns, by teaching, copying, preserving, disclosing, and keeping alternatives alive. He could see which humans hold the palace to the work of protecting that space and not to the work of ranking. And he could see which humans spend their energy deleting patterns, by burning, suppressing, enclosing, and capturing the arbiter, so that everyone downstream has fewer possible worlds to instantiate.

This is a strange picture of judgment, and it is a selective reading of a canon that also crowns kings and ordains priests. Even so, it fits the biblical picture better than it first appears. The God of Leviticus and Deuteronomy refuses to cache rank. The Deuteronomic law of the king allows a palace only if the king's heart is not lifted above his brothers. Samuel warns that the ordering function will become root. The Babel narrative interrupts the fusion of humanity into a single actor with unbounded reach. The prophets watch the gate, the scale, and the scribe. The Jubilee periodically returns the land. And in Jeremiah 36 the king burns the scroll, and it is written again, longer.

Humans set out to determine which claims come before which claims. What they repeatedly build instead is a machine for determining which humans can constrain which other humans. The palace and the library turn out to be two competing architectures for carrying agency through time. The palace transmits authority, and it must choose. The library transmits possibility, and it can afford to keep the alternatives side by side. A civilization needs both: a palace modest enough to keep the peace without ranking its people, and a library rich enough that the palace's choices can always be judged against the orderings it did not choose. Seen from outside, the difference between the two is the difference between ruling the present and stealing the future, and the second is the one the observer would hold against us.
